This map shows 19 of the most-searched sights in London as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Buckingham Palace, Tower of London, London Eye, Borough Market and Covent Garden lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Buckingham Palace

Buckingham Palace is the official London residence and administrative headquarters of the monarch, and it is one of the easiest big buildings to photograph on the map. The long, pale stone front fits neatly into a phone frame, and the open space in front gives you room to step back. The palace grew from Buckingham House, a large townhouse built in 1703 for the Duke of Buckingham and Normanby. George III bought it in 1761, and in the early 19th century the architects John Nash and Edward Blore enlarged it around a central courtyard. The East Front, the part with the balcony where the royal family traditionally greets the crowds, was one of the last major additions. The palace has 775 rooms, and its garden is the largest private garden in London. From there you look south-west towards the building and can get the whole width in one shot, with the balcony, flagpole and any guards or crowds adding life to the frame. Expect other visitors in your picture, especially around ceremonial events. Take a few frames at different zoom levels, then check them on screen for tilted rooflines. Some of the palace's state rooms open to visitors in summer, so check the official site before you go if you want to go inside.

Where it is: Buckingham Palace is in the City of Westminster, in central London.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the full front of the palace. If you are close and the building will not fit, switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x). Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the balcony, the roofline or the flag.

Where to stand: Stand on The Mall (looking west from the eastern end, toward the palace). A tree-lined view down the avenue with the Queen Victoria Memorial and the palace in the distance, including the flower beds.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the palace's horizontal edges with them so the building does not lean. Keep the phone level and at chest height, and leave some foreground, such as the open space, railings or people, to give a sense of scale. For a tighter shot, zoom to 2x or 3x and frame just the central balcony.

Settings tip: Tap on the palace front to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is bright, so the pale stone keeps its detail. Take a couple of shots in quick succession when people or flags are moving, and pick the sharpest.

Best time: Early in the day is usually quieter, which gives you a cleaner view with fewer people in front of the palace. If you want a livelier picture, ceremonial events draw crowds, but you will have less room, so check the official site for dates.

2Tower of London

The Tower of London is a medieval castle on the north bank of the Thames, and it makes a good subject because so many different shapes sit together: the pale White Tower in the middle, ringed by lower towers and walls. William the Conqueror built the White Tower in 1078, and it still rises about 27 metres at its southern battlements. The castle was later extended with two rings of defensive walls, and the whole site covers almost 12 acres. Over the centuries it has been a royal palace, a prison, an armoury, a treasury and the home of the Crown Jewels. Today it is a World Heritage Site, cared for by the charity Historic Royal Palaces.

That is far enough to fit a good part of the walls and towers into one frame, and near enough to pick out the stonework. The mix of old stone, battlements and the open space in front gives you layers to work with, which suits a phone camera well. Try one wide shot to show the scale, then a closer one of a single tower or section of wall. Keep your phone straight so the vertical walls don't lean, and take a few frames, as people often walk through the foreground.

Where it is: The Tower of London stands on the north bank of the River Thames in the London Borough of Tower Hamlets. It sits just east of the City of London, beyond the open space of Tower Hill.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole scene. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the walls and the sky in the frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the White Tower, battlements or a single turret from where you stand.

Where to stand: Stand in Trinity Square Gardens (south-facing part of the gardens at the top of Tower Hill). The Tower's stone turrets and medieval walls framed by greenery and lampposts in the foreground. From here you can see the walls and towers together without much of the foreground getting in the way.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the tops of the walls up with a horizontal line, so the towers don't lean. Leave a little space around the top of the highest tower rather than cropping tight. For a second shot, zoom in on one tower and let the stone texture fill the frame.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is bright and the walls look dark. Panorama mode can work for the long line of walls, but hold the phone steady and wait for passers-by to clear.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light on the stone and fewer people in the frame. Check the weather first, as an overcast day can flatten the stone but also gives even light with no harsh shadows.

3London Eye

The London Eye is a giant observation wheel on the South Bank of the River Thames, and it is easy to photograph because its shape is so simple. The rim is held by tensioned steel cables and looks like an enormous spoked bicycle wheel. Unusually, the whole thing is supported by an A-frame on one side only, so you can see a clear gap on the other. That one-sided design is worth showing in your picture.

The wheel is 135 metres tall, with a diameter of 120 metres. It was formally opened on 31 December 1999, but the public could not ride it until 9 March 2000. It stands between Westminster Bridge and Hungerford Bridge, beside County Hall. Its LED lighting was fitted in December 2006, so it can be lit in changing colours after dark.

Switch to the ultra-wide lens (0.5x), tilt the phone upwards and let the curve of the rim sweep across the picture. Keep the phone level, or the tall structure will look as if it is leaning. For a full view of the wheel, walk further back along the riverside. Use the telephoto lens (2x or more) to pick out the capsules and cables as close-up details.

Where it is: On the South Bank of the River Thames in the London Borough of Lambeth, between Westminster Bridge and Hungerford Bridge. It sits beside County Hall, at the western end of Jubilee Gardens.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide lens (0.5x), because you are very close to a very tall structure. Use the default lens (1x) for a tighter view of the lower part of the wheel and the A-frame. Use the telephoto lens (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for close-ups of the capsules and cables.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Step back along the riverside if you want the whole wheel in one frame.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the A-frame or the rim with a vertical line so the structure looks upright. With the ultra-wide lens, tilt the phone up and let the curve of the rim cross the frame. Try a second shot with the wheel's curve against the sky.

Settings tip: Tap on the wheel to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour and the wheel does not wash out. After dark, switch on night mode, rest your elbows on a wall or rail and hold still while it takes the shot.

Best time: Around dusk, the lit wheel stands out well against the darkening sky.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in London?

The first three on this map are Buckingham Palace, Tower of London and London Eye. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 19.

Where should I stand to photograph Buckingham Palace?

Stand on The Mall (looking west from the eastern end, toward the palace). Start with the default lens (1x) for the full front of the palace.

When is the best time to photograph the Tower of London?

Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light on the stone and fewer people in the frame. Check the weather first, as an overcast day can flatten the stone but also gives even light with no harsh shadows.

How do I get to the London Eye?

Start from Waterloo Underground station (Bakerloo line, Jubilee line, Northern line and Waterloo & City line).
