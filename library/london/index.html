This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in London as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Buckingham Palace, Big Ben, Tower of London, London Eye and Borough Market lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Buckingham Palace

Buckingham Palace is the official London residence and administrative headquarters of the monarch, and it is one of the easiest big buildings to photograph on the map. The long, pale stone front fits neatly into a phone frame, and the open space in front gives you room to step back. The palace grew from Buckingham House, a large townhouse built in 1703 for the Duke of Buckingham and Normanby. George III bought it in 1761, and in the early 19th century the architects John Nash and Edward Blore enlarged it around a central courtyard. The East Front, the part with the balcony where the royal family traditionally greets the crowds, was one of the last major additions. The palace has 775 rooms, and its garden is the largest private garden in London. The phone icon on the map is about 130 m north-east of the subject. From there you look south-west towards the building and can get the whole width in one shot, with the balcony, flagpole and any guards or crowds adding life to the frame. Expect other visitors in your picture, especially around ceremonial events. Take a few frames at different zoom levels, then check them on screen for tilted rooflines. Some of the palace's state rooms open to visitors in summer, so check the official site before you go if you want to go inside.

Where it is: Buckingham Palace is in the City of Westminster, in central London.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the full front of the palace. If you are close and the building will not fit, switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x). Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the balcony, the roofline or the flag.

Where to stand: Stand about 130 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the palace's horizontal edges with them so the building does not lean. Keep the phone level and at chest height, and leave some foreground, such as the open space, railings or people, to give a sense of scale. For a tighter shot, zoom to 2x or 3x and frame just the central balcony.

Settings tip: Tap on the palace front to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is bright, so the pale stone keeps its detail. Take a couple of shots in quick succession when people or flags are moving, and pick the sharpest.

Best time: Early in the day is usually quieter, which gives you a cleaner view with fewer people in front of the palace. If you want a livelier picture, ceremonial events draw crowds, but you will have less room, so check the official site for dates.

2Big Ben and the Houses of Parliament

Big Ben is strictly the nickname of the Great Bell, but most people use it for the whole clock tower at the north end of the Palace of Westminster, which has been called the Elizabeth Tower since 2012. Pugin designed it in a Gothic Revival style, and it was completed in 1859. It stands about 96 metres tall, and each clock dial is almost 7 metres across, so the tower fills a phone frame easily and the golden-grey stonework and pointed spire make a strong, simple shape. The tower leans very slightly to the north-west, but you will not notice that in a photo. Look near the top for the Ayrton Light, a lantern above the belfry that is lit whenever the House of Commons sits after dark. The whole building is Grade I listed and part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Keep your phone upright and level so the tower does not appear to lean backwards. Pavements here can be busy, so take a few quick shots and then step aside. Check the clock face in your photo before moving on, as it is the detail most people want to see.

Where it is: In Westminster, central London, at the north end of the Palace of Westminster, about 69 metres south-east of Westminster Underground station.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) from where you stand. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the tower and the long palace frontage together, or to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a close shot of the clock face and spire.

Where to stand: Stand about 160 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a vertical grid line up with the tower's edge. Hold the phone upright and level, since tilting it back makes the tower look as if it is falling over. Leave a little sky above the spire and include some of the palace roofline below for scale.

Settings tip: Tap the tower's stonework to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky stays blue and the clock face does not burn out. After dark, use night mode and rest your elbows against your body or a wall to keep the shot steady.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. After dark, try night mode, and look for the Ayrton Light above the belfry, which is lit when the Commons is sitting.

3Tower of London

The Tower of London is a medieval castle on the north bank of the Thames, and it makes a good subject because so many different shapes sit together: the pale White Tower in the middle, ringed by lower towers and walls. William the Conqueror built the White Tower in 1078, and it still rises about 27 metres at its southern battlements. The castle was later extended with two rings of defensive walls, and the whole site covers almost 12 acres. Over the centuries it has been a royal palace, a prison, an armoury, a treasury and the home of the Crown Jewels. Today it is a World Heritage Site, cared for by the charity Historic Royal Palaces.

The phone icon on the map is about 95 m west of the subject. That is far enough to fit a good part of the walls and towers into one frame, and near enough to pick out the stonework. The mix of old stone, battlements and the open space in front gives you layers to work with, which suits a phone camera well. Try one wide shot to show the scale, then a closer one of a single tower or section of wall. Keep your phone straight so the vertical walls don't lean, and take a few frames, as people often walk through the foreground.

Where it is: The Tower of London stands on the north bank of the River Thames in the London Borough of Tower Hamlets. It sits just east of the City of London, beyond the open space of Tower Hill.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole scene. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the walls and the sky in the frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the White Tower, battlements or a single turret from where you stand.

Where to stand: Stand about 95 m west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face east. From here you can see the walls and towers together without much of the foreground getting in the way.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the tops of the walls up with a horizontal line, so the towers don't lean. Leave a little space around the top of the highest tower rather than cropping tight. For a second shot, zoom in on one tower and let the stone texture fill the frame.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is bright and the walls look dark. Panorama mode can work for the long line of walls, but hold the phone steady and wait for passers-by to clear.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light on the stone and fewer people in the frame. Check the weather first, as an overcast day can flatten the stone but also gives even light with no harsh shadows.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in London?

The first three on this map are Buckingham Palace, Big Ben and Tower of London. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 20.

Where should I stand to photograph Buckingham Palace?

Stand about 130 m north-east of Buckingham Palace, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. Start with the default lens (1x) for the full front of the palace.

When is the best time to photograph Big Ben?

Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. After dark, try night mode, and look for the Ayrton Light above the belfry, which is lit when the Commons is sitting.

How do I get to the Tower of London?

Start at Tower Hill Underground station (Circle line and District line), which is about 3 minutes on foot from the Tower, with the castle to the south.
