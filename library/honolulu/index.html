The 3 free photo spots

1Waikiki Beach

Waikiki is the beach most people picture when they think of Honolulu, and the best phone photo of it comes from Duke Kahanamoku Beach. Stand on the sand in front of the bronze statue of Duke Kahanamoku, face Diamond Head, and the picture builds itself: the statue and its surfboards in the foreground, the beach and surfers in the middle, and the green ridge of Diamond Head behind. Guides call this the classic postcard view, and the statue gives the frame a clear local sense of place. Surfers and outrigger canoes add movement.

The beach has a long story. Waikiki was briefly the first capital of the Kingdom of Hawaii (1795 to 1796) and later a retreat for Hawaiian royalty, who surfed here. Duke Kahanamoku, a famous surfer, helped make it a surfing hotspot. The sand is almost entirely man-made, and sand was shipped in from California in the 1920s and 1930s. The waves here are known for a long, rolling break, which is ideal for longboarding and beginners, though the waves can be stronger on windy days.

For practical photography, expect the beachfront to be busy, so shoot a little wider than you think you need and let people be part of the scene. Wipe your lens, as sea air and sun cream leave smears. If you want a calmer alternative, Magic Island shows the Waikiki skyline and Diamond Head with sailboats and reflections in the lagoon. It is about two miles from central Waikiki, so plan a short bus, taxi or bike ride.

Where it is: Waikiki Beach is on the south shore of Oʻahu, in the Waikiki neighbourhood of Honolulu, which runs from the Ala Wai Canal across to Diamond Head. This viewpoint is on Duke Kahanamoku Beach, one of the beaches that make up the Waikiki shoreline.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the statue, the beach and Diamond Head all fit in one frame. Try the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the statue to look larger in the foreground, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull Diamond Head closer.

Where to stand: Stand in Duke Kahanamoku Beach (on the sand in front of the Duke Kahanamoku statue, facing Diamond Head). The bronze Duke Kahanamoku statue with surfboards in the foreground, Waikiki's beach and surfers in the middle, and Diamond Head in the background.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and place the statue on one side of the grid, with the beach and Diamond Head filling the rest of the frame. Crouch slightly so the surfboards sit against the sea rather than against the sand. For a second view, Magic Island shows the skyline and Diamond Head across calm lagoon water.

Settings tip: Tap the statue to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the bright sky does not wash out. Try a Live Photo while surfers or canoes are passing, then pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Sunset is the time guides recommend. Exact light varies by season, and the beachfront gets crowded. Sunrise can be shaded by tall hotels in some areas.

2Diamond Head

Diamond Head is the big, wedge-shaped crater that closes off the eastern end of Waikiki. It is a volcanic tuff cone, roughly 400,000 to 500,000 years old, and Hawaiians call it Lēʻahi, probably because the ridgeline looks like the dorsal fin of a tuna. British sailors renamed it in 1825 after finding sparkling calcite crystals in the sand and mistaking them for diamonds. It has been a National Natural Landmark since 1968 and turns up as a backdrop in many films and TV shows set in Hawaii.

It photographs well because the slope is smooth and the shape is instantly recognisable. From Kapiolani Park the crater rises above open lawns, so you get grass, sky and the ridge in one simple frame. The park is a good place to get a clean, uncluttered shot, and it is a much easier stop than the summit trail. Summit views look towards Waikiki rather than at the crater itself, so you do not see the shape you came for.

Keep the ridgeline level with the gridlines and leave some lawn in the foreground to give a sense of scale. A tree or a bench at the edge of the frame also helps. If you want a wider view of the crater, the coast and Waikiki together, the Diamond Head Road Vista Point is a good second stop.

Where it is: Diamond Head sits at the eastern end of Waikiki, on the south shore of Oʻahu in Honolulu.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the crater, the lawn and the sky all fit. Switch to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the ridge closer and fill the frame with the crater. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of lawn and sky; the crater will look small and distant.

Where to stand: Stand in Kapiolani Park (center of the park, near the tennis courts, facing the crater). Diamond Head rising directly above open lawns, with the crater centered in the frame. A bench near the tennis courts is a good spot to frame it from.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and set the ridgeline along a horizontal line, with the crater centred. Keep a strip of lawn at the bottom of the frame. For a different view of the crater with the coast and Waikiki, try the Diamond Head Road Vista Point.

Settings tip: Tap on the crater slope to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour and the slope does not wash out. Use the 0.5x lens only if you want to include a lot of foreground.

Best time: Early morning, when the park is often empty. Late afternoon may suit the crater too, as the sun will be behind you.

3Moana Surfrider

The Moana Surfrider is the grand old white hotel on Kalakaua Avenue in Waikiki. Its oldest part opened in 1901 as the first hotel in the area, and it is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The building has Ionic columns and fine woodwork. Two floors and the side wings were added in 1918, which gave it the H-shape you see today. A large banyan tree stands in the courtyard. It was planted in 1904 and is now about 23 m (75 ft) high and 46 m (150 ft) across.

The classic photo is the front of the hotel seen from the far side of Kalakaua Avenue. The old building stays still while cars and people move past it, so the picture feels lively but tidy. Stand close to the kerb, keep your phone level so the columns stay straight, and wait for a gap in the people crossing. If you have time, the banyan courtyard is a good second picture. There you can frame the big tree and its hanging roots against the courtyard buildings, with Diamond Head in the distance.

The hotel is private property, so take photos from public areas and be considerate of guests.

Where it is: In the Waikiki district of Honolulu, on the inland side of the beach, at 2365 Kalakaua Avenue. The hotel sits between the avenue and the shoreline.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) so the whole front of the hotel fits in from across the avenue. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer view with less edge stretching. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the columns and the porch.

Where to stand: Stand on Kalakaua Avenue (sidewalk across from the hotel's Kalakaua Avenue entrance, near the ABC store). The hotel's front facade framed by traffic and pedestrians, shot from the far side of the avenue with a wide-angle lens. Stay on the public pavement.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the building's vertical edges with them. Keep the phone level and the full facade in view, with a little street in the foreground to show the movement. For a different picture, walk into the courtyard through the Kalakaua Avenue entrance and shoot the banyan and its roots, looking toward the beach with Diamond Head in the distance.

Settings tip: Tap the hotel to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the white walls keep their detail. For streaks of cars and people, try the Long Exposure effect in Live Photos if your phone has it, and rest your phone against a post or your other arm to keep it steady. At night, use night mode.

Best time: Early evening, when the building is lit and car lights make streaks, is a good time for the long-exposure shot. Early morning is calmer for clear pictures with fewer people.
