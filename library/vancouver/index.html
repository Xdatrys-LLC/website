This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in Vancouver as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Capilano Bridge, Gastown, Stanley Park, Canada Place and Granville Island lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Capilano Suspension Bridge

The Capilano Suspension Bridge is a simple suspension bridge across the Capilano River in the District of North Vancouver. It is about 140 metres long and 70 metres above the water, so it photographs well: a thin line of deck hanging over a deep canyon, with dark forest on both sides. The first bridge was built in 1889 by George Grant Mackay, using hemp ropes and cedar planks. The present bridge dates from a complete rebuild in 1956. It belongs to a private park that draws over 1.2 million visitors a year, so expect company on the span itself.

For a picture of the whole bridge rather than a view along it, use the Cliffwalk. This attraction was added to the park in June 2011, and its designated photo platforms along the cliff face let you frame the full bridge with the river and trees below for scale. Photo-stop signs mark where to pause. Take your shots at those marked spots and stay behind the railings. If the Cliffwalk is busy, the Nature's Edge Boardwalk is a good alternative: it shows the bridge through the rainforest trees, away from the crowds on the span.

The bridge sways when it is full of people, so pictures taken from on it can come out blurred. Taking the wide shot from the platforms avoids that.

Where it is: In Upper Capilano, in the District of North Vancouver, inside Capilano Suspension Bridge Park on the Capilano River canyon.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the whole bridge across the canyon in one frame. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) only if the platform is crowded and you need to fit more in. Use telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the span and the walkers on it.

Where to stand: Stand about 110 m north-east of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. From here the whole suspension bridge spans the canyon, with the river and trees below to show how high it is.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the bridge deck along the lower third line, so the trees and river fill the rest. Keep the railing out of the corners of the frame, and wait for gaps in the crowd on the span. For a quieter, more natural view, the Nature's Edge Boardwalk shows the bridge through the forest trees.

Settings tip: Tap on the bridge to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the deck and trees keep their detail. Take a few frames, as the bridge moves when busy. In winter, if you stay for the Canyon Lights display, use night mode and hold the phone as still as you can against a rail or your body.

Best time: Choose a time of day when the park is quieter, as fewer people on the span give a cleaner picture. Soft, even light on a bright overcast day also works well under the trees. Check the official site before you go for current hours and the winter Canyon Lights times.

2Gastown and the Steam Clock

Gastown is Vancouver's oldest neighbourhood, named after "Gassy" Jack Deighton, a Yorkshire seaman who opened a saloon here in 1867. Its best-known photo subject is the steam clock on the corner of Water and Cambie Streets. The clock was built in 1977 over a steam grate, and it runs on steam from the downtown heating network. Steam also sounds its whistles, which play a Westminster-style chime. It has four faces, a domed top and, on a cold day, plumes of steam, with the brick heritage buildings of Water Street behind. That mix of old brick and a quirky machine is why it photographs so well. Gastown became a National Historic Site of Canada in 2009. The clock itself is a modern invention, though it is promoted as a heritage feature.

The phone icon on the map is on the south side of Water Street, facing the clock, which gives an open view of the steam above the dome. Arrive three or four minutes before a quarter hour to catch the whistle and the steam. Crowds gather, especially at the top of the hour and at midday in summer, so be patient and keep out of the walking path. If you have time, Maple Tree Square and the narrow Blood Alley are worth a few more frames, particularly after dark when the lamps are lit.

Where it is: In Gastown, in the north-west part of the Downtown Eastside next to Downtown Vancouver, at the corner of Water Street and Cambie Street, just back from the Burrard Inlet waterfront.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole clock with the brick buildings behind it. Switch to 2x or 3x telephoto if you want the dome and steam without the crowd around the base.

Where to stand: Stand about 160 m east of the subject, at the corner of Water Street and Cambie Street (the phone icon on the map), and face west. From here the full clock with its domed top and steam plumes sits against the brick heritage buildings behind it. Stay out of the pedestrian walking path while you shoot.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the clock upright in the centre or just off to one side, leaving space above the dome for the steam. For an alternative, try Maple Tree Square with its Victorian brick corners and a view down Carrall Street. The Gassy Jack statue is no longer there to frame.

Settings tip: Tap on the clock to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the steam stays white and the sky does not wash out. Turn on Live Photos or motion to catch the steam moving. After dark, use night mode and hold the phone steady against your body or a wall.

Best time: Arrive 3-4 minutes before a quarter hour for the whistle. The steam shows best on cold days, from late autumn to March. Crowds gather especially at the top of the hour and at midday in summer.

11Vancouver Lookout (Harbour Centre)

Harbour Centre is the office tower in Downtown Vancouver whose Vancouver Lookout sits on top, so it works as a photo subject and as a place to take photos from. The 360-degree viewing deck opened on 13 August 1977, and glass elevators carry visitors up 168 metres in about 40 seconds. Heights quoted for the building vary, so check on the day. The Lookout's own figure for the deck is 168 m above street level, while other listings give a lower architectural height. The tower is one of the tallest structures in the city and an easy landmark to spot on the skyline.

From the street, the tower stands out against the sky with its wide deck and the revolving restaurant section. From the deck you look over the harbour, the downtown towers and the water, with nothing blocking the view. Both are good for a phone: the street view suits an ultra-wide shot looking up, and the deck gives you layered city views.

The phone icon on the map sits right at the building. If you stay outside, step back along the nearby pavements to fit more of the tower in. If you go up, shoot straight out from the deck. Waterfront station is only a couple of minutes away on foot, so it is easy to add to a walk along the waterfront.

Where it is: In the central business district of Downtown Vancouver, at 555 West Hastings Street, right beside Waterfront station.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Outside, use the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole tower in when you look up at it. On the deck, use the default lens (1x) for the wide view and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out single buildings, boats or the harbour.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. The tower rises directly above you here, so for a full-height shot step back along the pavement. If you go up to the deck, stand at the windows or railings and shoot outwards.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the tower's edges parallel to the vertical lines so it doesn't lean. Leave some sky above the deck. From the top, include some water or street in the lower part of the frame to give a sense of height, and take one wide shot and one zoomed shot of the same view.

Settings tip: Tap on the sky or the tower to set focus, then use the exposure slider to stop the sky turning white. Through glass, hold the phone close to the window and keep your hand steady to cut down reflections.

Best time: Late afternoon into early evening is a good choice, because you can catch the daylight view and then the city lights from the same visit. Use night mode if you stay after dark.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Vancouver?

The first three on this map are Capilano Bridge, Gastown and Stanley Park. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 20.

Where should I stand to photograph Capilano Bridge?

Stand about 110 m north-east of Capilano Bridge, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. Start with the default lens (1x) to get the whole bridge across the canyon in one frame.

When is the best time to photograph Gastown?

Arrive 3-4 minutes before a quarter hour for the whistle. The steam shows best on cold days, from late autumn to March.

How do I get to Vancouver Lookout?

From Waterfront station (Canada Line and SkyTrain Expo Line), walk south-east; it is about 2 minutes on foot.
