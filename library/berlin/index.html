The 3 free photo spots

1Brandenburg Gate

The Brandenburg Gate is one of Berlin's best-known landmarks, a neoclassical monument built between 1788 and 1791 for King Frederick William II of Prussia. The architect was Carl Gotthard Langhans. Twelve fluted Doric columns, six along each side, form five passageways. Above them stands the Quadriga, a bronze chariot drawn by four horses and driven by a goddess figure, made by the sculptor Johann Gottfried Schadow. Through the Cold War the Berlin Wall blocked the gateway, and since 1990 the gate has stood as a symbol of unity and peace.

It photographs well because it is symmetrical and the stone is pale. The cobbled square in front gives your frame some depth. The classic view is the full-frontal one from Pariser Platz, where the six columns and the Quadriga sit centred and the horses face towards you. Stand on the east side of the square and look west.

The square gets very crowded from late morning, so arrive early if you want an empty foreground. Keep your phone level and use the gridlines so the columns stay upright. If people wander into the frame, take several shots and pick the cleanest. For a different angle, the western side, along Straße des 17. Juni, shows the gate in late-day light with the Quadriga seen from behind.

Where it is: The gate is in the western part of Berlin's city centre, in the district of Mitte. It stands where Unter den Linden meets Ebertstraße, with the Reichstag building one block to the north and the Tiergarten park to the west.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x), which fits the whole gate with the square in front of it. Switch to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the Quadriga and the column details. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of cobbles and sky, and keep the gate in the middle of the frame so the edges do not stretch.

Where to stand: Stand in Pariser Platz (east side of the square, facing west toward the gate). The gate's front with the six columns and the Quadriga centred above, with the cobbled square in the foreground.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the centre of the gate up with the middle vertical line, with the Quadriga on top. Hold the phone level so the columns do not lean. Include a strip of cobbles at the bottom for depth. For an alternative, try an angled view from the edge of Pariser Platz with the surrounding buildings partly in the frame, or walk round to Straße des 17. Juni for the western face.

Settings tip: Tap on the gate to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright so the stone keeps its detail. Take a few shots in a row so you can choose one without people in the way. Night mode can help after dark, but the floodlighting is switched off from 26 October to early December 2026 for renewal, so night shots may not work then.

Best time: Sunrise is best, when the square is quiet and the sun is behind you. Late morning onwards is very crowded. The Festival of Lights and New Year's Eve bring large crowds and possible restrictions, so check current conditions before you go.

2Reichstag building

The Reichstag is the seat of Germany's Bundestag, and the lawn in front of it gives the classic straight-on view. The building went up between 1884 and 1894 to designs by Paul Wallot. After wartime damage and decades of partial use, it was redesigned by Norman Foster between 1995 and 1999 and handed over for parliament use in April 1999. The walk-in glass dome is the best-known part of that rebuild. Look above the main entrance for the words "Dem deutschen Volke", added in 1916. The old stone and the modern glass in one frame are what make the view work. The wide lawn keeps the foreground clear, so you can step back until the whole façade fits. The lawn was reported muddy and under groundworks in June 2025, so check its condition on the day. For a second angle, the Marie-Elisabeth-Lüders-Haus, across the Spree, shows the Reichstag and dome with the river reflecting the buildings. Only the lower public level of the double bridge there is open to visitors. The dome and rooftop terrace give close-up views of the glass and the spiral ramp, but they need advance online registration.

Where it is: The Reichstag stands in the Tiergarten district, on the left bank of the River Spree, on Platz der Republik.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the whole west front with the dome behind. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are close or the building will not fit. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the dome or the inscription above the entrance.

Where to stand: Stand in Platz der Republik (open lawn directly in front of the west façade). The full west front of the Reichstag with its columns and the glass dome rising behind, framed by open grass. The Reichstag lies to the east of you.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the entrance on the centre line so the columns look straight. Keep a band of lawn at the bottom to frame the building and leave the dome clear above it. For a different view, the Marie-Elisabeth-Lüders-Haus across the Spree shows the Reichstag and dome with the river reflecting the buildings.

Settings tip: Tap the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky stays blue and the stone keeps its detail. Hold the phone level, using the gridlines, to avoid leaning verticals.

Best time: Afternoon, when the sun is behind you as you face the building, according to one traveller review. Go early if you want fewer people in the frame.

3Checkpoint Charlie

Checkpoint Charlie was the best-known crossing point between East and West Berlin during the Cold War. Today the junction of Friedrichstraße and Zimmerstraße is a busy city crossroads, but a replica guardhouse stands in the middle of the street, with its 'You are leaving the American Sector' sign, sandbags and flags. It is one of the most photographed spots in Berlin, and it is easy to see why: the booth, the sign and the street around it tell the story in a single frame.

A few facts help you read the scene. The name comes from the letter C in the NATO phonetic alphabet. In October 1961, ten Soviet and ten American tanks stood facing each other near here, and the stand-off ended peacefully on 28 October. The guardhouse you see now is a replica; the metal container that replaced the original wooden shed in 1976 is on display at the Allied Museum in Dahlem, not here.

For your phone, the main job is to get the booth and sign clear of people and traffic. Expect crowds, and take care at the junction, as cars keep moving past. Photograph from the pavement and never step into the road to get a better angle. Costumed actors sometimes pose with visitors, and photos with them may cost a few euros, so ask first if you want a picture with them. Nearby, the outdoor history panels of the Checkpoint Charlie Gallery make a good second picture, with escape stories in the foreground and the street behind.

Where it is: Checkpoint Charlie is in the Friedrichstadt district of central Berlin, at the junction of Friedrichstraße and Zimmerstraße, close to Mauerstraße.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the booth, sign and street in one frame. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the pavement is crowded and you cannot step back, but keep the booth away from the edges, where it can look stretched. A touch of zoom (2x) helps you pick out the sign and flags from the kerb.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From here you see the guardhouse with its 'You are leaving the American Sector' sign, sandbags and flags, framed by the street.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the booth on one of the vertical lines so the sign stays readable. Keep the phone level so the street edges do not lean. Wait for a gap in the people, or let a few figures in as a sense of scale. For a second picture, try the Checkpoint Charlie Gallery panels nearby, with the checkpoint area behind them.

Settings tip: Tap on the sign to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright so the flags keep their colour. Take a few shots in a row, as people and cars move through the frame all the time.

Best time: Early morning, when crowds and traffic are lighter.

When is the best time to photograph the Reichstag?

How do I get to the Checkpoint Charlie?
