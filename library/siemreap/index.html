This map shows 17 of the most-searched sights in Siem Reap as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Angkor Wat, Bayon, Pub Street, Angkor Thom and Ta Prohm lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Angkor Wat

Angkor Wat is the largest religious complex in the world, and it looks the part. A broad moat and an outer wall surround three raised galleries that climb towards a central group of five towers. The whole site covers about 162.6 hectares. It was built in the 12th century for the Khmer king Suryavarman II, first as a Hindu temple to Vishnu. Over the centuries it became a Buddhist temple and is still a place of worship today. Unusually for the temples around Angkor, it faces west.

It photographs well because of its scale and its order. The towers, galleries and moat repeat in clear lines, so even a quick phone shot has a strong shape. The walls also carry long bas-reliefs and carved figures called devatas, which reward a closer look.

Use that direction to build a wide view of the towers and galleries, then walk closer for details. The temple is huge, so decide first whether you want the whole building in one frame or a single carved wall. Trying to get both from one spot usually disappoints. Keep your phone lens clean, because dust and humidity can soften the picture. Take a few frames at each place, and step aside so other visitors can pass.

Where it is: Angkor Wat stands in the Angkor area, in the Siem Reap region of Cambodia, inside the old Khmer capital of Angkor.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit in the galleries, towers and moat at close range. Use the default lens (1x) for most views, as it looks most natural. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the tops of the towers and for carved details on the walls.

Where to stand: Stand about 160 m north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the towers or galleries with a horizontal line so the building does not lean. Leave some foreground, such as stonework or open ground, to show how big the place is. For carvings, move in close and shoot along the wall rather than straight on, to bring out the relief.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the sky or the stone to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour. Inside shaded galleries, tap the carvings and nudge the exposure up. Live Photos can catch passing monks or visitors, but ask before photographing people up close.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. Midday sun is harsh on pale stone and can make the shadows dark.

2Bayon

The Bayon is the temple people picture when they think of stone faces. Towers rise in clusters, and each one has serene, smiling faces carved on every side. There are 216 of these faces, which many scholars read as portraits of King Jayavarman VII, the Buddhist king who built the temple in the late 12th or early 13th century. It stands at the very centre of his walled capital, Angkor Thom, and it was the last state temple built at Angkor.

It photographs well because the faces are everywhere and the towers are packed close together, so you can find a new angle every few steps. The outer gallery also has long bas-reliefs showing armies on the march, processions and scenes of everyday life, which make good detail shots.

For the best close-up on a phone, stand right at the base of one of the central face towers (the phone icon on the map) and shoot upwards. The faces then look huge, and the vertical lines lean in towards each other. If you can step back, the full towers against the sky make a calmer picture, and a second good spot is one that lets you line up three towers in a single frame in warm late sun.

The upper terrace, where many older guides took their nose-to-nose shots, is reported closed for restoration, so check what is open when you arrive. Go early or late in the day, when the sun comes in low and shows off the carving.

Where it is: The Bayon is in the Angkor area near Siem Reap, at the exact centre of the walled city of Angkor Thom. Angkor Wat lies to the south of it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide lens (0.5x) for the looking-up shot at the base of a tower. Use the default lens (1x) for whole towers against the sky. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for single faces and carved details.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Looking up from here, you see a carved face with converging vertical lines and the huge scale of the tower.

Framing: Tilt the phone up and keep the face in the middle of the frame. Switch on gridlines so you can see how steeply the lines lean in. If you want a calmer view, step back and use the default lens (1x) to fit the full towers against the sky. Another good alternative is a spot where three towers line up in one frame.

Settings tip: Tap on the face to focus. Then slide the exposure down a little so the bright sky does not wash out the stone. Take a second shot with the telephoto for detail, as the ultra-wide can stretch the edges of the frame.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when low side light brings out the carvings.

4Angkor Thom

Angkor Thom, the 'Great City', was the last and longest-lasting capital of the Khmer Empire, founded in the late twelfth century by King Jayavarman VII. Its walls are 8 m high and 3 km long on each side, and a moat runs around them. The best phone photo here is the south entrance, Tonle Om Gate. A tower about 23 m high, carved with large calm faces, stands at the end of a stone causeway. Rows of figures line the causeway, gods (devas) on the left and demons (asuras) on the right, each row holding a serpent as if in a tug-of-war. This is a scene from the Churning of the Sea of Milk myth. The gate is the main way into the city for visitors, so it is busy, but the long straight lines of the causeway make a tidy, balanced picture. If the moat is still, the gate may reflect in it. Come in early morning or late afternoon, when the light is softer and there are fewer tuk-tuks and groups. Vehicles use the causeway, so keep an eye out while you line up your shot. If you have time, Victory Gate and the North Gate are good alternatives, with trees growing around the carved faces.

Where it is: Angkor Thom is inside the Angkor Archaeological Park, north of Angkor Wat and about 7 km north of Siem Reap town. Tonle Om Gate is its south entrance, about 1.7 km north of the Angkor Wat entrance.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the moat, causeway, statues and gate tower in one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, less stretched view of the gate. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the carved faces on the tower.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (on the causeway in front of the gate, standing on either side of the bridge over the moat). The gate tower with its giant faces at the end of a long causeway lined with gods and demons, often with the moat reflecting the scene. On either side of the bridge over the moat. The wider city of Angkor Thom stretches away to the north behind it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the causeway in the middle so the rows of statues lead to the gate. Crouch a little to bring the moat into the bottom of the frame and catch any reflection. Keep the tower upright, because tilting makes it lean. For a different view, Victory Gate gives a high angle down onto tuk-tuks passing under the gate, and the North Gate (Dei Chhnang Gate) shows its carved faces among trees.

Settings tip: Tap on the gate's faces to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright so the stone keeps its detail. Take a few frames in case a tuk-tuk or tour group walks through.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when the light is softer and there are fewer tuk-tuks and groups.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Siem Reap?

The first three on this map are Angkor Wat, Bayon and Pub Street. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 17.

Where should I stand to photograph Angkor Wat?

Stand about 160 m north-west of Angkor Wat, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east. Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit in the galleries, towers and moat at close range.

When is the best time to photograph the Bayon?

Early morning or late afternoon, when low side light brings out the carvings.

How do I get to Angkor Thom?

Travel north from central Siem Reap towards the Angkor temples; the journey takes around 30 minutes.
