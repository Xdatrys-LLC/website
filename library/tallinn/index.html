This map shows 17 of the most-searched sights in Tallinn as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Toompea Castle, Seaplane Harbour, Kadriorg Palace, Viru Gate and Telliskivi lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Toompea Castle

Toompea Castle sits on top of Toompea hill in central Tallinn, and today it is home to the Parliament of Estonia, the Riigikogu. It photographs well because several different eras stand side by side. The medieval walls and towers date largely from the 13th century onwards. A large Baroque and Neoclassical wing was added in the eastern part of the complex after Russia took over in 1710, and a separate parliament building from 1922 stands on the site of the old convent building. The tallest tower, Tall Hermann (Pikk Hermann), is 48 metres high and dominates the skyline. The Estonian flag is raised on it every day at sunrise and lowered at sunset, which gives you a good reason to time a visit. The hill is also the subject of a 19th-century legend that the heroine Linda built it boulder by boulder. For photos, think of this as a building to frame in layers: a wide view of the long façade, then closer shots of the stonework and, if it is in view, the tower. Keep your phone level so the walls and tower stay straight, and take a few frames with and without people in them for scale. Right next to it: Alexander Nevsky Cathedral.

Where it is: On Toompea hill in the central part of Tallinn.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole façade, then switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are too close to fit the wing in. If Tall Hermann is in view from where you stand, use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for it and for details of the stonework.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the walls and tower with the vertical lines so they do not lean. Include a little foreground, such as paving or a path, to give a sense of scale, and use the telephoto to isolate a single stretch of wall, or the tower if it is in view.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to set focus and drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stonework keeps its detail. Take a quick burst or Live Photo if people are crossing your frame, so you can pick the cleanest moment.

Best time: Around sunrise or sunset, when the flag is raised or lowered on Tall Hermann. Soft early or late light usually flatters the stonework, but check how the light falls on the day.

2Seaplane Harbour

Seaplane Harbour (Lennusadam) is a maritime museum that opened in spring 2012, set in a building first put up as a hangar for seaplanes. It stands in the area of Peter the Great's Naval Fortress. The hall covers 8,000 m2 and was taken out of service in the Soviet era, and its renovation began in 2010. That history is why the place photographs well: big, plain hangar shapes sit right beside the water, and old ships are moored along the harbour edge. The museum's best-known exhibit is the 1936 submarine Lembit, and the icebreaker Suur Tõll is another of its historic vessels. Walk along the water's edge for a few steps before you shoot, because small changes in position change what lines up with the hangar. For a second angle, the Noblessner marina promenade across the cove shows the arched hangars with a grey warship moored in front of them. If you also walk to the museum itself, the open-air pier behind it is an optional extra viewpoint, with moored ships close by for the foreground. Inside the museum the hangar is dim, so plan to take your best pictures outdoors.

Where it is: Seaplane Harbour is on Tallinn's waterfront, north of the city centre, in the old Peter the Great's Naval Fortress area. The museum building was originally a seaplane hangar and sits right on the harbour edge.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the hangar, the water and the sky together. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull the hangar curves and roofline closer from this distance. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you walk to the museum itself and the roofline will not fit.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (harbour side and the open-air pier behind the museum). The museum's hangar hull and seaplanes, with historic ships moored along the harbour edge in the foreground. The museum lies to the south-west of you, so face that way. From this distance the hangar hull and roofline appear as a distant shape across the water. If you walk to the museum, the open-air pier behind it is a separate, closer viewpoint where moored ships can make the foreground.

Framing: Turn on gridlines, keep the horizon level, and place the hangar hull on one of the vertical lines with the water filling the lower third. If you want a different view, the Noblessner marina promenade across the cove shows the arched hangars with a grey warship in front.

Settings tip: Tap on the hangar to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright so the building does not turn into a silhouette. Don't expect good results inside the dim hangar; if you shoot indoors, use night mode and hold still.

Best time: Soft light in the early morning or late afternoon usually flatters the hangar and the water, but the spot works in most daylight.

3Kadriorg Palace

Kadriorg Palace is a pale Baroque building that Peter the Great had built for his wife Catherine. Work began in 1718 and the main building was finished by 1725. Both its Estonian and German names mean "Catherine's valley". It photographs well because it is a single, well-proportioned building with a clear outline, set in a park, so there is little clutter around it.

The palace fell into neglect during the 20th century. After Estonia regained independence, restoration began in 1991 with Swedish government support, and it reopened to the public in 2000. Today it houses the Kadriorg Art Museum, which shows foreign art from the 16th to the 20th century. Estonian art is shown at Kumu, a short way off in the same park.

For your photo, stand back and keep the whole building in the frame, with a strip of the grounds in front of it to give a sense of scale. Turn on gridlines and line up the roofline with a horizontal line so the palace doesn't look tilted. If other visitors walk through the scene, take a few shots a few seconds apart and keep the best one. Tap the palace to set focus and lower the exposure slider slightly if the sky looks too bright.

Where it is: Kadriorg Palace stands in the Kadriorg district of Tallinn, in the park that surrounds it. The Kumu art museum is nearby in the same park.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x), which should take in the whole width of the palace from where you stand. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of foreground, and keep the palace central so the edges don't stretch. Use telephoto (2x or more) for details such as the roofline or windows.

Where to stand: Stand about 25 m north of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south.

Framing: Hold the phone level and switch on gridlines. Put the palace in the middle third and leave some grass or path in the lower part of the frame. For a second shot, step a little to one side so the building sits at an angle.

Settings tip: Tap the palace to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a touch so the sky keeps its colour and the walls don't wash out. If it is crowded, Live Photos can help you pick a frame with fewer people in it.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. At midday you will be shooting towards the sun, so the front of the palace may look dark against a bright sky.
