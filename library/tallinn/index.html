This map shows 18 of the most-searched sights in Tallinn as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Toompea Castle, Seaplane Harbour, Kadriorg Palace, Viru Gate and Telliskivi lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Toompea Castle

Toompea Castle sits on top of Toompea hill in central Tallinn, and today it is home to the Parliament of Estonia, the Riigikogu. It photographs well because several different eras stand side by side. The medieval walls and towers date largely from the 13th century onwards. A large Baroque and Neoclassical wing was added in the eastern part of the complex after Russia took over in 1710, and a separate parliament building from 1922 stands on the site of the old convent building. The tallest tower, Tall Hermann (Pikk Hermann), is 48 metres high and dominates the skyline. The Estonian flag is raised on it every day at sunrise and lowered at sunset, which gives you a good reason to time a visit. The hill is also the subject of a 19th-century legend that the heroine Linda built it boulder by boulder. For photos, think of this as a building to frame in layers: a wide view of the long façade, then closer shots of the stonework and, if it is in view, the tower. Keep your phone level so the walls and tower stay straight, and take a few frames with and without people in them for scale.

Where it is: On Toompea hill in the central part of Tallinn.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole façade, then switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are too close to fit the wing in. If Tall Hermann is in view from where you stand, use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for it and for details of the stonework.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the walls and tower with the vertical lines so they do not lean. Include a little foreground, such as paving or a path, to give a sense of scale, and use the telephoto to isolate a single stretch of wall, or the tower if it is in view.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to set focus and drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stonework keeps its detail. Take a quick burst or Live Photo if people are crossing your frame, so you can pick the cleanest moment.

Best time: Around sunrise or sunset, when the flag is raised or lowered on Tall Hermann. Soft early or late light usually flatters the stonework, but check how the light falls on the day.

2Seaplane Harbour

Seaplane Harbour (Lennusadam) is a maritime museum that opened in spring 2012, set in a building first put up as a hangar for seaplanes. It stands in the area of Peter the Great's Naval Fortress. The hall covers 8,000 m2 and was taken out of service in the Soviet era, and its renovation began in 2010. That history is why the place photographs well: big, plain hangar shapes sit right beside the water, and old ships are moored along the harbour edge. The museum's best-known exhibit is the 1936 submarine Lembit, and the icebreaker Suur Tõll is another of its historic vessels. Walk along the water's edge for a few steps before you shoot, because small changes in position change what lines up with the hangar. For a second angle, the Noblessner marina promenade across the cove shows the arched hangars with a grey warship moored in front of them. If you also walk to the museum itself, the open-air pier behind it is an optional extra viewpoint, with moored ships close by for the foreground. Inside the museum the hangar is dim, so plan to take your best pictures outdoors.

Where it is: Seaplane Harbour is on Tallinn's waterfront, north of the city centre, in the old Peter the Great's Naval Fortress area. The museum building was originally a seaplane hangar and sits right on the harbour edge.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the hangar, the water and the sky together. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull the hangar curves and roofline closer from this distance. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you walk to the museum itself and the roofline will not fit.

Where to stand: Stand about 460 m north-east of the subject, at Lennusadam (the phone icon on the map), and face south-west. The museum lies to the south-west of you, so face that way. From this distance the hangar hull and roofline appear as a distant shape across the water. If you walk to the museum, the open-air pier behind it is a separate, closer viewpoint where moored ships can make the foreground.

Framing: Turn on gridlines, keep the horizon level, and place the hangar hull on one of the vertical lines with the water filling the lower third. If you want a different view, the Noblessner marina promenade across the cove shows the arched hangars with a grey warship in front.

Settings tip: Tap on the hangar to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright so the building does not turn into a silhouette. Don't expect good results inside the dim hangar; if you shoot indoors, use night mode and hold still.

Best time: Soft light in the early morning or late afternoon usually flatters the hangar and the water, but the spot works in most daylight.

6Alexander Nevsky Cathedral

Alexander Nevsky Cathedral is the largest cupola church in Tallinn, and its five onion domes make it easy to spot on Toompea hill. It was built between 1894 and 1900, when Estonia was part of the Russian Empire, in the Russian Revival style, to a design by Mikhail Preobrazhensky. The base is Finnish granite and the domes carry gilded iron crosses. Inside there are eleven bells cast in Saint Petersburg, and the biggest weighs about 16 tons. The cathedral has a complicated place in Estonian history, and many Estonians have seen it as a symbol of Russian rule. It is also a working Orthodox church, so it is worth photographing with some respect. It photographs well because the shapes are so strong: the rounded domes, the pale decorated walls and the dark granite base give you clear layers even on a plain phone snap. Keep the whole building in view, then move in for a few detail shots of the domes and crosses. If people are coming and going, wait a moment for a clear gap rather than cropping them out later.

Where it is: On Toompea hill in central Tallinn, in the upper part of the Old Town area, with the cathedral standing as the main landmark of the hill.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the whole cathedral. If the domes are cut off at the top of the frame, switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x). Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the domes, crosses and decoration.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face east. From here the whole building and its domes should be in view.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and keep the vertical lines straight by holding the phone upright, not tilted back, so the walls do not lean. Place the cathedral on one side of the grid and leave a little sky above the highest dome. Then try a closer, zoomed shot of the five domes against the sky.

Settings tip: Tap the cathedral to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the pale walls and the sky keep their detail and the gilded crosses do not burn out.

Best time: Mid to late afternoon, when the sun is behind you and lights the side you are facing. Early morning also tends to be quieter.
