This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in Edinburgh as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Edinburgh Castle, Arthur's Seat, Royal Mile, Holyrood Palace and St Giles' Cathedral lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Edinburgh Castle

Edinburgh Castle sits on top of Castle Rock, the remains of an extinct volcano that rose roughly 350 million years ago. That is much of why it photographs so well. The rock is about 130 metres above sea level, with cliffs to the south, west and north that drop around 80 metres to the land below. The walls seem to grow straight out of the stone, and a phone camera picks up that drama easily. The only gently sloping approach is from the east. A royal castle has stood here since the 11th century, and research in 2014 counted 26 sieges in its 1,100-year history. St Margaret's Chapel, from the early 12th century, is regarded as the oldest building in Edinburgh. Most of the other buildings are younger, because the medieval defences were largely destroyed in the Lang Siege of 1573. From the recommended spot the castle is about 251 metres away, which is far enough to fit the whole rock and the skyline into one frame. Keep the rocky slope in the lower part of the picture to show how high the castle stands, and leave some sky above the towers. Take a few shots at different zoom levels, as a closer crop shows the walls and a wider one shows the setting.

Where it is: In the heart of Edinburgh, on top of Castle Rock above the city centre. The recommended spot is on the north-west side of the castle, about 251 m from it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the whole castle and rock in one frame. Switch to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the walls and towers. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of foreground or sky, because it makes the castle look small and far away.

Where to stand: Stand about 120 m north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east. From here you see the rock and the walls together. Stay put rather than walking closer, because the cliffs would then fill the frame and cut off the top of the castle.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the castle's skyline on the upper third line, with the rocky slope filling the lower third. Keep the phone level so the walls do not lean. Take one shot at 1x and one zoomed in, and choose the one that suits you later.

Settings tip: Tap on the castle walls to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the stonework keeps its detail. If the day is grey, nudge the exposure up slightly.

Best time: Soft light in the early morning or late afternoon usually flatters the stonework. After dark, switch to night mode and hold the phone as steady as you can against a wall or railing.

2Arthur's Seat and Salisbury Crags

Arthur's Seat is an ancient extinct volcano and the main peak of the group of hills that make up most of Holyrood Park, just east of Edinburgh city centre. It rises to 250.5 m (822 ft). Beneath it, the Salisbury Crags form a line of dark basalt cliffs facing the city, which is why the view works so well. You get a rugged hillside and sheer rock close to the streets and buildings. The lava is dated at roughly 341 to 335 million years old, and a glacier later scraped away the western side to leave those crags. From some angles the whole hill is said to look like a lion lying down, and two of its old volcanic vents are called the Lion's Head and the Lion's Haunch. Robert Louis Stevenson described it as "a hill for magnitude, a mountain in virtue of its bold design". For a phone photo, treat it as a big landscape. Include some foreground, such as the city or a bit of parkland, so the hill has a sense of scale. Zooming in gives you the crags and the summit as a clean, layered shape. Keep the horizon level, and wait for a moment when the sky has some cloud in it.

Where it is: Arthur's Seat sits in Holyrood Park, just east of Edinburgh city centre and about 1 mile (1.6 km) east of Edinburgh Castle. The Salisbury Crags run along the hill's edge on the city side.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start on the default lens (1x) for a wide view with the hill in context. Then try telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the crags and summit. Skip the ultra-wide (0.5x) here, as it will make the hill look small and distant.

Where to stand: Stand about 1.1 km north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the skyline of the hill on the upper third line. Fill the lower part of the frame with the city or the nearer land, so the hill looks as big as it is. Use telephoto for a tighter shot of the Salisbury Crags and the summit, and hold the phone steady with both hands, elbows tucked in.

Settings tip: Tap on the crags to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour and the rock stays dark and textured. If the haze is heavy, tapping on the hill rather than the sky usually helps.

Best time: Late afternoon or evening is usually best, when the low sun falls on the crags and the hill. Clear days after rain give the sharpest view.

3The Royal Mile

The Royal Mile is the main street of Edinburgh's Old Town. It is really a chain of streets: the Castle Esplanade, Castlehill, the Lawnmarket, the High Street, the Canongate and Abbey Strand. Together they run downhill from Edinburgh Castle to Holyrood Palace, and the distance is almost exactly one mile. The street falls from about 109 m above sea level at the castle to about 42 m at the palace, so it has a gentle, steady slope that shows well in a photo.

It photographs well because of the tall old tenement buildings (the 'lands') pressing in on both sides, with narrow alleyways called closes dropping away off the main street. Much of the south side of the High Street was rebuilt or refaced in a Georgian style after the Great Edinburgh Fire of 1824, and the buildings step down the hill. That gives you long, receding lines of rooftops and chimneys.

It is also the busiest tourist street in the Old Town, with shops, pubs, restaurants and visitor attractions, and the High Street gets especially crowded during the Edinburgh Fringe. So expect people in most shots. You can use them to give a sense of scale, or go early for a clearer view. Hold the phone upright for the height of the buildings, or turn it sideways to follow the street as it runs away from you. Look for a spot where the street bends or a close opens up, as these give your photo a clear focal point.

Where it is: The Royal Mile runs through the Old Town of Edinburgh, along the ridge between Edinburgh Castle at the top and Holyrood Palace at the bottom. The subject sits within the Royal Mile area.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the full height of the buildings and the sweep of the street. Use the default lens (1x) for natural-looking street scenes. Try the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out details such as rooflines, signs or a close entrance.

Where to stand: Stand about 25 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west. You are looking up the slope of the street, with the buildings on both sides leading your eye along it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the tall building edges with the vertical lines so they do not lean. Keep the street running diagonally through the frame, and include a close entrance or shopfront at the edge for depth. For a tighter shot, zoom in to 2x or more and frame a stretch of rooftops and chimneys.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest building or the sky to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky does not wash out. In the evening, use night mode and hold the phone steady against a wall or railing. A short Live Photo can capture the movement of the crowds.

Best time: Early in the morning is likely to be quieter and gives you the street with fewer people in it. Be prepared for crowds later in the day and especially during the Edinburgh Fringe.
