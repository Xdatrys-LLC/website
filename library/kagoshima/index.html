The 3 free photo spots

1Sakurajima

Sakurajima is the smoking volcano that sits across the bay from Kagoshima city, and it is the picture most people come here to take. Its summit has three peaks, and the highest, Kita-dake, rises to 1,117 m. The volcano is only about 4 km from the city across the water, so a single frame can hold the rooftops, the bay and the mountain together. It has been erupting almost constantly since 1955, so a small plume of ash is often part of the view. It was once a true island. The huge 1914 eruption sent lava into the narrow strait and joined it to the mainland, which is why it is now called a peninsula. The best-known view is from the Shiroyama Park observation deck, which looks over the city and Kagoshima Bay with the volcano rising behind the water. Guides treat it as the best free panorama in town. Early morning is usually the clearest, with fewer people, and the city lights after dark are also popular. Ash and haze are common, so look at the sky before you climb. If you want the volcano bigger in the frame, the Sakurajima ferry terminal gives a closer view, while the Sengan-en garden frames the volcano as a backdrop behind the garden.

Where it is: Sakurajima lies in Kagoshima Bay (Kinkō-wan), directly across the water from central Kagoshima city. The best-known viewpoint is on the hill of Shiroyama, above the city.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the classic city, bay and volcano frame. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the peaks closer and to pick out any ash plume. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of foreground, because the volcano will look smaller.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. The subject is Sakurajima itself. The Shiroyama Park observation deck (城山公園展望台), at the top of the Shiroyama promenade, is an alternative viewpoint across the bay. From the deck, Sakurajima rises across the water behind the city and Kagoshima Bay, so you can frame the city below and the volcano above it.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the horizon on the lower third line, so that the volcano and sky fill most of the frame. Keep a strip of city and bay at the bottom for scale. For a closer, more dramatic volcano, use the view from the Sakurajima ferry terminal as an alternative. The Sengan-en garden is another option, with the volcano as a backdrop behind the garden.

Settings tip: Tap the volcano to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky and ash plume keep their detail. In hazy conditions, a small boost in contrast when editing can help. After dark, use night mode and rest your elbows on the railing to keep the city lights sharp.

Best time: Early morning, before haze and crowds build up. The night city lights are a good second choice.

2Sengan-en

Sengan-en is a Japanese garden attached to a former residence of the Shimazu clan in Kagoshima. It is a designated Place of Scenic Beauty. Together with the neighbouring Shōko Shūseikan, it forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site 'Sites of Japan's Meiji Industrial Revolution: Iron and Steel, Shipbuilding and Coal Mining'. The garden is managed by Shimadzu Ltd.

It photographs well because the designers used the view of Sakurajima across Kagoshima Bay as 'borrowed scenery', so the volcano becomes the backdrop to the ponds, paths and planting. That gives you a layered picture, with garden in front and bay and mountain behind.

The residence was built by Shimazu Mitsuhisa in 1658. In 1736 Shimazu Yoshitaka added a winding water feature (a kyokusui) and moso bamboo that came from China by way of the Ryūkyū Kingdom. In the Bakumatsu and Meiji periods it hosted visiting dignitaries, including Harry Smith Parkes in 1866 and Edward VIII in 1922.

For your phone, look for a foreground such as water, a lantern or foliage, then let Sakurajima fill the top of the frame. Walk a little between pictures, because small changes in position make a big difference to how the garden and the mountain line up.

Where it is: Sengan-en is in Kagoshima, Kagoshima Prefecture, next to the Shōko Shūseikan, and its gardens borrow the view of Sakurajima across Kagoshima Bay.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the garden and the bay together. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) in tighter spots to fit in a pond or path. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring Sakurajima closer and to pick out details such as lanterns or bamboo.

Where to stand: Stand about 120 m north of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south. Take a few photos here, then walk towards the garden and try different positions.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the horizon or the line of the bay on the upper third. Keep something from the garden in the lower part of the frame so the picture has depth. If the volcano is visible, leave it room at the top rather than cropping it tight.

Settings tip: Tap on the garden to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is washing out. Try portrait mode for close-ups of leaves, lanterns or bamboo. Live Photos can capture moving water or leaves.

Best time: Morning or later in the afternoon usually gives softer light on the garden and a gentler sky behind the volcano than the middle of the day.

3Shiroyama Observation Deck

Shiroyama point. The hill is small, with a true height of 107 m. It was originally called Tsuru ga mine (鶴ヶ峯). It is best known as the place where the Battle of Shiroyama was fought in 1877, the closing episode of the Satsuma rebellion, so many visitors come for the history as well as the view.

For photographs, the appeal is height. On a clear day the view may extend over parts of the city, giving you a wide scene rather than a single building or street. If there are branches or a railing at the edge of the frame, you can use them to add depth.

Practical advice: allow a few minutes to look around before choosing your angle, because the view can change noticeably even a few steps to either side. Keep your lens clean, since a smudged phone lens is the most common reason for hazy distance shots. If the sky is bright and the land below is darker, tap on the distance and lower the exposure slightly. Check current local information before you go in case there are any access notices for the area.

Where it is: The spot is on Mount Shiroyama, a low hill within Kagoshima city in Kagoshima Prefecture, Japan.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the view. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the scene and sky in one frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out single details in the distance.

Where to stand: Stand about 70 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. Face north to photograph the hill itself, then turn around to see what view opens out.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and put the horizon on the upper or lower line, depending on whether the sky or the land below is more interesting. If there are branches or a railing at the edge of the frame, use them to give a sense of depth. Hold the phone level, because a tilted horizon is obvious in a wide view.

Settings tip: Tap on the part of the view you care about and slide the exposure down a little if the sky looks washed out. For a sweeping view, try panorama mode, moving slowly and keeping the phone steady. Use the 0.5x lens for tight spots.

Best time: A clear day gives the longest view. Morning or late afternoon usually brings softer light than midday. Check the weather before you go.

Where should I stand to photograph the Sakurajima?

When is the best time to photograph Sengan-en Garden?
