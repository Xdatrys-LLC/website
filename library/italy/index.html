The 3 free photo spots

1Colosseum, Rome

The Colosseum is an elliptical amphitheatre in the centre of Rome, just east of the Roman Forum. It is the largest ancient amphitheatre ever built, and it is still the largest standing amphitheatre in the world. Work began under Emperor Vespasian in AD 72 and finished in AD 80 under his successor and heir, Titus, with further changes made later under Domitian. The walls are built of travertine, Roman concrete and tuff, a volcanic rock. You can see how the stone has weathered, and where earthquakes and stone robbers have taken whole sections away.

It photographs well because the shape is so clear. The curved rows of arches repeat round the whole building, and the broken outer wall shows the layers behind it. That gives you strong patterns, depth and a real sense of age. The sources estimate that it held between 50,000 and 80,000 spectators, with an average crowd of about 65,000. Try a wide shot that shows how big it is, then a closer shot of the arches or the worn stone.

Expect people in most shots, as it is one of Rome's most popular tourist attractions. Use them to show scale, or wait a moment for a gap. Hold the phone steady and keep the horizon level so the arches don't lean.

Where it is: In the centre of Rome, just east of the Roman Forum, in the low valley between the Caelian, Esquiline and Palatine hills. The map marks the middle of the Colosseum's outline, which is 229 metres across.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the curve of the walls into one frame, because you are close. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view of a section of arches. If your phone has telephoto (2x-5x), use it for details such as the arches and the worn stone.

Where to stand: Stand about 110 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the arches up with the horizontal lines to keep the building straight. Leave some sky above the top edge so the outline reads clearly. Include a few people at the bottom of the frame to show the scale. For a detail shot, fill the frame with a run of arches and let the rhythm do the work.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the stone keeps its colour and texture. Live Photos can help if people walk through the frame, because you can pick the best moment afterwards.

Best time: Early morning is usually quieter, and the side facing you should catch the low sun. Around sunset, try night mode or a warm-toned shot as the light fades.

2Tre Cime di Lavaredo, Dolomites

The Tre Cime di Lavaredo, also called the Drei Zinnen, are three blocky, battlement-like peaks in the Sexten Dolomites. Seen from east to west they are Cima Piccola, Cima Grande and Cima Ovest. The tallest, Cima Grande, reaches 2,999 metres, with Cima Ovest at 2,973 metres and Cima Piccola at 2,857 metres. Together they are among the best-known mountain groups in the Alps, and the surrounding area has been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2009.

They photograph well because the three towers stand side by side with nothing in front of them but open alpine ground, so the whole group fits into one tidy frame. The pale rock stands out against sky, and the sheer walls look huge even on a small screen. Cima Grande was first climbed on 21 August 1869, which gives you a nice story to tell with your picture.

The phone icon on the map is about 860 m north-east of the subject. That is close enough to feel their scale, while the whole group still sits in one frame. Keep the horizon level and include some foreground path or grass to give a sense of size. Take a few frames over several minutes, because mountain weather here can change fast, with fog or wind arriving quickly even in summer. Dress warmly and wear proper shoes for the walk.

Where it is: The Tre Cime stand in the Sexten Dolomites in north-eastern Italy, on the border between South Tyrol and the province of Belluno. They lie about 15 km north-east of Cortina d'Ampezzo in a straight line, with the town of Toblach to the north-west.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: From the viewpoint the peaks are just under 1 km away. Start with the default lens (1x) to take in all three towers with the path and slopes below them. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more foreground and sky, and telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a close shot of one summit, such as Cima Grande.

Where to stand: Stand about 860 m north-east of the subject, at the viewpoint just above Forcella Lavaredo (Paternsattel), at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. The three north faces line up in front of you, Cima Piccola nearest, Cima Grande in the middle and Cima Ovest on the right.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the base of the peaks on the lower third line, with the sky taking the upper two thirds. Keep the phone level so the walls do not lean. Add a bit of path, rock or grass at the bottom edge for scale. Try one wide shot and one tighter shot of a single peak.

Settings tip: Tap on the rock face to set focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the sky keeps its colour and the pale rock does not wash out. Try a panorama if you want the whole ridge, holding the phone steady and moving slowly. Wipe the lens first, as cold and damp air can smear it.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when the sun is lower, usually gives more shape and colour to the rock than the flat light of midday. Weather here changes quickly, so check the forecast before setting out and be ready to shoot when the cloud breaks.

3Positano, Amalfi Coast

Positano is a small town on the Amalfi Coast, in the province of Salerno in Campania. It can photograph well on a phone, because buildings give you colour, texture and a sense of height, and any water in view gives a simple backdrop. Start with a wide view that shows the houses and, if it is visible, the sea. Then move in on smaller details such as doorways, balconies, shutters and plants. If people pass through your frame, wait for a gap. Keep your phone steady against your body, or rest your arms on a wall. Take a few frames of each view and choose the best one later. Watch your footing while looking at the screen, and step aside so others can pass. If you want to visit a particular viewpoint, beach or paid attraction, check the official site before you go.

Where it is: Positano is on the Amalfi Coast in the province of Salerno, in the Campania region of southern Italy. The spot is in Positano, in the Campania region of Italy.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the overall view of the town. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) in narrow lanes or when you want more of the buildings in the frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out groups of buildings.

Where to stand: Stand about 90 m south-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and, if the sea or horizon is in view, place its line on a lower third line. Let the buildings fill the rest of the frame. Keep the phone level so the buildings do not lean. For a second shot, step in and frame a doorway or balcony with a small bit of colour in the foreground.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest wall to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the sky and pale walls keep their detail. Try a Live Photo while people walk past, as you can pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light, but the town can be photographed at any time of day.
