This map shows 14 of the most-searched sights in Galway as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Galway Cathedral, Spanish Arch, Eyre Square, Shop Street and Salthill Promenade lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Galway Cathedral

Galway Cathedral is the big limestone landmark on Nun's Island, with a green dome that shows above the rooftops and the river. Building started in 1958 on the site of the old Galway Gaol and finished in 1965. It is often described as the last great stone cathedral built in Europe. The architect was John J. Robinson. The dome and pillars follow a Renaissance style, and the rose windows and mosaics draw on wider Christian art. The dome stands 44.2 metres high, which is why it is easy to spot across the city.

Guides call this the classic postcard view. The cathedral rises from Nun's Island with the River Corrib and the bridge in the foreground, so you get the whole building and some water in one frame. The stone is a pale, even colour, so the green dome stands out well against the sky.

For a second view, try the pedestrian bridge beside Salmon Weir Bridge. It frames the cathedral from closer range, with the canal and water in front. Check the map before you go to find it. The cathedral is an active place of worship, so don't photograph services and don't use flash inside.

Where it is: Galway Cathedral stands on Nun's Island, beside the River Corrib. The Salmon Weir Bridge viewpoint is on the river beside it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) on the bridge to fit the dome, the building and some river in one frame. Use the telephoto (2x-3x, depending on your phone) for the dome and its details. Only use the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you move to the closer pedestrian bridge and the cathedral won't fit otherwise.

Where to stand: Stand on Salmon Weir Bridge. The cathedral's green domed exterior rising from Nun's Island, with the river and the bridge in the foreground. From there the green dome rises from Nun's Island, with the Corrib and the bridge in the foreground. Pick a spot where the parapet or railing doesn't cut across the cathedral.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the water in the bottom third and the dome near the upper line. Keep the phone level so the walls don't lean. If the crowd or traffic is in the way, try the pedestrian bridge beside the bridge for a closer view across the canal.

Settings tip: Tap the dome to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is washing out. If the river is calm, a Live Photo can catch small ripples in the reflection.

Best time: A quiet morning or around sunset, when the dome catches the changing light.

3Eyre Square

Eyre Square is a rectangular public park in the middle of Galway, right in the city centre, next to the shopping streets of William Street and Shop Street. It began as a medieval green in front of a town gate and was given to the city in 1710 by Mayor Edward Eyre, who gave it his name. The west side was pedestrianised when the square reopened on 13 April 2006. John F. Kennedy made a speech here on 29 June 1963, and a bust of him stands in the park.

For photographs, the Quincentennial Fountain at the northern end is the easiest starting point. Its bronze hooker-boat sculpture makes a strong foreground, with the open square and shopfronts behind it, and it ties the square to Galway's maritime heritage. Guides call it the most photographed feature here. For a second picture, walk to the Browne Doorway on the east side. It is a carved stone doorway that was moved to the square in 1905 from a Browne family home on Lower Abbeygate Street. Frame it from the lawns or paving so the trees and open space show around it. In the north section you may also spot the banners of the fourteen Tribes of Galway, which add colour to a wider shot.

The square gets busy. Street performers are often about, so ask before photographing one person. The Christmas market (November to January, with a Big Wheel) and festival events can block views and crowd the fountain.

Where it is: Eyre Square is in Galway city centre, next to the William Street and Shop Street shopping area. Galway railway station is right beside it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the fountain and its bronze boat. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) when you want the whole open square and the shopfronts behind. Use 2x on the telephoto for a closer, tidier view of the Browne Doorway.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (northern end of the square). The fountain's bronze hooker-boat sculpture in the foreground, with the open square and surrounding shopfronts behind it. At the northern end of the square. From here the bronze hooker-boat sculpture is in the foreground, and the open square and surrounding shopfronts spread out to the south behind it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the boat sculpture on one of the lower third points, leaving the open square and shopfronts behind it. Crouch slightly to lift the boat against the background. For a second picture, go to the Browne Doorway on the east side and frame it from the lawns or paving, with trees and open space around it.

Settings tip: Tap the boat sculpture to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright so the bronze keeps its detail. If the Browne Doorway is behind plexiglass, hold the phone close to the glass and angle it slightly to cut reflections. If people walk through your shot, try a Live Photo and pick the cleanest frame.

Best time: Golden hour, for warm light and reflections on the fountain water. Early in the day is likely to be quieter. Expect crowds and blocked views during the Christmas market and festival events.

11Salmon Weir Bridge

Salmon Weir Bridge carries traffic and pedestrians across the River Corrib in the middle of Galway, with the weir running beside it and Galway Cathedral's dome rising above the trees on the far bank. That mix of old stone, fast water and a big landmark is why it photographs so well. The bridge was designed by William Vitruvius Morrison to link the new Courthouse with the new Gaol on Nun's Island, and it was first known as the Gaol Bridge. Its foundation stone was laid on 29 June 1818 and it opened in October 1819. It is on Galway City Council's Record of Protected Structures.

It is on Droichead an Dóchais, the newer pedestrian and cycle bridge that runs alongside the original. From this parallel deck you look across at the older stone bridge with the Cathedral behind it. Guides suggest it as a quieter alternative to the busier view from the old bridge itself, and there is seating here with a good view of the Cathedral. The older bridge itself gives the classic view of the river tumbling over the weir with the dome above the trees, so it is worth a second shot if you have time. If you want a salmon photo, check the run dates on the day. The Corrib can be dangerous in flood, so keep to the marked walkways.

Where it is: In the centre of Galway city, where the River Corrib passes the weir beside Nun's Island, with Galway Cathedral on the far bank.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the old bridge, the water and the Cathedral together. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the dome. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want the whole deck and railings in the foreground.

Where to stand: Stand on Droichead an Dóchais (the new pedestrian and cycle bridge, alongside the original bridge). The older stone bridge and the Cathedral seen from a parallel deck, giving a less crowded angle on the same scene. From here the older stone bridge and the Cathedral appear side by side, with the old bridge to the north-west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the old bridge's arches along a lower line, with the Cathedral dome near an upper third. Keep the phone level so the parapets stay straight. For a different view, try the old Salmon Weir Bridge itself, looking at the river over the weir with the dome above the trees.

Settings tip: Tap the water or the stonework to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky does not turn white. Try a Live Photo to catch the movement of the water.

Best time: Early in the day is likely to be quieter on the bridge. Soft, even light usually suits the stonework and the water. Check the salmon run dates if you hope to photograph fish.
