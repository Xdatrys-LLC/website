This map shows 10 of the most-searched sights in Banff as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Banff Springs Hotel, Banff Gondola, Upper Hot Springs, Bow Falls and Vermilion Lakes lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Fairmont Banff Springs Hotel

The Fairmont Banff Springs, long known as the Banff Springs Hotel, looks like a Scottish-French castle set among the Rockies. It opened in 1888 as one of Canada's early grand railway hotels, built by the Canadian Pacific Railway. The original wooden building burned down in 1926. What you see now is mostly the 1914 centre tower with the 1927 and 1928 wings either side, built in Rundle limestone with steep copper roofs, dormers and corner turrets. The hotel buildings became a National Historic Site of Canada in 1988. It photographs well because the pale stonework and pointed roofs stand out against the forested slopes behind. Water fills the foreground, which gives the picture depth, and the hotel rises in the distance. It is a closer and usually quieter alternative to the roadside lookout. The angle is described less precisely than at the main lookout, so spend a minute walking along the bank to find a clear line between the rapids and the towers. If you also want the classic view across the river, Surprise Corner shows the hotel at the foot of Sulphur Mountain, with the mountains framing it.

Where it is: The hotel stands near the southern edge of Banff town, close to where the Spray and Bow rivers meet. Bow Falls lies north of the hotel on the Bow River.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the rapids and the hotel together. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the towers closer. The ultra-wide (0.5x) suits a close shot of the rushing water, but the hotel will look small.

Where to stand: Stand in Bow Falls (east side of the falls, looking upstream over the rapids toward the hotel). Rapids in the foreground with the hotel rising in the distance, a closer and less crowded alternative to the roadside lookout. On the east side of the falls, and look upstream over the rapids. The hotel sits to the south, rising in the distance beyond the water.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the rapids in the lower third, with the hotel roofs on one of the upper lines. Keep the horizon level. If you want the classic view with the hotel at the foot of Sulphur Mountain, Surprise Corner is an alternative; keep well back from the cliff edge there, as loose gravel is reported.

Settings tip: Tap the hotel to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the pale stone and bright water don't wash out. A Live Photo can capture the movement of the water, and you can choose the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Early morning or later in the day usually gives softer light on the stonework and fewer people on the bank.

2Banff Gondola

The Banff Gondola is a gondola lift, and it is the second stop on this map. It photographs well because it combines a clear, recognisable subject (the building and the cabins on their cable) with open scenery behind it, so even a quick snap tells a story about where you are. The phone icon on the map is at the gondola itself, so you can shoot the building and the cabins without walking far. Start with a wide shot to show the building against the sky. Then step in closer for details such as a cabin, the cable and any people nearby, which give the picture a sense of scale and activity. If the cabins are moving, wait for one to come into a clear patch of sky before you press the shutter. Keep the horizon level using your phone's gridlines, and watch for glare on glass and metal, which can fool the exposure. If you plan to ride, the views from the top are a separate set of photos. Check the official site for current access information before you go.

Where it is: The Banff Gondola is the second spot on the Banff map.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the building and the cabins together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the surroundings and sky in the frame as well. Use the telephoto lens (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a single cabin on the cable.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Move a few steps back or to one side to find a clear view without obstructions in front of you.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the horizon and the building's edges level. Include some of the surroundings and sky behind the gondola to show where it is. For a second picture, move in and frame a cabin against the sky with a bit of empty space around it.

Settings tip: Tap the cabin or building to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright, so the details stay clear. Try a Live Photo or motion shot when a cabin is passing, then choose the best frame.

Best time: Go when the light is soft, such as early morning or late afternoon, and aim for a day with some clear sky. Midday sun can bring harsh glare.

3Banff Upper Hot Springs

Banff Upper Hot Springs are outdoor hot pools on the slopes above the Banff townsite, set in Banff National Park. The water is kept at about 38 °C, the hottest of the Banff springs, and at 1,585 metres the pool is the highest hot spring in Canada. Indigenous Peoples including the Stoney Nakoda and Secwépemc used the springs long before Europeans arrived in 1883. The bathhouse was declared a Registered Federal Heritage Building in 1931.

For photographs, the best free view is from Sulphur Mountain Trail, a short way up the trail above the springs. From there you look down over the bathhouse buildings and pools, with the surrounding peaks beyond. The height lets you show the whole setting in one frame instead of a close-up of steam and heads. Bathers in the pool look across the valley to Mount Rundle, while the trail view shows the surrounding peaks.

Take a few frames from the trail, then walk back down for a closer look at the buildings. If you go into the pool area, you can also photograph the steaming water with Mount Rundle's jagged profile behind it. That area is paid, so check the official site before you go. Keep your phone away from wet hands and steam, and wipe the lens before each shot, because a misted lens makes everything look soft.

Where it is: The springs are on the slopes above the Banff townsite, in Banff National Park.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start on the default lens (1x) for the full view down over the buildings and pools. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the pools and the peak shapes. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of sky and slope, and keep buildings away from the edges, where they stretch.

Where to stand: Stand in Sulphur Mountain Trail (a short way up the trail above the springs). A higher, elevated view looking down over the hot springs buildings and pools toward the surrounding peaks. A short way up the trail above the springs. From here the springs lie to the west, and you look down over the hot springs buildings and pools towards the surrounding peaks.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the buildings and pools in the lower third, with the mountains filling the top. Include a bit of trail or trees at the edge of the frame to give depth. As an alternative, the pool edge gives a close view of the steaming water with Mount Rundle rising behind it, but that part is paid.

Settings tip: Tap on the pools or buildings to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky keeps its colour and the steam does not turn white. Wipe the lens first, as steam and spray leave a film.

Best time: Soft light early or late in the day usually flatters the mountains and the buildings. Cooler air tends to show more steam over the pools.
