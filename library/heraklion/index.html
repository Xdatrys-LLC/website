This map shows 11 of the most-searched sights in Heraklion as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Knossos Palace, Archaeological Museum, Koules Fortress, Morosini Fountain and Historical Museum lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Knossos Palace

Knossos is the largest and best-known centre of the Minoan civilisation, and the palace on Kephala hill is the part most visitors come to photograph. The first palace was built around 1900 BC. After a major destruction around 1700 BC it was rebuilt on a grander scale, with a central court, monumental entrances, staircases and light wells. In 2025 Knossos was inscribed as part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site Minoan Palatial Centres. For a phone photo, the best place to start is the Central Court. This open rectangular courtyard is surrounded by palace walls and columns, so one frame can show how the whole complex is laid out. If you can reach the upper terrace looking down into the court, that gives the clearest view. Then walk down to the middle of the courtyard floor and look south towards Mount Juktas. Once you have the wide view, move in on details such as column bases, stone steps and the rhythm of shadows on the walls. If you have time, the South Propylaeum (the entrance front) is lovely in warm evening light. The Grand Staircase has repeating stone landings and columns that suit close-up pictures. Some interior rooms, such as the Throne Room, can only be seen from the doorway behind a rope, and flash is not allowed there. Go early if you can, to get fewer people in your shots.

Where it is: Knossos lies in the southern part of Heraklion, on Kephala hill in the Kairatos river valley, about 5 km south of the historic centre. The palace is the main feature of the archaeological site.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole courtyard and its surrounding walls into one frame. Use the default lens (1x) for a more natural look, and the telephoto or zoom (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as columns and stonework.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (upper terrace overlooking the court, then the courtyard floor at its centre). The open rectangular courtyard with the surrounding palace walls and columns, framed to show the layout of the whole complex. From the middle of the floor, look south towards Mount Juktas.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the courtyard floor in the lower third, with the walls and columns filling the middle and the sky or Mount Juktas above. Keep the phone level so the walls don't lean. For a second picture, try the South Propylaeum for the entrance front in evening light, or the Grand Staircase for repeating shapes and shadows.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest stone wall to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the pale stone keeps its detail in strong sun. Don't use flash in the interior rooms; it is banned there. If you want a panorama of the court, pan slowly and keep your arm steady.

Best time: Early morning, before the tour groups arrive. Late afternoon is also worth trying for warmer light.

2Heraklion Archaeological Museum

The Heraklion Archaeological Museum is a calm, pale modernist building in Heraklion, and it is the main place to see Minoan art in Crete. It holds most of the finds from the palace at Knossos and from other Minoan sites on the island, with objects spanning about 5,500 years, from the Neolithic period to Roman times. The collection began in 1883. The present building was designed by Patroklos Karantinos in the 1930s and has stood up to earthquakes and wartime bombing. Its use of veined, multicoloured marble echoes the painted imitation marble of Minoan palaces, which makes the front a pleasing subject on its own. Outside, the clean lines and flat facade suit a phone well. Stand square on to the building, switch on the gridlines and line up the horizontals with the edge of the frame. The building is the subject at the phone icon on the map itself, so you do not need to walk far to find a good angle. Step back a little to fit more of the front in at the default lens (1x), or use the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the pavement is narrow. Inside, some exhibits cannot be photographed, and signs are usually posted near them, so look for those before you raise your phone. The museum is a ticketed site, so check the official site before you go for current times and entry rules.

Where it is: The museum is in Heraklion on the island of Crete.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural view of the front. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you cannot step back far enough to fit the building in.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the building's horizontal lines level with them. Hold the phone straight rather than tilting it upwards, so the walls do not lean inwards. Include a little of the pavement for scale.

Settings tip: Tap the front of the building to focus, then slide the exposure slider down a touch if the pale walls look washed out against the sky.

Best time: Go in the morning or late afternoon, when the light is softer and the stone looks less flat. Check the official site for opening times.

3Koules Fortress

Koules is the fortress that stands at the entrance to Heraklion's harbour, and it photographs best from the water's edge. The classic picture is taken from the harbour side, on the approach along the breakwater (the outer harbour mole). From there the fortress sits at the end of the view, with moored yachts and fishing boats in the foreground and the water all around it. The boats give the stone walls scale and a bit of colour, so the picture feels like a place rather than just a building.

Guides treat the breakwater as the best outside vantage point and recommend it for sunset, when golden light falls on the stone. Walk a little way along the mole before you start shooting, and move along it to try different arrangements of the boats in the foreground. Keep the fortress in the upper part of the frame and let the boats and water fill the lower part.

There is a second option inside the fortress. The rooftop gives wide views over the harbour, the fishing boats below and the city of Heraklion spreading out behind. The rooftop is inside the ticketed fortress, so admission is needed and opening hours change with the season. Check the current opening hours before you go. Photography inside is generally fine as long as you stay considerate of other visitors.

Where it is: Koules stands at the mouth of Heraklion's harbour, at the end of the outer breakwater, next to the moored yachts and fishing boats of the harbour.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the full scene of fortress, boats and water. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close to the boats and want more of them in the foreground. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the walls.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (harbour side, from the approach along the breakwater (mole) toward the fortress). The fortress standing at the harbour entrance with moored yachts and fishing boats in the foreground, framed against the water.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the fortress on one of the upper lines, with the moored boats along the lower part of the frame. Keep the horizon level. For a different version, the rooftop inside the fortress gives wide harbour and city views, though you will need a ticket.

Settings tip: Tap on the stone walls to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the sky and water keep their colour at sunset. Try a Live Photo, as the boats moving on the water can give you a nicer frame to pick afterwards.

Best time: Sunset, when the golden light falls on the stone.
