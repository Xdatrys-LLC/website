The 3 free photo spots

1Château Frontenac

The Château Frontenac is the big copper-roofed hotel that dominates the Old Quebec skyline, and it has a fair claim to being the most photographed hotel in the world. It was designed by the New York architect Bruce Price for the Canadian Pacific Railway and opened in 1893. It has 18 floors and stands about 80 metres tall. It also sits on a high promontory above the St. Lawrence River, so it looks even taller than it is. The style is called Châteauesque. The steep roofs, round towers, turrets, dormers and tall chimneys were inspired by medieval châteaux in France's Loire Valley, and the building's outline is deliberately uneven. That makes it very easy to photograph. The hotel was named a National Historic Site of Canada in 1981, and an earlier building, the Château Haldimand, stood on the site until it was demolished in 1892. The classic view is from Terrasse Dufferin, the boardwalk in front of the hotel. From there you see the full façade and the copper roof rising above the boards, with the river at your back. The boardwalk runs along the cliff edge, so you can try several framings. If you want a second angle, Terrasse Pierre-Dugua-De-Mons looks down on the hotel and terrace with the skyline around them. Rue Dalhousie shows the hotel towering over older stone buildings on a cobbled street. The terrace gets very crowded in summer and during cruise season, so go early if you want a clear shot.

Where it is: In the Upper Town of Old Quebec, on the southern side of Place d'Armes, right on the edge of the cliff above the Lower Town and the St. Lawrence River. Terrasse Dufferin wraps around its eastern side.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the whole façade and the roof in one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the towers, turrets and copper roof.

Where to stand: Stand on Terrasse Dufferin (boardwalk directly in front of the hotel, near the Dufferin kiosk). The Château Frontenac's full façade and copper roof rising above the boardwalk, with the St. Lawrence River behind you as you face the hotel. Face north-west towards the hotel, with the St. Lawrence River behind you. From here you see the full façade and copper roof rising above the boardwalk.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a tower up with a vertical line so the building doesn't lean. Keep the phone level and don't tilt it upwards, or the sides will converge. Use the boardwalk rail or boards as a foreground line. Winter toboggan runs on the terrace can end up in the frame, so check the edges before you shoot. For a different view, try Terrasse Pierre-Dugua-De-Mons from above, which involves stairs or a walk up from the terrace.

Settings tip: Tap the sky or the roof to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour and the copper roof isn't washed out. In crowds, take a few shots in a row and keep the one with the fewest people in it.

Best time: Early morning, before the tour buses arrive. One local guide gives about 6:30 AM in summer and 8:30 AM in winter. The terrace is very crowded in summer and during cruise season.

2Montmorency Falls

Montmorency Falls drops 83 m into the St Lawrence River, which is about 30 m higher than Niagara Falls. That height is what makes it so photogenic. A tall white ribbon of water falls against a dark cliff, with the river and the western end of Île d'Orléans beyond. The falls sit at the mouth of the Montmorency River, about 12 km from the heart of old Quebec City, inside a protected park. In summer the water can take on a yellowish tint from the iron in the riverbed, which gives the scene a warmer colour. For a closer, quieter angle, head to La Baronne, a clifftop observation deck beside the falls. From the deck the cascade looks tight and close, and you can also take in the gorge and the view across toward Île d'Orléans. Guides call it quieter than the bridge. If you have time, two other spots in the park give different views. The suspension bridge over the top of the falls looks down on the drop with the river and the Quebec City skyline behind. The Escalier Panoramique gives an upward view through the spray. Take a few frames from each and compare. The park has staircases, a suspension bridge and a funitel, so allow time to walk between viewpoints. Early morning is the calmest time to photograph.

Where it is: Montmorency Falls lies on the boundary between the Beauport borough of Quebec City and Boischatel, where the Montmorency River meets the St Lawrence. It is about 12 km from the heart of old Quebec City, and the viewing area is inside Montmorency Falls Park.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole scene. Then try telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the cascade, and the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the gorge and the view across toward Île d'Orléans.

Where to stand: Stand on La Baronne (clifftop observation deck beside the falls). A tighter, closer angle on the cascade from the clifftop, with the gorge and the view across toward Île d'Orléans. From here the falls are close by, to the north-east, and you look at the cascade from a tighter, closer angle than from the bridge, with the gorge and Île d'Orléans in the view across the water.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and put the cascade on one of the vertical lines, with the cliff edge along a lower line so the drop looks tall. Use telephoto for a close crop of the water. Use the ultra-wide when you want the gorge and the distant island in the same frame. For a different view, the suspension bridge looks down the drop toward the river and the skyline, and the Escalier Panoramique looks up through the spray.

Settings tip: Tap on the white water to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the foam keeps its detail instead of turning plain white. A Live Photo can capture the movement of the water. Wipe the lens often if there is spray.

Best time: Early morning, before the crowds arrive, according to one photo guide.

6Dufferin Terrace

Dufferin Terrace is a long boardwalk that runs along the cliff edge beside the Château Frontenac, with the St. Lawrence River open below. It photographs well because it gives you a strong leading line, the boardwalk, with the grand hotel on one side and the wide river on the other. This is on Pierre-Dugua-De-Mons Terrace, an elevated lookout above the boardwalk. From there you see the length of Dufferin Terrace below, with the Château Frontenac and the river beyond. The terrace was built under the Marquess of Dufferin, then Governor General of Canada, and opened on 28 June 1879. Parks Canada looks after it. Along the boardwalk are six named gazebos, which make pleasing repeating shapes in a wide shot. At the southern end is a 150-metre ramp that becomes a toboggan run during the Quebec Winter Carnival. Cruise season and summer street performers can make the boardwalk busy, so expect people in your frame. If you want a close view of the boardwalk running toward the hotel, the Monument Samuel-de-Champlain makes a good second spot. Plateforme du Roi is another, for open river views along the terrace edge. Take your time and let people walk out of the frame before you shoot.

Where it is: Dufferin Terrace is in the historic heart of Quebec City, wrapped around the Château Frontenac on the cliff edge above the St. Lawrence River. It runs from the hotel towards the Citadelle.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to show the boardwalk, the hotel and the river together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if the terrace is busy or you want the whole length of the boardwalk and the hotel in one frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the gazebos and the hotel towers.

Where to stand: Stand on Pierre-Dugua-De-Mons Terrace (elevated lookout above the boardwalk, reached by a path or stairs from near the Château Frontenac). The length of Dufferin Terrace below, with the Château Frontenac and the St. Lawrence River beyond. The elevated lookout above the boardwalk, with Dufferin Terrace to the north of you. From here the photo shows the length of the terrace below, with the Château Frontenac and the St. Lawrence River beyond. It is the classic higher-angle view.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the boardwalk up with a grid line so it leads the eye towards the hotel. Keep the river as a calm band along one edge. For a different view, Plateforme du Roi gives open river views framed by the boardwalk. The Monument Samuel-de-Champlain gives a close view with the statue in the foreground.

Settings tip: Tap on the hotel to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour. Try panorama mode only if you can hold the phone steady, and wait for passers-by to clear.

Best time: Early in the day or later in the afternoon is usually calmer and kinder to the light than midday. Cruise season and summer street performers can make it crowded.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Quebec City?

The first three on this map are Château Frontenac, Montmorency Falls and Plains of Abraham. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 13.

Where should I stand to photograph the Château Frontenac?

Stand on Terrasse Dufferin (boardwalk directly in front of the hotel, near the Dufferin kiosk). Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the whole façade and the roof in one frame.

When is the best time to photograph Montmorency Falls?

Early morning, before the crowds arrive, according to one photo guide.

How do I get to Dufferin Terrace?

There are no stations or tram stops for this spot, so head for the Château Frontenac.
