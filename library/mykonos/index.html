This map shows 9 of the most-searched sights in Mykonos as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Little Venice, Kato Mili windmills, Paradise Beach, Psarou Beach and Paraportiani Church lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Little Venice

Little Venice is a row of 18th-century captains' houses whose balconies hang out over the water, with the Aegean right behind them. That mix of old houses, sea and open sky is why it is so popular with visitors, and guides call it the most photographed viewpoint on the island. The best-known angle is from the small promenade at the north end, looking south along the balconies. From there you get clean, front-on shots of the building facades without too much clutter in the frame. A phone copes well here, because the whole scene is close and colourful. Stand still for a moment before you shoot and check that the horizon and the balcony lines are straight. If you have time, try a second shot a little lower or from a different corner so that the water fills more of the picture. The place gets very crowded from late afternoon in summer, and the terrace seats fill up before sunset, so arrive early if you want a clear view. Two nearby spots give a different take. Kato Mili shows the whitewashed windmills in the foreground with Little Venice and the bay below, but it is often windy at the top. Panagia Paraportiani gives a lower angle of Little Venice with the windmills in the background; take care on the uneven ground there.

Where it is: Little Venice is on the Mykonos waterfront. It sits close to the Kato Mili windmills and the Panagia Paraportiani church.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole row of balconies and the sea into one frame from the small promenade. Then switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural look, and use the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) to pick out balcony and window details.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From there you see the row of 18th-century captains' houses with their balconies over the water, framed against the Aegean.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the balcony row with one of the horizontal lines so the houses do not lean. Keep a strip of sea or sky in the frame to show how close the houses are to the water. For a different view, try Kato Mili for the windmills in the foreground with Little Venice below, or Panagia Paraportiani for a lower angle.

Settings tip: Tap on the whitewashed walls to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the white does not wash out and the sea and sky keep their colour. If the light is low near sunset, hold the phone steady against your body or a railing. Take a few frames in case someone walks through.

Best time: About 45 minutes before sunset, before the promenade fills with people. Some visitors prefer sunrise for emptier streets.

2Mykonos windmills

The Mykonos windmills are the island's best-known skyline: a row of round, whitewashed towers with pointed roofs and very small windows, standing on a hill above the town and the sea. There are 16 windmills on the island, and seven of them stand on this hill in Chora, the main town. Most were built by the Venetians in the 16th century, though building carried on into the early 20th century. They ground wheat and were an important source of income for local people until production stopped in the middle of the 20th century. One of them is now a museum.

They photograph well because the shapes are simple and repeat: white walls, pointed roofs and a lot of open sky. Most of them face north, towards the strongest winds. Take one wide shot to show the whole row against the sky, then step closer for a single tower with its door and small windows. Look down the hill too: Chora and part of the harbour can be seen from this high ground, and they make a good second picture with the windmills at the edge of the frame. Allow a few minutes, as other visitors will be walking through your shot.

Where it is: On the hill overlooking the harbour on the edge of Chora (Mykonos Town), on the island of Mykonos in the Cyclades.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the whole row against the sky, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a tidier view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out one windmill or the town below.

Where to stand: Stand about 30 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. Take your wide shots from here, then walk the minute or so towards them for close-ups.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the row of windmills along the lower third, leaving the sky above. Keep the horizon level, as the white towers make any tilt obvious. For a close shot, frame one windmill with its pointed roof against clear sky and keep other visitors out of the edges.

Settings tip: Tap on a white wall to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the white does not turn grey or lose detail in bright sun. If the sky looks flat, tap on it and check the brightness again.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when the sun is lower, gives softer light and more shape to the white walls. Early or late in the day is often calmer too, but check on the day.

3Paradise Beach

Paradise Beach is a beach on Mykonos, and it photographs well because of its shape. The sand curves around a bay, with beach clubs, umbrellas and sunbeds lined up along it. That repeating pattern gives a phone photo plenty of colour and structure.

The best overview comes from the rocks at the edges of the bay, above the beach clubs. From up there you can see the whole bay at once, with the umbrellas and sunbeds running along the sand. A guide also says these rocks work well for a photoshoot, so they suit portraits of friends with the bay behind them. Check on the day that the rocks are still accessible.

On the sand, look for simple compositions: the water's edge, rows of sunbeds, or one umbrella against the sea. Early morning is suggested for quieter water and sand compositions. In high season, expect crowds and music from the beach clubs, so keep people out of the frame or use them to show the atmosphere. Try a wide shot first, then a closer one of a detail such as a row of umbrellas. Keep your phone lens clean, as sea air and sunscreen quickly smear it.

Where it is: Paradise Beach is on the coast of Mykonos, on a bay lined with beach clubs. Agia Anna is a separate beach nearby.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) from the rocks to fit the whole bay and the line of beach clubs into one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural view of the umbrellas and sunbeds. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a row of umbrellas or a single detail on the sand.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Stand on Paradise Beach, on the rocks at the edges of the bay, above the beach clubs. From here the photo shows a wide view of the whole bay, with the beach clubs and their umbrellas and sunbeds lining the sand.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on one of the lines so the bay looks level. Let the curve of the sand lead the eye along the picture. For a portrait, use portrait mode with your subject on the rocks and the bay behind. Panorama also works if you move slowly and keep the phone steady. Wait for people to move out of the way before you press the shutter.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on the beach clubs or your subject, then use the exposure slider to bring the brightness down a little so the pale sand and sky keep their detail. Clean the lens before you start.

Best time: Early morning is suggested for quieter water and sand compositions, and it also means fewer people and less music.
