The 3 free photo spots

2Bourbon Street

Bourbon Street runs through the heart of the French Quarter, twelve blocks from Canal Street to Esplanade Avenue. It is best known for its bars, restaurants and music, but it also photographs well in daylight. Wrought-iron balconies, shuttered doorways and old brick and stucco fronts line both sides. Much of that look comes from the Spanish rebuilding after the Great New Orleans Fire of 1788, which is why the street feels more Spanish than French. The name itself dates from 1721 and honours the French royal House of Bourbon. Since the early 1970s the street has been a pedestrian mall, so you can stand and frame a shot without traffic, though the crowds can be thick later in the day. For a phone photo, the quietest option is early morning, when the street is nearly empty and the shutters are down. You get clean lines, the balconies and the full length of the street without people in the way. If you want the classic busy look, with neon, bars and balconies stretching into the distance, come back after dark and shoot down the strip. Another good frame is at Orleans Street and Bourbon Street, where the narrow street leads the eye to the back of St. Louis Cathedral. Keep your phone close and be aware of people around you in the evening.

Where it is: Bourbon Street is in the French Quarter, the historic centre of New Orleans. It runs between Canal Street at one end and Esplanade Avenue at the other.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural street-level view. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit in the balconies and the full width of the street, and try telephoto (2x or more) to compress the street and pick out signs and ironwork.

Where to stand: Stand at the corner of Bourbon Street and Iberville Street. A quieter, emptier view of the street, useful when the crowds are gone. From here you get a quieter, emptier view of the street, which works best when the crowds are gone. Shoot along the street so the balconies and shutters line up on both sides, with the subject lying to the north-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the phone level so the building edges stay straight. Use the balconies on each side as a frame leading down the street. If you want the classic neon-and-balconies view, take the long view down the strip from further along. A second option is Orleans Street and Bourbon Street, where the narrow street frames the back of St. Louis Cathedral.

Settings tip: Tap on the part of the street you want sharp, then slide the exposure down a little so bright signs and neon do not wash out. After dark, use night mode and rest your elbows against your body to keep the phone steady. A Live Photo can catch movement in the crowd.

Best time: Early morning, before the street gets busy, when it is nearly empty and the shutters are down. For neon and atmosphere, go after dark, but expect crowds.

3The National WWII Museum

The National WWII Museum is a museum made up of several pavilions in the Central Business District, on Andrew Higgins Drive between Camp Street and Magazine Street. It began in 2000 as the National D-Day Museum and was named America's official National WWII Museum by Congress in 2003. The story starts with the Higgins boat, the landing craft designed and built in New Orleans, which is why the museum is here. Over the years new pavilions have been added, the latest being the Liberation Pavilion, which opened in 2023.

It photographs well because of the scale and the variety. The original Louisiana Memorial Pavilion has a tall atrium with a Supermarine Spitfire and a Douglas C-47 Skytrain suspended from the ceiling. A Higgins boat is usually on display below. The third floor has an observation deck for a closer look at the hanging aircraft. The US Freedom Pavilion: The Boeing Center is the largest building and holds bombers and fighters, including the B-17E My Gal Sal.

For phone photos, look up and look for layers: aircraft overhead, with visitors and exhibits below to give a sense of size. Outside, step back far enough to fit a whole facade in the frame. Ask a member of staff about photography rules inside, and check the official site for access details before you go.

Where it is: In the Central Business District of New Orleans, on Andrew Higgins Drive between Camp Street and Magazine Street.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the atrium and the aircraft hanging overhead. Use the default lens (1x) for the building fronts and for people-sized details such as the Higgins boat. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a single aircraft or marking without walking closer.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Photograph the buildings and open spaces nearby, and step back from any one building until its full height fits in the frame.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the vertical edges of walls and windows to keep them straight. Indoors, tilt the phone up slightly and shoot the hanging aircraft from the atrium floor, then try again from the third-floor observation deck for a closer, level view. Keep a person or an exhibit in the foreground for scale.

Settings tip: Tap on the aircraft to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the lit areas don't wash out. In dim galleries, use night mode and hold the phone against your body or a rail to keep it steady. Use panorama only for the outdoor facades, as it distorts people and moving objects.

Best time: Inside, the light is much the same all day, so choose a quieter moment to get clear shots. For the outside of the buildings, morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light.

8Preservation Hall

Preservation Hall is a jazz venue on St Peter Street in the French Quarter, and the street itself is a big part of the picture. The building began in the 1950s as an art gallery run by Larry Borenstein, who invited local jazz musicians to play for tips. The music soon drew more attention than the paintings. In 1961 it was renamed Preservation Hall, and it now welcomes roughly 180,000 visitors a year. That history helps explain why people often gather outside.

It sits on the St Peter Street corner at the hall's block and looks along the 700 block. You get the hall's building in the frame beside the French Quarter balconies and neighbouring buildings. It is also a quieter spot than standing in front of the door. If you want the close-up, walk a minute or so to the hall itself for the weathered street front and its hanging sign.

Keep your phone outside the door. Interior photography is not allowed during performances, and flash is prohibited, so plan on exterior shots only. Expect queues and a crowd on the pavement before showtimes. Be patient and wait for a gap, or shoot from the corner so the queue becomes part of the street scene. Check the official site before you go for current details.

Where it is: Preservation Hall is on St Peter Street in the French Quarter, the old heart of New Orleans.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the street-corner view. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the balconies are being cut off, and the telephoto (2x) for the hanging sign.

Where to stand: Stand at the corner of Saint Peter Street and Royal Street (corner at the Preservation Hall block, shooting along Saint Peter). A street-corner view of the 700 block of Saint Peter with the Preservation Hall building in the frame beside the French Quarter balconies and buildings. Stand on St. Peter Street, at the corner by the Preservation Hall block, and look north-west along the street toward the hall. The building sits in the frame beside the French Quarter balconies and neighbouring buildings.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the verticals of the buildings straight. Let the balconies lead the eye toward the hall. Tilting the phone up too far makes the buildings lean, so hold it level and step back instead. For a different view, go to the hall's front and photograph the weathered facade and sign, with any crowd or musicians outside.

Settings tip: Tap the front of the hall to focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky or lit windows are washing out. Skip flash. It is prohibited inside, and it does little for a street scene anyway.

Best time: Before or after shows is a sensible general suggestion, as the street may be calmer than during the pre-show queue. Dusk can give warm lights along the balconies; use night mode if it gets dark, and hold the phone steady against a wall or post.
