The 3 free photo spots

1Promenade des Anglais

The Promenade des Anglais is the long seafront walkway of Nice, running along the Mediterranean from the airport in the west to the Quai des États-Unis in the east, about 7 kilometres in all. It began in the 1820s as a path built with money from the Reverend Lewis Way and members of the English church in Nice, and it takes its name from those winter visitors. Locals simply call it 'La Prom'. It also finished the 2024 Tour de France.

The Promenade is far too long for one photo, so the trick is to pick a spot that shows its sweep. The best-suited place for a phone is Jardin Albert Ier, at the blue chairs between the garden and the Musée Masséna. The view is flat and open along the bay. A blue chair in the foreground and palm trees along the coastline give the picture depth, so it reads as a place rather than just a long road. It is also a quieter option than the busier Castle Hill and Quai Rauba Capeu viewpoints.

Take a few frames: one with a chair close to the lens and the bay behind, one wider with the palms, and one with the Promenade curving away. If you have time, Colline du Château gives a high view of the whole Baie des Anges, and Quai Rauba Capeu shows the curve with the pebble beach in front.

Where it is: The Promenade runs along the whole seafront of Nice, from the airport to the Quai des États-Unis.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the blue chair and palm trees close to the lens, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view of the bay. If your phone has telephoto (2x-5x), use it to pull the curve of the coast closer.

Where to stand: Stand in Jardin Albert Ier (the blue chairs between the garden and the Musée Masséna, facing the sea). A flat, unobstructed view along the Promenade and bay with a blue chair in the foreground and palm trees framing the coastline. From here you get a flat, open view along the Promenade and the bay, with a blue chair in front and palm trees framing the coastline. The Promenade itself lies to the west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the horizon on a line so the sea sits level. Crouch slightly and place a blue chair low in the foreground, with the palms to one side and the bay filling the rest. For a different view, Colline du Château shows the whole curve of the Baie des Anges from above, and Quai Rauba Capeu shows the Promenade curving away with the pebble beach in front.

Settings tip: Tap on the sea or sky to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky keeps its colour. Try a Live Photo to catch movement along the shore, or panorama for the sweep of the bay, moving slowly and keeping the phone level.

Best time: Early morning or around sunset. Both give softer light and the area is good for sunrise.

2Musée Matisse

The Musée Matisse is the city's museum devoted to Henri Matisse, and it sits in the Villa des Arènes, a villa built between 1670 and 1685 in the Cimiez district. From outside, the building is the draw: an old villa with a newer wing beside it, which makes a pleasing mix of old and new in one frame. The museum opened in 1963 and reopened in 1993 after four years of renovation, when the modern wing was added. Matisse lived and worked in Nice from 1917 to 1954, and the collection here is one of the largest of his work anywhere. It includes paintings, gouaches, drawings, prints, sculptures and many personal objects that belonged to him.

For a phone photo, the villa's façade is the main subject. Take a step or two back as the surroundings allow so the whole front fits, then check the edges of the screen so the walls are not cut off. The building is fairly large, so you may find it easier to shoot it in a few frames: one wide shot, then a closer one of a doorway, window or textured wall. Switch on gridlines so the vertical lines of the villa stay straight. If you go inside, check the official site for photography rules before you go.

Where it is: The museum is in the Cimiez neighbourhood of Nice. It is housed in the Villa des Arènes, close to the city's ancient site.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the villa's front. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are very close and cannot fit the building in; keep the villa centred so the edges do not stretch.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. If the whole villa will not fit, step back as the surroundings allow.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the villa's vertical edges up with the grid so the walls do not lean. Try one wide shot of the whole building, then a closer frame of the old villa next to the newer wing to show the contrast. For detail shots, the telephoto (2x or more) lets you pick out windows and stonework without walking closer.

Settings tip: Tap on the wall to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the villa is not too dark. Keep the phone steady with both hands.

Best time: Soft morning or late-afternoon light usually flatters a building front and avoids harsh shadows from midday sun.

3Musée Marc Chagall

The Musée Marc Chagall is a French national museum devoted to the painter's work, especially the pieces inspired by religion and spirituality. It opened in 1973 while Chagall was still alive, and he had a hand in how it was arranged. The museum was built around seventeen paintings illustrating the biblical message (Genesis, Exodus and the Song of Songs), which he gave to the French State in 1966. Chagall also made the mosaic that overlooks the pond and the blue stained glass windows in the concert hall, and he gave detailed instructions for the garden, which was created by Henri Fish.

For a phone photographer, the appeal is the outdoor setting: calm water, a garden and a mosaic made by the artist himself. A simple, uncluttered frame of the building and grounds works well, and you can then move in for a close shot of the mosaic and its colours. Take the wide view first, then look for details such as texture, colour and reflections in the pond. Rules on photography inside a museum can change from room to room, so treat the exterior and garden as your main subject and check the official site before you go for what is allowed indoors.

Where it is: The museum is in Nice, in the Alpes-Maritimes, in its own grounds with a garden and pond.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the building and garden, switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want to fit the pond and mosaic into one frame, and use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details of the mosaic.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Then walk slowly around the garden, choosing spots that show the pond and the mosaic above it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the edges of the building with them so the walls stay straight. Include a little water in the foreground and let the mosaic be the colourful focus of the frame. With ultra-wide, keep the phone level and avoid tilting it up, or the walls will lean inwards. Then take a tighter 2x shot of the mosaic alone.

Settings tip: Tap the mosaic to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright so the colours stay rich and the whites don't burn out. If the water is calm, try a Live Photo to catch any ripples.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light on the colours and less glare on the water. Midday sun can be harsh on a bright facade.
