The 3 free photo spots

1Piazza Maggiore

Piazza Maggiore has been the central meeting place of Bologna since medieval times, and it photographs well because the whole square is ringed by large historic buildings. Palazzo d'Accursio (city hall and museum) stands on the west side, with the Palazzo dei Notai to the south-west, the Basilica of San Petronio to the south-east, the Palazzo dei Banchi to the east and the Palazzo del Podestà to the north. San Petronio is the city's Duomo, and its unfinished facade is one of the easiest things to recognise in a frame. The best-regarded view looks down from the top of the clock tower, the Torre dell'Orologio, inside Palazzo d'Accursio. From there you see the square and its surrounding buildings, with a sea of red rooftops and the towers of the city beyond. The clock tower is nearby, in Palazzo d'Accursio on the west side. If you only have time for ground level, start for a wide shot of the full frontage, then go to the north-west corner. It opens into Piazza del Nettuno, where Giambologna's bronze Neptune fountain makes a good close-up with the neighbouring buildings behind it. Entry to the tower is ticketed and access can change, so check the official site before you go. Keep the lens clean and hold the phone steady against your body or a solid ledge.

Where it is: In the historic centre of Bologna, Emilia-Romagna. The square sits beside Palazzo d'Accursio, the Basilica of San Petronio and Piazza del Nettuno, which opens off its north-west corner.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Whether you shoot from where you stand or from the top of the tower, start with the default lens (1x) for the square and the buildings around it. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the whole square in one frame, and use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out San Petronio or the distant towers.

Where to stand: Stand at Torre dell'Orologio (top of the clock tower, inside Palazzo d'Accursio on the Piazza Maggiore side). A bird's-eye view down onto the square and its surrounding buildings, with the sea of red rooftops and the towers beyond. The square lies to the east of you, ringed by San Petronio and the palaces. For a bird's-eye view, the top of the Torre dell'Orologio, the clock tower inside Palazzo d'Accursio on the west side, is a separate, ticketed alternative. From there the photo looks down onto the square and the buildings around it, with the red rooftops and towers stretching away beyond.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the horizon level. If you go up the tower, place the square in the lower half, with the rooftops and towers filling the upper half.

Settings tip: Tap on the square to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky does not wash out and the rooftops keep their colour. Try a Live Photo for a bit of movement from the people crossing the square.

Best time: Late afternoon is likely to suit the rooftops best. Check the tower's opening times before you plan the visit.

2Two Towers

The Two Towers, the Asinelli and the Garisenda, are the symbol of Bologna, and Via Rizzoli gives the classic head-on view of them. From the portico you look east along the straight street, with both towers rising at the far end. The Garisenda's lean shows clearly against the sky next to its taller neighbour. This is the angle on most postcards. The long line of arches also gives you a natural frame.

The towers are traditionally credited to two local families and dated to between 1109 and 1119, though few documents survive to confirm it. The Asinelli now stands about 97 metres tall. The Garisenda is only about 48 metres, but it leans by around 3.2 metres. It was once much taller and was shortened in the 14th century when the ground beneath it gave way. Dante mentions it in his writing.

The Garisenda has been sealed off since October 2023 while work to stabilise it goes on, so you can only photograph it from outside. Barriers and fences may appear in your frame near the base, so it helps to shoot from further back along the street. Leave yourself a little time to try both portrait and landscape shots. For something different, Piazza di Porta Ravegnana at the foot of the towers lets you shoot upward, and Strada Maggiore shows the Asinelli between old façades.

Where it is: In the historic centre of Bologna, at the eastern end of Via Rizzoli, where the street meets Piazza di Porta Ravegnana and the towers stand.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) held upright, so you can fit the Asinelli's full height and the Garisenda's lean in one shot. The default lens (1x) suits a tighter, cleaner view of the pair. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details, such as the top of the Asinelli or the tilt of the Garisenda.

Where to stand: Stand on Via Rizzoli (from the portico, looking east toward the towers at the end of the street). Both towers rising at the far end of the straight porticoed street, with the Garisenda's lean visible against the sky. Under the portico, and face east toward the towers at the end of the street. From here you see both towers rising at the far end of the straight porticoed street, with the Garisenda's lean visible against the sky. For a low upward shot, walk on to Piazza di Porta Ravegnana. Strada Maggiore gives a quieter view of the Asinelli between old façades.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the street's centre line on the middle vertical line. Keep the phone level so the façades do not tilt inwards. Let the porticoes frame the left or right edge. The Garisenda's lean is the point of the picture, so keep it clear of fences and lampposts. If barriers get in the way, move to the other side of the street. Take a second shot from Piazza di Porta Ravegnana, pointing the phone upward.

Settings tip: Tap the sky near the towers to set focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the sky keeps its colour and the brickwork stays visible. Take a few frames, because passers-by often walk through the foreground.

Best time: Early morning or evening, when the light is soft. In the evening, if the light fades, try night mode with the phone braced against a pillar of the portico.

3Basilica of San Petronio

The Basilica of San Petronio fills the south side of Piazza Maggiore, and its red-brick and marble front is one of the most recognisable sights in Bologna. It is dedicated to the city's patron saint, Saint Petronius, who was Bishop of Bologna in the fifth century. Work began in 1390. The main façade has never been finished, so its red brick and marble make a distinctive two-tone picture. The church is huge: about 132 metres long, 66 metres wide and 47 metres tall. The main doorway, the Porta Magna, carries carved scenes and figures by Jacopo della Quercia. That wide view is the classic postcard shot. Stay outside for the façade; there is plenty to see without going in. Early in the day the light is softer and the square is quieter, so you can get a clean frame without crowds in front of the doors. If you have time, walk round to Piazza del Nettuno for a second picture, with the bronze Neptune in the foreground and the basilica rising behind him.

Where it is: In the historic centre of Bologna, on the south side of Piazza Maggiore, the city's main square, with the surrounding palaces on the other sides.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole façade across the square. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the palaces and open space around it. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the Porta Magna carvings and the brick-and-marble join.

Where to stand: Stand in Piazza Maggiore (stand on the square facing the basilica, which sits on the south side of the piazza). The unfinished red-brick and marble façade filling the frame across the open square, with the surrounding palaces around it. Which sits on the south side of the piazza. From here the unfinished red-brick and marble façade fills the frame across the open square, with the palaces around it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the edges of the façade with the vertical lines so the building doesn't lean. Keep the phone level and leave a little space above the roof. Include some open square at the bottom to give a sense of scale. For a second option, Piazza del Nettuno gives you Giambologna's bronze Neptune in the foreground with the basilica behind.

Settings tip: Tap the façade to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a touch so the pale marble keeps its detail. Try panorama for the whole sweep of the square and palaces, holding the phone steady and level.

Best time: Before about 9 a.m. for an emptier square and softer light. Sunrise or sunset gives warm light on the façade.
