The 3 free photo spots

1La Bombonera

La Bombonera is Boca Juniors' stadium in the La Boca neighbourhood, and its name means 'the chocolate box'. The nickname stuck after a third tier was completed in 1953, giving the stands their boxy, steep shape. The stadium opened on 25 May 1940. In 1996 a fourth tier was added, bringing capacity to 57,500. Outside, murals show everyday life in La Boca, which makes the walls as interesting to photograph as the structure.

This spot is the corner of Brandsen y Del Valle Iberlucea, on the stadium's west side. Boca's earlier ground stood on this same corner from 1924, before the present stadium replaced it. The corner gives a wider, more sideways view than the classic front-on shot. You see the curved stands and the flank of the ground, and one stand can look cut off at the edge. Treat it as an alternative angle rather than the postcard view. It works best if you like the stadium's odd, layered shape and want a different picture from the usual entrance shot. If you want the main street-side facade and entrance, with other visitors photographing from the pavement, walk round to the front of the stadium.

The stadium is large, so step back as far as is safe to fit it in. Look for the curve of the stands against the sky and keep the vertical lines straight. A planned expansion on the Del Valle Iberlucea side could change this view in future.

Where it is: In the La Boca neighbourhood of Buenos Aires, on the streets directly around the Boca Juniors stadium. The spot is the corner of Brandsen and Del Valle Iberlucea, at the stadium's west side.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the curved stands in from close range. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, less stretched picture. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out details such as the stand edges or a mural.

Where to stand: Stand at the corner of Brandsen and Del Valle Iberlucea (corner at the stadium's west side). A wider view of the stadium's curved stands and the side of the ground, with one stand appearing cut off at the corner. Stand on Brandsen y Del Valle Iberlucea, at the corner by the stadium's west side. The stadium's centre lies to the north-west of you. From here you see the side of the ground and its curved stands, with one stand appearing cut off at the corner.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a stand edge up with a vertical grid line so the building does not lean. Let the curve of the stands lead across the frame. Keep a bit of street in the foreground for scale. If you want the main facade and entrance with visitors in front, walk round to the street side instead and use the default lens (1x).

Settings tip: Tap on the stands to set focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stand details don't wash out. Hold the phone level to keep the lines straight.

Best time: Daytime on a day with no match, when the streets are quieter and easier to stand in.

2Teatro Colón

The Teatro Colón is Buenos Aires' grand opera house, and from Plaza Lavalle you see the front facade and dome framed across the square. The theatre took about twenty years to build and opened on 25 May 1908 with Verdi's Aida. The sculpted busts and bas-reliefs on the facade are by Luigi Trinchero, and they reward a closer look with your zoom. The building was declared a National Historic Monument in 1989. It then closed for a long refurbishment from 2006 and reopened in May 2010. It has a reputation as one of the world's great opera houses, partly because of its acoustics, though you will not see that from outside. From there the theatre lies to the south-east, and the open square lets you fit the whole width of the building into one frame. One guide (Buenos Tours) treats the Avenida 9 de Julio side as the back and the Libertad/Plaza Lavalle side as the front, so check on the day. If you have time for one more angle, one guide suggests the corner of Cerrito and Tucumán for the full height of the facade and dome. Keep your phone level so the vertical lines stay straight, and wait for a gap in the people crossing the square.

Where it is: The Teatro Colón is in central Buenos Aires, on Libertad street facing Plaza Lavalle.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the facade and dome across the square. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if people or trees block the full width. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the statues, busts and carved details.

Where to stand: Stand in Plaza Lavalle (front (Libertad side) of the theatre). The theatre's front facade and dome, framed from across the square. From here the front facade and dome are framed across the square. Try not to stand too close to the building, or you will have to tilt the phone upwards and the walls will lean inwards.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the vertical edges of the building up with them. Keep the dome near the top of the frame with a little sky above it. Use a tree or the edge of the square as a natural frame if one is handy. For a different view, one guide suggests the corner of Cerrito and Tucumán for the full height of the facade, and the Avenida 9 de Julio side offers another perspective on the building.

Settings tip: Tap the facade to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little if the sky is bright, so the stonework keeps its detail. Take a few shots in a row, as people often walk through the frame.

Best time: Daylight, when the facade is evenly lit, is the easiest time to photograph it. Early morning is usually quieter on the square. After dark, try night mode and hold the phone steady against something solid.

3Recoleta Cemetery

Recoleta Cemetery is a small city of tombs in the Recoleta neighbourhood. It began in 1822 as the first public cemetery in Buenos Aires, laid out in the old garden of a convent whose church, Our Lady of the Pillar, dates from 1732. Today it covers about 5.5 hectares and holds 4691 vaults, all above ground. Ninety-four of them are protected National Historical Monuments. The layout works like a set of city blocks, with wide tree-lined walkways branching into narrower lanes packed with marble mausoleums in Art Deco, Art Nouveau, Baroque and Neo-Gothic styles.

It photographs well because the first view is already a composition. You enter through neoclassical gates with tall Doric columns, and the ironwork and stone frame the lanes and tombs beyond. Once inside, the repeating rooflines and statues give you plenty of close-up subjects. The Gothic tomb of Liliana Crociati de Szaszak is of special interest because of its unusual design. A small colony of stray cats lives here too, about a dozen as of 2024.

Start at the entrance, then work inwards. Take a wide frame first, then move on to details such as statues, doors and carved stone. Be quiet and discreet around anyone visiting a grave, and don't climb on or touch the monuments.

Where it is: Recoleta Cemetery is in the Recoleta neighbourhood of Buenos Aires. It is built around the old convent and the church of Our Lady of the Pillar.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to frame the whole entrance portico with the lanes beyond. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are very close to the columns and want to fit them all in. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for statues, carvings and rooftops without leaning over anything.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (main entrance portico, Junín side). The neoclassical Doric-columned entrance framing the cemetery's tree-lined lanes and mausoleums beyond. From here, look along the tree-lined lanes towards the mausoleums. As a separate photo stop, the main entrance portico on the Junín side shows the neoclassical Doric-columned entrance framing the lanes beyond.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a column or the gate with a vertical line so the portico stands straight. Let the ironwork and columns form a frame around the lane beyond. For a different subject, the Gothic tomb of Liliana Crociati, with its statue of a woman in a wedding dress beside her dog, suits close-up shots. The Claustros del Pilar are said to give an overhead view across the tomb tops, but check whether they are open to visitors before you go.

Settings tip: Tap the gate or a column to focus. Then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky looks washed out. Keep flash off, especially near chapels. Portrait mode can work for statues if you stay at a respectful distance.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when the light gives good shadows on the gate.
