This map shows 10 of the most-searched sights in Vatican City as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: St Peter's Basilica, St Peter's Square, Vatican Museums, Pietà and St Peter's Dome lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1St Peter's Basilica

St Peter's Basilica is the great Renaissance church of Vatican City, and its central dome dominates the Rome skyline. The building is approached across St Peter's Square, which has two sections, an oval and then a trapezoid, both wrapped in tall colonnades. The wide façade, with its giant columns, stretches across the far end. Two statues of Saints Peter and Paul, each 5.55 metres tall, stand on the steps in front.

That is why it photographs so well. The colonnades lead the eye towards the façade, and the dome rises behind it as a clear finishing shape. Building work began on 18 April 1506 and finished on 18 November 1626, and several famous architects shaped the design, including Bramante, Michelangelo and Carlo Maderno. Bernini designed the square and many of the fittings. It is also the largest church in the world by interior measure.

The phone icon on the map is about 290 m east of the subject. At that distance the whole building fits in the frame with room for the open space and people in front of it. Take one wide shot to show the scale, then zoom in for the dome and the statues. Keep your phone level so the tall columns do not lean. If the square is busy, wait a moment for a gap, or use people walking across it to show how big the place is.

Where it is: St Peter's Basilica stands in Vatican City, at the western end of St Peter's Square. It lies west of the River Tiber, near the Janiculum Hill.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole façade and dome together. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the colonnades and the open square in the frame, and to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the dome and the statues on the steps.

Where to stand: Stand about 290 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the centre of the façade and dome with the middle vertical line. Use the horizontal lines to keep the rooftop level and the columns upright. Leave some open ground and people in the lower part of the picture to give a sense of scale. Then zoom in for a tighter shot of the dome.

Settings tip: Tap on the façade to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the bright sky does not wash out the stonework. If you use telephoto, hold the phone steady with both hands and elbows tucked in.

Best time: Late afternoon usually gives warm light on the stone, but go early in the day if you want fewer people in the square.

4Michelangelo's Pietà

Michelangelo's Pietà shows Mary holding the body of Jesus after he has been taken down from the cross. It is carved from Carrara marble and dates from 1498–1499. It stands in St Peter's Basilica, in the first chapel on the north side after you come in, where it has been since the 18th century. It was made for a French cardinal's funeral chapel in the older church that stood on the site.

It photographs well because the whole group forms a calm pyramid, with Mary's head at the top and her wide, heavy drapery spreading out to the base. Mary looks very young, and the faces and folds of cloth are what draw the eye, so a close, simple frame suits it. It is also the only work Michelangelo ever signed. His name is carved on the sash across Mary's chest, though it is too small to read clearly in a phone photo from a distance.

The sculpture sits behind a bulletproof glass screen, which was replaced and updated in November 2024 ahead of the 2025 Jubilee. The screen can pick up reflections, so move a little to one side and watch for bright patches before you shoot. Take a few frames, then pick the sharpest. The basilica can be dimly lit and busy, so hold the phone steady with both hands and keep your elbows against your body.

Where it is: Inside St Peter's Basilica in Vatican City, in the first chapel on the north side just after the main entrance (about 41.9023, 12.4546).

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for the whole chapel, then try telephoto (2x-3x, depending on your phone) to fill the frame with the two figures. Because of the glass, telephoto lets you stay back and still get a close view. Ultra-wide (0.5x) is better kept for the basilica interior around it.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Stand as near the front of the chapel as is allowed, slightly off to one side rather than dead centre.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the pyramid shape of the group in the middle, with Mary's head near the top of the frame. Keep the sculpture upright and avoid cutting off the base. For a second shot, go closer with telephoto on the faces and the folds of Mary's drapery.

Settings tip: Tap on the marble (the face of Jesus or Mary) to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the white stone keeps its detail. Night mode can help in dim light, but hold very still, as it needs a steady phone. Check your photo for reflections in the glass.

Best time: Go when the basilica is quieter so you have a clear spot at the front of the chapel. Check the official site for visiting times.

5Dome of St Peter's (rooftop view)

The subject is the great dome of St Peter's Basilica, and the phone icon on the map sits right at it, so the picture here is the view looking out from the dome rather than a shot of it from the square. The dome is one of the largest in the world and dominates the Roman skyline, so from up here you look out over St Peter's Square, the rooftops of Rome and the city beyond. The square is a forecourt in two parts, an oval and a trapezoid, both ringed by tall colonnades. From above, that shape reads clearly and makes a strong, graphic foreground for a phone photo. The basilica was designed principally by Bramante, Michelangelo and Maderno, with the square and fittings by Bernini. Building began on 18 April 1506 and finished on 18 November 1626. That is more than a century of work behind the stonework around you. For phone photos, keep the horizon level and use the gridlines to line up the colonnades and rooflines. Tap on the distant skyline to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky looks washed out. If you are shooting through railings or a window, hold the lens close to the barrier and keep your hand steady. Take a few frames at different zoom levels, because the same view can look very different at 0.5x and at 3x. Expect to share the space with other visitors.

Where it is: In Vatican City, at the middle of St Peter's Basilica, under its central dome, which stands at the end of St Peter's Square on the west side of the River Tiber. The basilica is close to the Janiculum Hill and Hadrian's Mausoleum.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the square, the colonnades and the sweep of Rome's rooftops. Use the default lens (1x) for a natural view. Use the telephoto/zoom (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out distant domes and towers.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the horizon on the top or bottom third line. Use the curve of the colonnades as a leading shape and keep the lines straight. Take one wide shot, then a tighter zoomed shot of a single landmark. Take a few frames, since people move through the view.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the sky or the distant rooftops to focus, then lower the exposure slider slightly to keep the sky from burning out. If the view is hazy, a Live Photo can give you a choice of frames. Wipe your lens before you start.

Best time: Earlier or later in the day usually gives softer light and gentler shadows over the rooftops than midday sun. Check the official site for visiting times before you plan.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Vatican City?

The first three on this map are St Peter's Basilica, St Peter's Square and Vatican Museums. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 10.

Where should I stand to photograph St Peter's Basilica?

Stand about 290 m east of St Peter's Basilica, at the phone icon on the map, and face west. Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole façade and dome together.

When is the best time to photograph Pietà?

Go when the basilica is quieter so you have a clear spot at the front of the chapel. Check the official site for visiting times.

How do I get to St Peter's Dome?

The nearest stop is the tram 19 stop Risorgimento/S. Pietro, about 9 minutes on foot from the phone icon on the map, with the dome lying to the south-west.
