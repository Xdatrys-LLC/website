The 3 free photo spots

1Bryggen Hanseatic Wharf

Bryggen is the row of old wooden merchant buildings along the eastern side of the Vågen harbour in Bergen. Its name means 'the dock'. It is also called Tyskebryggen, 'the German dock'. Around 1350 a Kontor of the Hanseatic League was established here. Bergen itself is said to have been founded within this area around 1070, and the wharf has been on the UNESCO World Heritage list since 1979.

The buildings you see today are much younger than the site. Fire has swept through Bergen many times, and after a big fire in 1702 the wharf was rebuilt. Another fire in 1955 cleared part of it, and the dig that followed turned up the runic notes known as the Bryggen inscriptions. Today the buildings hold museums, shops, restaurants and pubs.

It photographs well because the narrow, tightly packed gabled fronts make a strong repeating pattern, and the harbour in front gives you open space to step back. Start with a wide view of the whole row, then move closer for details such as gable tops, doors and window shutters. Look for neighbours' roofs and lines of buildings that lead the eye along the front. Keep your phone steady against the pull of the crowd, and check people's heads aren't cutting into your frame.

Where it is: Bryggen runs along the eastern side of the Vågen harbour in Bergen, Norway.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the row of fronts as the eye sees them. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you cannot step back far enough to fit the whole line in. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for gables, signs and window details.

Where to stand: Stand about 30 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. From there the long line of gabled buildings sits in front of you with the harbour beside it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the vertical edges of the buildings up with them so they do not lean. Put the row on a diagonal through the frame to add depth, with a bit of harbour or sky for breathing space. Then take a second shot with the telephoto and fill the frame with just three or four gables. Keep your phone level; if you tilt it upward the buildings will look as if they are falling backwards.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest building front to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is washing out. Try a Live Photo if boats or people are moving, then pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Late afternoon to evening usually gives warmer light on the fronts. Early morning is typically quieter, if you want fewer people in the picture.

2Fløibanen Funicular

Fløibanen is the funicular that carries visitors from the centre of Bergen up the mountain of Fløyen. It is one of Norway's most visited attractions and carries nearly two million passengers a year. The line is 848 m long and climbs 302 m. It is a single track with a passing loop in the middle, so the two cars meet halfway. They are named Blåmann (blue) and Rødhette (red), and together with the white lower station building they echo the colours of the Norwegian flag. The line opened on 15 January 1918.

For a photo, the railway itself is the best subject. Ride to the Fjellveien stop and walk a little back uphill. The frame works because of the two bold colours against the hillside, and because the cars keep coming. Daytime departures run roughly every quarter of an hour, so you can wait for the next pair if you miss one.

If you would rather photograph the white lower station building, Fisketorget gives a view of it from the harbour side. Both spots are only thinly documented, so check the position on the map before you rely on it. Keep your distance from the track and stay on the public path.

Where it is: Fløibanen starts in the centre of Bergen and climbs the hillside towards the mountain of Fløyen.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to show the cars and the track together. If you can't get far enough back, try the ultra-wide (0.5x) for a wider scene. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to fill the frame with the two cars as they pass.

Where to stand: Stand on Fjellveien (Fjellveien stop on the Fløibanen line, then a short walk uphill). Two funicular cars passing each other on the track, so the railway itself is the subject of the frame. This is where the two funicular cars pass each other on the track, so the railway itself is the subject of your photo. Check the exact spot on the map before you set off.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the track along one of the diagonal lines so the climb is clear. Wait until the blue and red cars are level with each other, then take the photo. For a different view, Fisketorget shows the white lower station building from the harbour side.

Settings tip: Tap on the cars to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright, so the red and blue stay rich. Switch on Live Photos or motion to catch the moment the cars pass, then choose the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Daytime, when the cars run more often and you won't wait long for them to pass. Check the Fløibanen timetable before you go.

3Mount Fløyen Viewpoint

Mount Fløyen (Fløyfjellet) is one of the seven mountains around the centre of Bergen, and its viewpoint offers a popular view over the Bergen Peninsula. From up here the harbour, the old town roofs, the islands and the water beyond all fit into one wide scene, which is exactly what a phone camera likes. The viewpoint sits at about 320 m above sea level, close to the top of the mountain, which reaches 400 m. Most visitors arrive on the Fløibanen funicular, which climbs from Vetrlidsallmenningen in the city centre in roughly eight minutes and opened in 1918. At the top, the viewpoint was rebuilt and extended in 2002 and is known as Fløytrappene, a broad staircase running from the funicular station towards the Fløien Folkerestaurant. The steps give you different heights to shoot from, so try a few before settling. Start with a wide frame of the whole city, then move in on a detail such as the harbour, a ship or a cluster of rooftops. Keep your phone level so the horizon and the water stay straight, and watch for people leaning on the railings, who can add a sense of scale. If you have time, the signed paths along the mountain open up other angles on the same view.

Where it is: Mount Fløyen rises directly above the centre of Bergen, in Bergen Municipality, Vestland county. The viewpoint is near the top station of the Fløibanen funicular, looking out over the city and the peninsula.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the main view, and switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) when you want to include more of the city and the sky. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out details such as boats or rooftops.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. The subject is the mountain's viewpoint right where you are standing, so you are looking out over the city from the spot the map marks.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower third line, depending on whether the sky or the city is more interesting. Use the railings or steps as a foreground line leading into the view. Take one wide shot and one closer telephoto shot of a detail.

Settings tip: Tap on the city to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright so the clouds keep their detail. Try panorama mode for a sweeping view, moving slowly and keeping the phone level.

Best time: Clear, dry days give the longest view. Late afternoon or early evening often gives softer light, and after dark night mode can capture the city lights, but hold the phone very steady.
