The 3 free photo spots

1Statue of Liberty

The Statue of Liberty is a huge copper-clad figure of a robed, crowned woman holding a torch high in her right hand. In her left she carries a tablet inscribed with the date of the Declaration of Independence, 4 July 1776, in Roman numerals. She was a gift from the people of France, designed by the sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, with an inner metal framework built by Gustave Eiffel. She was dedicated on 28 October 1886 and stands on Liberty Island, once called Bedloe's Island, in New York Harbour. Since 1933 she has been looked after by the National Park Service.

The free land view most guides recommend is from The Battery, at the southern tip of Manhattan. From the waterfront path you look across open water to the statue, with the park's greenery framing the scene. The railings and benches give you several angles without leaving the park. The statue is a long way off, so she will look small at the default lens (1x). Use that setting for a wide, scenic shot with the trees and water, then zoom in for a closer portrait of her. Come with realistic expectations: this is a view across the harbour, not a close-up. If you want to see her from behind, with the torch raised over the water and the Lower Manhattan skyline beside her, Liberty State Park is the usual alternative.

Where it is: The statue stands on Liberty Island in New York Harbour, about 3 km south-west of The Battery. The Battery, at the very southern end of Manhattan, is the classic free land view of her, according to guides.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to show the harbour, the greenery and the statue together. Then switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a closer view of her, because she is a long way across the water. Keep the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the park and waterfront around you, not for the statue itself.

Where to stand: Stand in The Battery (southern tip of Manhattan, waterfront path; the spot marked 'Statue of Liberty Viewpoint' on the park map). Lady Liberty across the harbor with the water and park greenery framing her in the distance. Along the waterfront path.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the statue on one of the grid intersections, with water filling the lower part of the frame. Use the waterfront railings or a bench as a foreground edge. If you want a different view, take the Staten Island Ferry, which boards at Whitehall Terminal, for moving-deck shots of the statue passing by, framed with the harbour and skyline. A telephoto helps there.

Settings tip: Tap on the statue to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright, so she keeps her detail. Hold the phone against the railing to keep zoomed shots steady. Take a few Live Photos, as boats and water often move through the frame.

Best time: One guide suggests fall and winter sunsets, when the sun can line up behind the statue. At other times of year, check where the light falls on the day, and look out for clear weather so she stays sharp at a distance.

2Central Park

Central Park is the first landscaped park in the United States, laid out by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, whose 'Greensward Plan' won the design competition in 1858. The first areas opened that same year and the park was finished in 1876. It covers 843 acres, about 2.5 miles from north to south and 0.5 miles across. The lakes, lawns and woods look natural, but almost all of it was built by hand. That is why it is so easy to photograph: water, trees and stone bridges come in ready-made frames. Because the park is so big, the best phone photo is one classic view, not the whole park. Stand on the shoreline just east of the bridge and you can fit the stone bridge, the Pond and the trees in the foreground, with Midtown towers and the Plaza Hotel behind. Guides rate it among the park's best skyline frames. If you have time, Bow Bridge over the Lake is a second classic, and Turtle Pond, with Belvedere Castle above it, is the closest recommended view to the middle of the park. Exact spots can vary, so check a current park map on the day.

Where it is: Central Park is in Manhattan, between the Upper West Side and the Upper East Side, with Harlem to the north and Midtown to the south. Gapstow Bridge is at the Pond, inside the park near the Fifth Avenue entrance.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the bridge, the Pond and the skyline in one frame. Try the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull the Midtown towers closer behind the bridge. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of foreground water and trees.

Where to stand: Stand on Gapstow Bridge (shoreline east of the bridge at the Pond's edge). The stone bridge, the Pond and trees in the foreground, with Midtown towers and the Plaza Hotel behind. On the shoreline east of the bridge at the Pond's edge. From there the stone bridge and the trees are in front of you, and the Midtown towers and the Plaza Hotel stand behind them. The middle of the park lies off to the north-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the bridge on one of the lower thirds, with the Pond filling the bottom of the frame and the towers along the top. Keep the phone level so the buildings don't lean. Branches at the edge of the picture can frame the scene. For a second view, Bow Bridge over the Lake is a classic alternative.

Settings tip: Tap the water or the bridge to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky doesn't wash out. Try a Live Photo for a moment when a duck or a bird passes through the frame.

Best time: Early morning, when guides say it is less crowded.

3Grand Central Terminal

Grand Central Terminal is the big Beaux-Arts railway station at 42nd Street and Park Avenue in Midtown Manhattan. It opened in 1913 on the site of two earlier stations, and it is still a working commuter terminal. It has 44 platforms, more than any other railway station in the world, though they are all underground. About 21.6 million people visited in 2018, not counting train and subway passengers. That says a lot about how popular it is with visitors.

It photographs well because of the Main Concourse. This huge hall covers roughly 35,000 square feet and is nearly always full of people crossing it. At its centre is the information booth, topped with a four-sided brass clock. The clock is the easiest single subject to pick out, and the departure boards at the south end of the hall make a good busy backdrop.

For a phone, think in three shots. First, a wide view of the hall with the crowd moving through it. Second, a tighter shot of the clock and booth. Third, a detail such as a board, a sign or a figure crossing the floor. Keep your phone straight, because the architecture looks best with upright walls and symmetrical lines. Move around the concourse a little between shots, since a few steps left or right changes the background a lot. Be ready to step aside, as this is a commuter route and people will be walking through constantly.

Where it is: Grand Central Terminal is in Midtown Manhattan, New York City, at the corner of 42nd Street and Park Avenue. Its 42nd Street facade carries the full name of the terminal.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide lens (0.5x) to fit in the concourse and its crowds. Switch to the default lens (1x) for the information booth and clock. Use the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) to pick out details such as the clock face or the departure boards.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Walk into the Main Concourse and pick a spot with a clear view of the central information booth and its clock, and keep a little distance from the busiest walking lines.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the walls and windows so they stay upright. Place the clock in the centre for a balanced shot, or off to one side with the crowd filling the rest of the frame. Try one wide shot and one close shot of the same view.

Settings tip: Tap on the brightest part of the scene to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so windows and lamps do not wash out. If you want people to look blurred and moving, try Live Photos and pick a long-exposure effect afterwards. Use night mode only if the light is low, and hold the phone steady against your body.

Best time: The concourse is usually busy, and trains arrive very frequently at morning rush hour. If you want fewer people in the frame, try a quieter time outside the commuter peaks. If you want lively movement, go when it is busy.
