This map shows 15 of the most-searched sights in Nara as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Nara Park deer, Tōdai-ji, Kasuga-taisha, Kōfuku-ji pagoda and Mount Wakakusa lead the list. The 3 free photo spots

1Nara Park

Nara Park is a large public park at the foot of Mount Wakakusa, in the city of Nara. It opened in 1880, which makes it one of the oldest parks in Japan. The park is officially about 502 hectares. Counting the grounds of Tōdai-ji, Kōfuku-ji, Kasuga Grand Shrine and the Nara National Museum around its edges, the area grows to about 660 hectares. That is far too much to cover in one photo, so think of it as a place for several small pictures rather than one big one.

The main draw is the more than 1,200 wild sika deer that roam freely among the lawns, pines, maples and cherry trees. Local folklore treats them as messengers of a god of Kasuga Grand Shrine, and today they are protected as natural monuments. That mix of grass, old trees and animals gives you easy, friendly subjects, with a hillside backdrop on clear days.

The deer are wild animals. Signs in the park ask visitors not to tease them, especially when feeding. Keep your phone hand steady and your snacks out of reach, and give the deer a little space. Let them come into your frame rather than chasing them. Take a few shots in each area you pass, with a deer in the foreground and a tree-lined path or the hill behind.

Where it is: Nara Park lies in the city of Nara, at the foot of Mount Wakakusa. Tōdai-ji, Kōfuku-ji, Kasuga Grand Shrine and the Nara National Museum sit on its edge or within it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for most shots of deer and paths. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) for wide lawns and tall trees. If your phone has telephoto (2x-5x), use it for close-ups, so you can stay a comfortable distance from the animals.

Where to stand: Stand about 400 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. Pause wherever the trees open out onto a lawn and take your pictures from there.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place a deer on one of the crossing points, with a path or tree line behind it. Crouch a little so you shoot at the animal's eye level. For a wider scene, use 0.5x and keep the horizon level, with trees framing the sides.

Settings tip: Tap on the deer to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is bright. Live Photos can catch a head turn or an ear flick. Portrait mode works well on a deer standing a short way off, as it softens the background.

Best time: Early morning is usually quieter and the light is softer. Late afternoon can also be pleasant. On sunny middays, look for shade under the trees to avoid harsh contrast.

2Tōdai-ji

Tōdai-ji (the 'Eastern Great Temple') is a huge Buddhist temple complex in Nara, and its Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) is the main hall of the complex. The hall houses the world's largest bronze statue of the Buddha Vairocana, known in Japan as Daibutsu. The temple was founded in 738 and opened in 752. The statue itself is about 16 m high, and 48 big lacquered pillars hold up the blue-tiled roof. The hall you see today dates from a rebuilding in 1709. It was strengthened with steel framing between 1974 and 1980, because the weight of the roof had put it at risk of collapse. The whole complex is part of the UNESCO listing 'Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara'.

The scale is what makes it photograph well. The broad roof, the dark timber and the wide approach give you strong lines and plenty of depth, even on a phone. The site covers a very large area, so walk around a little and try more than one angle. Keep the whole roofline in the frame, and have people walking through the picture to show how big it is. Check the official site before you go for current access, and for any rules about photographing inside.

Where it is: Tōdai-ji lies in the city of Nara, Japan, and is one of the city's best-known temple complexes. It covers a very large area, so this view shows just one part of it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the full width of the big roof and the approach into one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural, less stretched view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for roof ends, carved details and the colour of the pillars.

Where to stand: Stand about 45 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. Take a few steps either way along your line of sight to clear other visitors from the foreground.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and keep the horizontal roof lines level with them, so the building does not look tilted. Hold the phone steady and at chest height to keep verticals straight. Leave a little space around the roof so it does not feel cramped. Include a person or two for scale. If you can, take one wide shot and one closer shot of the roof or a detail.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to focus, then lower the exposure slider slightly if the sky is bright, so the roof tiles and timber keep their colour. Try a Live Photo if people are walking through the scene, and pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Early in the day or later in the afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people in the frame. Check the official site for the opening times.

3Kasuga-taisha

Kasuga-taisha is the Shinto shrine of the Fujiwara family, founded in 768 and rebuilt many times since. In fact it has been rebuilt to the same plan every 20 years for over a thousand years, most recently in 2016. For a phone photographer, the appeal is the setting and the detail. The path to the shrine runs through a deer park where the deer roam freely and are seen as sacred messengers. It is lined with more than three thousand stone lanterns, and bronze lanterns hang inside. The buildings have sloping gabled roofs, decorative logs called katsuogi and forked roof ends called chigi, which are good subjects for close-up shots. The shrine and the nearby Kasugayama Primeval Forest are part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara", and the forest forms a green backdrop behind the buildings. Wisteria trees here bloom in late April and early May. The lanterns are lit only at the Setsubun Mantoro festival (3 February) and the Chugen Mantoro festival (14-15 August). From the phone icon on the map, the main subject is a short step to the north, so you can start shooting almost at once. Then walk on slowly, because the best pictures are usually the lantern-lined path, a deer in front of a lantern, and the red-and-white details of the halls.

Where it is: Kasuga-taisha is in Nara, next to the Kasugayama Primeval Forest. The path to the shrine passes through a deer park. The Man'yo Botanical Garden is next to it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the gates, halls and lantern-lined path as the eye sees them. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) in tight spots, such as under trees or in the cloisters. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for roof details and bronze lanterns, and to photograph deer without getting close.

Where to stand: Stand about 35 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. From here the shrine area sits ahead of you, so you can take an opening shot and then walk closer for details.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and use the path or a row of lanterns as a leading line towards the buildings. Keep the trees and lanterns at the edges to frame the shot. For detail shots, move closer or use the telephoto to fill the frame with a roof end or a hanging lantern. Keep the phone level so the vertical lines of the halls stay straight.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky or white walls look too bright under the dark trees. In portrait mode, shoot a deer with a lantern behind it. On the festival evenings, try night mode and hold the phone steady against a post or your body.

Best time: Late April to early May for the wisteria. 3 February or 14-15 August if you want the lanterns lit. Check the official site for festival times. Early morning usually gives softer light and fewer people.
