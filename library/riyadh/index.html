This map shows 9 of the most-searched sights in Riyadh as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Deera Square, Kingdom Centre, Al Faisaliah Tower, National Museum and Masmak Fortress lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Deera Square

Deera Square is a large open public square in the old heart of Riyadh. It is also called Justice Square or Safa Square, and its Arabic name is Sāḥat ad-Dayrah. It photographs well because the wide paved space gives you room to step back. The fountains sit in the foreground, and the Imam Turki bin Abdullah Grand Mosque fills the background. The square sits next to the al-Hukm Palace compound in the Qasr al-Hukm District. In the past it was the place where public executions were carried out, but it had stopped hosting them by 2019. Keep that in mind: take calm, respectful photos of the open space and the mosque, and keep the government and palace buildings out of your shots, as they should not be photographed. For a second picture, the mud-brick walls and watchtowers of Al Masmak Fort, at the edge of the square, make a good contrast between old and modern Riyadh. Early evening light, around golden hour, is the time to aim for, when the mosque and paving glow warmly. Move into the open expanse and face the mosque. Include some of the fountains and paving for depth, and wait a moment for people to move through the frame. Check opening hours before you plan a visit to the fort.

Where it is: Deera Square is in the Ad-Dirah neighbourhood of central Riyadh, in the Qasr al-Hukm District. It sits next to the Imam Turki bin Abdullah Grand Mosque and the al-Hukm Palace compound.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the paved expanse, the fountains and the mosque together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the fountains in front. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details of the mosque.

Where to stand: Stand about 240 m south-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-east. From there the photo shows the open paved expanse with its fountains, and the mosque filling the background.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the mosque on the upper third line, with the fountains and paving in the lower part of the frame. Keep the phone level so the mosque's vertical lines stay straight. For a different picture, Al Masmak Fort at the square's edge gives you old mud-brick walls and watchtowers beside the modern square. Keep the palace and government buildings out of the frame.

Settings tip: Tap the mosque to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour and the building does not wash out. Live Photos can catch the movement of the fountains.

Best time: Golden hour, when the light is warm and softer on the mosque.

2Kingdom Centre

Kingdom Centre is a 99-storey tower, about 302 m tall, finished in 2002 and still one of the most recognisable shapes on the Riyadh skyline. Its almond-shaped body is topped by a huge inverted arch, which is nicknamed the "bottle opener". A glass-sided skybridge spans the opening. The lower floors hold offices, a hotel, apartments and the Al-Mamlaka shopping mall.

This spot is the Sky Bridge itself, high up between the two upper ends of the tower. From here you get a wide view over the city, with the suspended glass walkway as part of the scene. It works best around dusk, when the sky changes colour and the city lights start to come on. Visitors say phone photos come out well, though the glass can throw reflections back at you.

The bridge is a public observation deck reached by lift after paying admission, so check the official site before you go for current prices, hours and rules. If you would rather stay at street level, the tower also looks good from King Fahd Road, where you can fit in the whole curved top and the arch. Olaya Street gives a quieter frame with the tower rising above buildings and palms.

Take a few frames at different zoom levels, and wipe your phone lens before you start, as glass and lifts leave smudges.

Where it is: Kingdom Centre stands in the al-Olaya district of central Riyadh. The complex includes the Al-Mamlaka shopping mall in the podium and the Four Seasons Hotel Riyadh in the tower.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the skyline and the glass bridge together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) for the broadest view, or a panorama along the windows. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out single towers and streets far below.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Try to stand at a window with a clear view rather than a crowded gap.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the horizon level, with the city filling the lower two-thirds and the sky above. Press the phone lens gently against the glass, or close to it, so reflections drop away. Shade it with your hand or jacket if the glass still shows reflections. For a different picture, King Fahd Road shows the full tower and arch from outside, ideally against the sunset.

Settings tip: Tap on the brightest part of the city and drag the exposure slider down a little so the sunset colours stay rich. After dark, use night mode and brace your elbows on the rail or window frame, because the exposure is longer. Live Photos can help you catch a moment when people walk through your frame.

Best time: Just before sunset, so you catch the colour change over the city and the first lights coming on.

9Imam Turki bin Abdullah Grand Mosque

The Imam Turki bin Abdullah Grand Mosque, also called the Grand Mosque of Riyadh, stands in Deera Square in the ad-Dirah neighbourhood, right beside the al-Hukm Palace compound. A grand mosque has stood on this site since around 1830, in the reign of Turki bin Abdullah Al Saud. The present building dates from 1992, when it was rebuilt in the Modern Najdi style, and it reopened in January 1993. It seats 17,000 worshippers and covers 16,800 square metres, which makes it one of the largest mosques in Saudi Arabia. It won the Aga Khan Award for Architecture in 1995.

It photographs well because of its colour and its simple, strong shapes. The outside is mostly brown Riyadh limestone, which looks golden when lit after dark. The rectangular minarets give you clean vertical lines, and two bridges link the mosque to the palace across as-Safaat Square, so you can include some of the square around it.

Start wide to show the mosque with the square in front of it. Then move in on the minaret and the stonework for a second shot. Keep a little space around the building so the walls and minaret tops are not cropped. Stay aware of worshippers and keep your phone discreet around prayer times.

Where it is: In the ad-Dirah neighbourhood of Riyadh, in Deera Square next to the al-Hukm Palace compound.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the mosque and the open square together. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if people or barriers stop you stepping back. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the minaret and the stonework.

Where to stand: Stand in Deera Square. The mosque's facade and minarets across the open square, with the square's fountains in the foreground. From there you can see the building across the open space in front of it.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the minaret on one of the vertical lines so it stays straight. Include a strip of open square in the foreground to show the scale. Then zoom in for a tighter shot of the limestone walls and the minaret top.

Settings tip: Tap the sunlit stone to focus, then pull the exposure slider down a little so the limestone keeps its colour and the sky does not wash out. After dark, use night mode and hold the phone steady against a wall or railing.

Best time: After dark, when the limestone looks golden under the lights, or in the morning when the square is quieter. Try a few frames at dusk, when the sky still has some colour.
