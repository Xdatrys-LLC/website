This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in Barcelona as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Sagrada Família, Park Güell, Cathedral, Bishop's Bridge and Casa Batlló lead the list. The 3 free photo spots

1Sagrada Família

The Sagrada Família is the great basilica designed by Antoni Gaudí in Barcelona's Eixample district. Building began in 1882, and Gaudí took over the design the following year and gave it his own mix of Gothic and flowing Art Nouveau forms. He worked on it for the rest of his life and is buried in the crypt. When he died in 1926 it was far from finished, and work has carried on ever since. The central Tower of Jesus Christ is 172.5 m high, which makes this the tallest church in the world. Parts of the building, including the Nativity façade and the Crypt, are part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing for Gaudí's works.

It photographs well because nothing else looks like it. Dozens of carved spires, textured stonework and sculpted detail give your phone plenty to work with, even in a quick snap. The sheer height is the main challenge, so decide early whether you want the whole building or just a detail. From the phone icon on the map you are very close, so the towers will lean inwards if you tilt the phone upwards. That can look dramatic, but if you want straighter lines, step back and keep the phone level. Expect other visitors in the frame, so be patient and shoot in the gaps.

Where it is: The Sagrada Família stands in the Eixample district of Barcelona. Sagrada Família Metro station is right beside it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit more of the towers into the frame from close up. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural view of the façade. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out spires and carved details high above you.

Where to stand: Stand about 65 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. If you want the whole building in one shot, walk a little further back from this point.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and use them to keep the vertical lines of the towers straight. Hold the phone as level as you can rather than tilting it far upwards. Look for a foreground element, such as people or a tree, to give the huge building some scale. For a close-up, fill the frame with the stone carving rather than trying to include everything.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the bright sky does not wash out the detail. If you want the full height in one frame, try panorama mode held vertically, moving slowly and steadily.

Best time: Early morning usually gives softer light and fewer people in your shots. Around midday the sun is high and the light can be harsh on the stone.

2Park Güell

Park Güell is a hillside garden complex designed by Antoni Gaudí for the industrialist Eusebi Güell. It sits on the southern slope of the Turó del Carmel, so the city spreads out below it. It was meant to be a private estate of sixty villa plots, but only two houses were built. It opened to the public as a park in 1926 and is now a municipal garden. It is also part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing for Gaudí's works. It photographs well because almost everything is curved or colourful: the two gatehouses at the entrance, the long wavy bench around the main terrace, and the mosaic-covered vaulted space beneath it. The raised stone walkways have columns that look like tree trunks, which makes good leading lines. For the widest view, climb to the hill of the three crosses at the top, where the Sagrada Família, Torre Glòries, Montjuïc and the sea can be seen. Expect other visitors in most shots, so shoot a little higher or tighter to keep people out of the frame, or let them add a sense of scale. Keep your phone's lens clean, as you will be handling it a lot on the stairs and terraces. The main area has a ticket system and tickets often sell out, so book ahead where you can.

Where it is: Park Güell is in the La Salut neighbourhood of Barcelona's Gràcia district, on the southern slope of the Turó del Carmel hill, at about 41.4136° N, 2.1528° E. It is a large site, around 854 m across, and it looks over the city from above the streets of Gràcia.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the terrace, the bench and the gatehouses. Use the default lens (1x) for natural-looking views of the walkways and columns. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for mosaic details and for distant views of the city.

Where to stand: Stand about 60 m north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east. Take a few steps either way along the path to get a clean line of sight without other visitors in front of you.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and use the curve of the bench or a walkway as a line leading into the frame. Include a bit of sky and city beyond the terrace edge to show the hillside setting. Keep the phone level so the architecture does not look tilted.

Settings tip: Tap on the mosaics to set focus, then slide the exposure down slightly so the bright tiles and sky do not wash out. If the sun is behind you, check the screen for your own shadow before you shoot.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. Midday is often harsh and busy.

5Casa Batlló

Casa Batlló is one of Antoni Gaudí's best-known buildings, and it is easy to see why it draws a camera. The façade has almost no straight lines. Instead you get rippling stonework, irregular oval windows on the ground floor and a surface of broken ceramic tiles (trencadís) that shifts from golden orange to greenish blue. Locals call it the House of Bones because of its skeletal, bony look. It stands on Passeig de Gràcia in the Eixample, in the row known as the Illa de la Discòrdia, the "Block of Discord", where four very different Modernista buildings sit side by side. That makes the street a good place to compare styles in a single walk. The house was built in 1877 and Gaudí redesigned it from 1904. The work was finished in 1906, and the building became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2005. For a phone photo, the colours and curves do most of the work. Start with one wide shot of the whole building, then move in for details such as the balconies, the tile colours and the carved stone around the lower windows. Take a few shots from slightly different spots, because passers-by and street furniture can drift into the frame. If you want the whole façade without tilting the phone steeply, step back to the phone icon on the map and shoot from there.

Where it is: Casa Batlló is on Passeig de Gràcia in the Eixample district, in the centre of Barcelona, at about 41.3915 N, 2.1648 E. It is part of the Illa de la Discòrdia row of Modernista buildings.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole façade from where you stand. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if people or a tight pavement stop you stepping back. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the tiles, balconies and roof details.

Where to stand: Stand about 55 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. This distance lets you fit most of the façade in without pointing the phone steeply upwards.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the building's vertical edges up with them. Keep the phone level, because tilting it upwards makes the walls lean inward. Leave a little sky above the roofline. For a detail shot, zoom in on one balcony and the tile colours around it.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the façade to focus, then lower the exposure slider slightly so the tile colours stay rich and the sky does not wash out. Take a few frames in case people walk through.

Best time: Early morning usually means fewer people on the pavement. Soft, even daylight shows the colours of the tiles best, and harsh midday sun can create strong contrast across the façade.
