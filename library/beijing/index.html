The 3 free photo spots

1Tiananmen Square

Tiananmen Square is the huge open square in the middle of Beijing, named after the Tiananmen (Gate of Heavenly Peace) on its north side, which separates it from the Forbidden City. It photographs well because of its sheer scale and the big, formal buildings around it. The Monument to the People's Heroes, the Great Hall of the People, the National Museum of China and the Chairman Mao Memorial Hall all stand on or beside the square. Together with the Central Axis they were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2024. The square was first laid out in 1651 and made about four times bigger in the 1950s. It now measures roughly 765 by 282 metres. A national flagpole stands at the northern end, and the flag is raised and lowered there every day at sunrise and sunset. For a phone, the challenge is that everything is wide and far apart. Pick one clear subject, such as the gate, the monument or the flag, and let the empty space around it show the size. Use the gridlines to keep the horizon and building edges straight. Expect people in most frames, so include a few as a sense of scale rather than waiting for a clear shot.

Where it is: In the city centre of Beijing, directly south of the Tiananmen gate and the Forbidden City. The square is flanked by the Great Hall of the People to the west and the National Museum of China to the east.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the width of the square, and use the default lens (1x) for a more natural view of a single building. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the gate, the flag or the monument from a distance.

Where to stand: Stand about 55 m west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face east. Stay on the open ground rather than close to the buildings so you can fit more in.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the horizon on the lower third, so the sky and the large buildings take up most of the frame. Pick one subject and leave empty space around it to show the scale. For a sweeping view of the whole square, try panorama mode, holding the phone level and turning slowly. Keep the telephoto for close shots of the flagpole or monument details.

Settings tip: Tap on the main building to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky looks washed out. In night mode, brace your elbows against your body and keep still for the few seconds it takes.

Best time: Sunrise and sunset are when the daily flag ceremonies happen, so they are good times for atmosphere, though the square can be busy then. Early or late in the day the light is also softer than at midday.

2Forbidden City

The Forbidden City was the imperial palace of the Ming and Qing emperors. It was built between 1406 and 1420 and was home to twenty-four emperors until 1924. It covers about 72 hectares, is now run as the Palace Museum, and has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987. At ground level its size is hard to fit into a phone frame, so the best picture is taken from above. It marks the general area; the pavilion called 万春亭 on top of Jingshan Hill is nearby, and from there the whole palace spreads out below you to the south. You can follow the central axis through the rows of roofs, and the White Pagoda of Beihai is visible from the same spot. Guides call this the standard panorama of the palace, and it is the place most photographers aim for. Allow roughly 10 to 15 minutes to climb from the park's south entrance. Early morning or late afternoon suits it best. The pavilion is busiest around sunset and the front rail fills quickly, so arrive early if you want a clear spot. If you would like a street-level view instead, the palace's northwest and northeast corner towers rise above the moat and the red walls. On calm days the water gives a reflection of the northwest corner tower.

Where it is: In the centre of Beijing, inside the old Imperial City.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the main view. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if the full width of the palace will not fit, and to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out roofs and gates in the middle distance.

Where to stand: Stand at 万春亭 (summit of Jingshan Hill, south side of the pavilion). The whole Forbidden City spread out below to the south, with the central axis and the White Pagoda of Beihai visible from the same spot. Stand on the south side of the pavilion and face south. The whole Forbidden City lies below you, with the central axis running away from you and the White Pagoda of Beihai visible from the same spot.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the central axis up with the middle of the frame. Keep a little sky at the top and the nearer roofs at the bottom so the palace looks as large as it is. Try one wide shot and one zoomed-in shot of the halls. For a sweep including the pagoda, use panorama mode and pan slowly. As an alternative, the northwest and northeast corner towers can be photographed from outside the palace, across the moat.

Settings tip: Tap the palace to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky does not wash out. Hold the phone against the rail or your body to keep it steady.

Best time: Late afternoon before sunset, or early morning. One guide says the hour before sunset gives the warmest light. The crowd peaks around sunset, so arrive early within that hour to get a place at the rail.

19Monument to the People's Heroes

The Monument to the People's Heroes is a ten-storey granite obelisk in the southern part of Tiananmen Square, standing just in front of the Mausoleum of Mao Zedong. It honours those who died in the revolutionary struggles of the 19th and 20th centuries. At 37.94 metres tall, it rises alone from the open square, so it makes a clean, simple subject for a phone photo. Architect Liang Sicheng led the design, and building ran from August 1952 to May 1958. The front carries Mao Zedong's handwritten inscription, 'Eternal glory to the people's heroes', and the base is inlaid with eight large and two small relief panels showing major events in modern Chinese history. It has been a nationally protected heritage site since 1961.

The wide, open space around it is the main advantage. From the middle of the square you get a clear view with nothing in the way, and the Tiananmen Gate and the big state buildings frame the obelisk. The monument also works well as a foreground subject for photos of the gate. For a different picture, a close, low-angle shot shows the granite and the marble reliefs around the base. Barriers keep you back from the monument itself, so work from where you are allowed to stand. Expect security checks and large crowds, and go early or late in the day for softer light.

Where it is: In the southern part of Tiananmen Square, in central Beijing, just in front of the Mausoleum of Mao Zedong.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the obelisk with the gate and buildings around it. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the whole open square, and to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the inscription and relief panels from behind the barriers.

Where to stand: Stand in 天安门广场 (centre of the square, shooting from the north or south approach). The obelisk standing alone in the open square, with the Tiananmen Gate and surrounding state buildings framing it. For a straight-on view with the gate and state buildings framing the obelisk, move to the centre of the square by the north or south approach and shoot along the line of the square. For a close, low-angle view of the granite and the reliefs at the base, stand as near as the barriers allow.

Framing: Turn the phone upright to show the full height of the obelisk, with a little empty sky above the top. Switch on gridlines and keep the sides of the monument parallel to the vertical lines. To make the monument a foreground subject, place it to one side and let the Tiananmen Gate show behind it. A close, low-angle shot of the base is a good alternative.

Settings tip: Tap on the stone to set focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the sky does not turn white. Do not shoot straight into the sun, as faces and the shadowed side of the stone will go dark.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when the light is softer. Avoid noon, when strong backlight darkens faces.
