The 3 free photo spots

1Gatun Locks

Gatun Locks is a working set of canal locks where ships are raised and lowered in three chambers, with Gatún Lake stretching away beyond. What makes it good for photos is that the action is close and easy to read: a large ship sits in a long stone-edged chamber, and you can watch it move up or down. The best-known place to watch from is the Gatún Locks View Deck, a raised platform beside the locks, reached from the Agua Clara Visitor Center. From up there you look along the chambers and out to the lake, so a single frame can include the ship, the lock walls and the water behind. A second option is the Gatún Locks Visitors Pavilion, which gives an elevated view down onto the locks and the ships passing through. Which of the two is open can change, so check on arrival. For phone photos, start wide to show the whole scene, then move in on the ship once something is happening. Ships move slowly, so you have time to try a wide shot, a closer one and a short Live Photo of the water level changing. Ask at the visitor centre when ships are expected, as a quiet chamber makes a much less interesting picture. Entry to the View Deck is part of the Agua Clara ticket, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: Gatun Locks are on the Panama Canal in the Colón area, with Gatún Lake lying behind them. The View Deck is beside the locks, reached from the Agua Clara Visitor Center.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the chambers and the lake in one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a ship in the chamber, and use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the ship's bow or the lock gates.

Where to stand: Stand on Gatún Locks View Deck (beside the locks, reached from the Agua Clara Visitor Center). Ships rising and falling through the three Gatún chambers from a raised platform, with Gatún Lake visible beyond. For a raised view, go to the Gatún Locks View Deck beside the locks, reached from the Agua Clara Visitor Center. From this raised platform you look over the three Gatún chambers, with ships rising and falling in them and Gatún Lake visible beyond. If the deck is busy, the Gatún Locks Visitors Pavilion is an alternative with an elevated view down onto the locks.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and use the lock walls as leading lines, keeping them level with the grid. Place the ship in the lower third and leave the lake and sky above it. Take one wide shot with the whole chamber, then a closer one of the ship once it settles in the lock. A Live Photo can catch the movement of the water and the ship.

Settings tip: Tap on the ship to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is washing out. Wipe the lens before you start, as a smudge shows up in bright light.

Best time: Go when a ship is due, as the scene is much better with one in the chamber. Ask the visitor centre for the transit schedule on the day.

2Atlantic Bridge, Panama

The Atlantic Bridge (Puente Atlántico) carries a road across the Atlantic entrance to the Panama Canal, near Colón. It opened in 2019 and is the third bridge over the canal, after the Bridge of the Americas and the Centennial Bridge on the Pacific side. It is a cable-stayed bridge with two towers. The main span is 530 metres, with a 230-metre span on each side, and it was built by Vinci Construction.

It photographs well because the shapes are clean and repeated: two tall towers, fans of cables and a long, smooth deck. Visitor reviews also say the view of the canal locks from the top is good on both sides, so you can pair the structure with the working waterway below. Take a few frames looking back along the deck towards the Agua Clara side, then turn round for the Fort San Lorenzo side.

The bridge carries heavy road traffic and there is little room to stop. Do not stand on the carriageway. Only stop where it is legal and safe, and keep your phone away from the traffic side. For a calmer view of the whole bridge, the Agua Clara visitor centre shows the lock chambers in the foreground with the bridge in the distance. Check the official site before you go, as that is a separate site that may have its own entry rules.

Keep the lens clean, as humid air often leaves a film on it, and take a few frames of each view so you can pick the sharpest.

Where it is: The Atlantic Bridge is in the Colón area of Panama, where the Panama Canal meets the Atlantic. It is part of a local connection road that links Bolivar Highway in the east with the undeveloped area to the west. Agua Clara and Fort San Lorenzo are the two reference points for the views from the bridge.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit a tower and the sweep of cables into one frame. Use the default lens (1x) for the deck and towers with less distortion. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the cable lines or the locks below.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Shoot there only where it is legal and safe to stop, as no pull-off at that exact point is confirmed, and never stand on the carriageway. From the deck you can look back toward the Agua Clara side or the Fort San Lorenzo side. You get a close view of the cable-stayed deck and towers, with the locks visible below from either side of the span.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the cables up with the grid, so the towers look straight and not leaning. Keep the deck as a diagonal leading line into the frame. For a wider view of the whole bridge, the Agua Clara visitor centre shows the lock chambers in the foreground with the bridge far off.

Settings tip: Tap on the tower or sky to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour and the cables stay sharp. Hold the phone with both hands, since traffic on the bridge can make it shake.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light on the concrete and cables, and traffic may be lighter then.

3Agua Clara Locks

Agua Clara Locks are a lock complex on the Colón map. The appeal is likely to be scale: large structures, long stretches of water and plenty of open sky give you strong lines to work with. A person in the foreground helps show how big everything is.

On a phone, work with the layers. Put something close to you at the bottom of the frame, such as a railing, a sign or a friend, then let the water, the structures and the sky fill the rest. Turn on gridlines and keep the horizon level, because straight edges make a tilted shot obvious. If something is moving through the scene, take a few frames a few seconds apart rather than one, and switch on Live Photos so you can choose the best moment afterwards.

The phone icon on the map is at the spot itself, so you are already in the right place. Take a wide shot first, then step closer and use the zoom for details. Weather can change quickly, so keep a dry cloth for your lens, as damp air and drizzle can smear it. Check for any access restrictions locally before you go.

Where it is: Agua Clara Locks are on the Colón map.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the full width of the scene and the sky. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural view. Use the telephoto/zoom (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Put a railing, a sign or a person in the lower part of the frame, and let any straight edges run away from you.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the horizon up with one of the horizontal lines. Let the straight edges in the scene lead the eye towards the water or the sky. Take one wide shot, then a closer one with the zoom. If something is moving, take several frames a few seconds apart.

Settings tip: Tap the bright sky to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the sky keeps its colour and the concrete does not wash out. Switch on Live Photos when something is moving so you can pick the best frame afterwards. Wipe the lens before shooting, as humidity can leave a haze.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer harsh reflections on the water than midday. Check access details locally before you go.
