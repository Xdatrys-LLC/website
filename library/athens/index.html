The 3 free photo spots

1Acropolis of Athens

The Acropolis is the ancient citadel standing on a flat-topped rock above Athens. It rises about 150 m above sea level and covers roughly 3 hectares. The best-known building is the Parthenon, and the Propylaea, the Erechtheion and the Temple of Athena Nike stand beside it. Most of what you see today was built in the fifth century BC under Pericles, though people lived on the hill thousands of years earlier. The Parthenon was badly damaged in 1687, when a Venetian bombardment set off gunpowder stored inside it. That is why the temple is a ruin today, and the broken columns add to the picture.

It photographs well because the rock stands apart from the city, so the skyline is easy to read from a distance. A good place to shoot from is the Pnyx, a bare rocky hill facing the Acropolis. From there you get a frontal, fairly symmetrical view with pine trees in the foreground. Guides describe it as quieter than the busier hills. Keep the whole rock in the frame, with the Parthenon on top and a little sky above it. Pines on one or both sides make a natural frame. If you want a different angle, Filopappou Hill shows the Parthenon at about eye level across the valley.

Where it is: The Acropolis stands on its rocky hill in the historic centre of Athens, above the old city.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the whole rock and the pines in one frame. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the Parthenon on top. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of foreground rock and sky.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (the bare rocky outcrop of the Pnyx hill, facing the Acropolis). A more frontal, symmetrical panoramic view of the Acropolis with pine trees in the foreground.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the Parthenon up with the centre of the frame, so the rock sits level and balanced. Let a pine branch or trunk fill one side as a frame. If you have time, Filopappou Hill is an alternative with the Parthenon at roughly eye level and the city and sea behind.

Settings tip: Tap on the Parthenon to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the pale stone keeps its detail and the sky does not wash out. A panorama can work for the full hilltop, but hold the phone steady and turn slowly.

Best time: Late afternoon, according to one guide.

2Parthenon

The Parthenon is the great temple to Athena on top of the Acropolis, built between 447 and 438 BC, with the sculpted decoration finished by 432 BC. Its life since then has been busy. It became a Christian church in the late 6th century AD and a mosque after the mid-15th century. In 1687 a Venetian bomb hit it while the Ottomans were using it as a munitions dump, and the explosion severely damaged it. Restoration work has been going on since 1975, so you may spot scaffolding or cranes in your shot.

This view is from the Pnyx, on the north-eastern side of the hill. You look due east across open ground and trees, with the Acropolis and the Parthenon on the skyline and Lycabettus Hill rising behind. It is a calmer, free place to stand, and a good spot to sit for a few minutes with the temple in front of you. The trees and rocks make natural foreground for a phone photo, and the distance lets you fit the whole temple on its rock in one frame.

If you want a different angle, the Areopagus gives a closer, slightly higher view, and Filopappou Hill shows the temple side-on across a valley of pines. Areopagus can get very crowded around sunset. Wherever you stand, keep the phone level, because the temple's horizontal lines show any tilt.

Where it is: The Parthenon stands on the Acropolis, in the historic centre of Athens. The Pnyx is on a neighbouring hill to the west, in the group of rocky hills close to the Acropolis.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the telephoto lens (3x to 5x if your phone has it) to bring the temple closer and compress it against Lycabettus Hill. If you only have the default lens (1x), take one shot with the temple small among the trees and another cropped in afterwards. Avoid the ultra-wide (0.5x), which makes the Parthenon tiny.

Where to stand: Stand in Pnyx (northeastern side of the hill). The Acropolis and Parthenon seen due east across open ground and trees, with Lycabettus Hill in the background.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the Parthenon on one of the upper thirds, with the horizon of the rock on a horizontal line. Use trees or the hillside as a frame at the edge of the picture. If you want a closer or side-on view instead, Areopagus and Filopappou Hill are the alternatives.

Settings tip: Tap the temple to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky stays blue and the stone keeps its colour. Take a few shots in a row, as people and branches move through the frame.

Best time: Late afternoon is likely to suit this view, as the sun should be behind you and light the front of the temple. Check the official site for the Acropolis hours if you also plan to go up.

4Erechtheion

The Erechtheion is an ancient Greek temple of the Ionic order on the north side of the Acropolis, mainly dedicated to the goddess Athena. It is best known for the Porch of the Caryatids. The building is also unusual because its layout is lopsided, which sets it apart from other Greek temples. This may be down to the uneven ground or to the several cults it housed. Nobody is sure exactly when it was built. It was traditionally dated to about 421-406 BCE, though more recent work favours the 430s. The surviving building accounts from 409-404 BC allow an unusually secure dating of construction.

It photographs well because the porch gives you a strong foreground subject, and the Parthenon rises directly behind it. Guides single out this angle as the best one for the Caryatids. The recommended approach is to stand on the south side of the porch and shoot north, so the figures sit in front and the Parthenon fills the background. Keep the frame fairly tight on the porch, then take a second, wider shot to show the setting. The figures you see on the temple are replicas, and the originals are in the Acropolis Museum, which is worth knowing if you want to see them close up.

Where it is: The Erechtheion stands on the north side of the Acropolis, in the historic centre of Athens. It and the Parthenon are both on the Acropolis.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the Caryatid porch and the Parthenon both fit in one frame. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a closer shot of the figures. Keep the ultra-wide (0.5x) for a different view that takes in more of the surroundings.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. For the angle the guides recommend, move to the south side of the Porch of the Caryatids and face north. From there the Caryatid porch is in front of you, with the Parthenon appearing behind it in the same frame.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and put the porch on one of the lower thirds, leaving the Parthenon visible above and behind it. Keep the phone level so the columns and roofline stay straight. For a different view, the Propylaea gives a wider shot of the Erechtheion among the other Acropolis monuments. Filopappou Hill shows the whole Acropolis from outside, where the temple is small in frame and a telephoto helps.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework of the porch to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is washing out. Take a few shots, since other visitors often walk through the frame.

Best time: Morning or late afternoon, when the sunlight falls from the side and shows the shape of the figures, according to one guide.
