The 3 free photo spots

1Uluwatu Temple

Uluwatu Temple (Pura Luhur Uluwatu) is a Balinese Hindu sea temple on the south-western tip of the Bukit Peninsula. It sits right on the edge of a cliff about 70 metres high, with the sea below. That drop is why it photographs so well: the temple's dark stone and carved gateways sit against open sky and sea, and the cliff gives every frame a sense of height and distance. The temple is dedicated to Sang Hyang Widhi Wasa in his form as Rudra. It was greatly enlarged in the 11th century by the Javanese sage Empu Kuturan, and the name Luhur is linked to the later sage Nirartha, who is said to have attained moksha here. A Kecak dance based on the Ramayana is staged on the cliff-side in the evening, so there is a lot going on around sunset. Long-tailed macaques live around the temple and are known for grabbing cameras, sunglasses and flip-flops. Keep your phone in a hand or wrist strap and out of reach when you are not shooting. Take a wide view first to show the cliff and the sea, then move in on gateways, stonework and the edge of the headland. Check the official site before you go for access rules, any areas closed to visitors and performance details.

Where it is: On the south-western tip of the Bukit Peninsula, in Pecatu village (South Kuta), Badung Regency, in southern Bali. The temple stands on a clifftop above the sea.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the temple and cliff together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) for the sweep of coastline and sky, and to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out gateways and carvings from a distance.

Where to stand: Stand about 25 m north of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the temple on one third of the frame, leaving the sea and sky for the other two. Keep the horizon level. With ultra-wide, keep the temple away from the edges so it does not stretch or lean. Include a path, wall or gateway in the foreground to give a sense of depth.

Settings tip: Tap on the temple stone to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour. Near sunset, try night mode only if the light is really low, and hold the phone steady against a wall or your body. Keep a firm grip on it, because of the macaques.

Best time: Late afternoon into sunset suits the sea view, and the Kecak dance is staged at about 6pm. Check the official site before you go for current visiting arrangements.

2Seminyak Beach

Seminyak Beach is a beach in Seminyak, in Badung regency, Bali. It is the kind of place where the sky can do much of the work. With a horizon, a band of wet sand and waves rolling in, even a simple phone photo can have space and depth. The sand is a good surface for leading lines. Wave edges, footprints and the line where the tide has been all draw the eye towards the horizon.

From there the shoreline runs away from you towards the south-east, which suits a photo with a clear foreground, a middle stretch of beach and a distant horizon. If there are people on the beach, they can give a sense of scale and life when used carefully. If you would rather have an empty frame, wait a moment for people to move out of the shot.

Keep your phone away from spray and blown sand, and wipe the lens with a soft cloth before you start, as salty haze softens pictures. Stand on firmer, damp sand to keep your footing, and watch for incoming waves before you crouch down for a low angle. Take a few frames in a row as waves arrive, then pick the one where the water shape looks best.

Where it is: Seminyak Beach is in Seminyak, Tibubeneng, Kuta, Badung, Bali.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the full sweep of beach and sky, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a cleaner, less stretched view. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out surfers, waves or distant figures on the sand.

Where to stand: Stand on Jalan Double Six (west end, where the road meets the sand of Double Six Beach). The open western horizon with the sun sinking into the Indian Ocean, framed by the wide beach and colourful beanbags. Use it as a distant anchor and make the shoreline in front of you the main subject, or take the short walk towards it for a closer view.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the horizon on the upper or lower third line, depending on whether the sky or the sand is more interesting. Let the edge of the wet sand lead the eye diagonally towards the south-east. Keep the horizon level, and add one person or object in the foreground for scale.

Settings tip: Tap the sea or sky to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so bright sky keeps its colour and the sand does not wash out. Try a Live Photo or motion capture to catch waves, and pick the best frame afterwards. Use night mode only if the light fades after sunset, and brace your elbows against your body to keep the shot steady.

Best time: Late afternoon towards sunset is often a rewarding time for softer, warmer light, though check where the sun sets from where you stand. Early morning is often quieter, if you want fewer people in the frame.

3Mount Batur

Mount Batur is an active volcano on the island of Bali, northwest of Mount Agung, sitting inside a huge double caldera. The bowl-shaped caldera is about 10 by 13 km, and a 700-metre-tall cone rises from its floor above Lake Batur. That layering of rim, cone and lake is what makes it so rewarding on a phone. You can show the whole landscape in one frame, then pick out single details such as the cone, the water and the dark lava. The first recorded eruption was in 1804 and the most recent was in 2000. The large lava field from 1963 can still be seen from Kintamani, a town on the southwest ridge of the caldera, which is a good place to look across at the volcano. About 15 villages lie inside the caldera, including Kedisan, Songan, Trunyan and Toya Bungkah, so village roofs, fields and farmland can add foreground interest and a sense of scale. UNESCO added the caldera to its Global Geoparks Network in 2012. For photos, start wide to capture the shape of the caldera, then zoom in on the cone and the lava field. The phone icon on the map is at the volcano itself, so it suits a summit-area view. If you want the classic whole-mountain picture, step back to a ridge viewpoint such as Kintamani. Wipe your lens before you start, as haze and moisture can soften distant shots.

Where it is: Mount Batur is in the Batur caldera on Bali, northwest of Mount Agung. Kintamani, a town on the caldera's southwest ridge, looks across to it, and the volcano is photographed at the spot itself.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the caldera, lake and sky together. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a natural view, then use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the cone and the dark lava field.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. This suits close-up views from the volcano. For a full view of the mountain and its lava field, move back to a ridge viewpoint such as Kintamani.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the horizon on the upper or lower third line. Include some foreground, such as a path, a wall, a field or the rim edge, to give depth. Keep the cone off-centre so the lake has room in the frame. Try a panorama to capture the sweep of the caldera, moving slowly and keeping the phone level.

Settings tip: Tap on the cone to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour and detail. Use night mode only if the light is low, and hold the phone steady against a wall or your body.

Best time: Early morning often gives clearer air and softer light, but conditions vary, so check the weather on the day.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Bali?

The first three on this map are Uluwatu Temple, Seminyak Beach and Mount Batur. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 20.

Where should I stand to photograph Uluwatu Temple?

Stand about 25 m north of Uluwatu Temple, at the phone icon on the map, and face south. Start with the default lens (1x) for the temple and cliff together.

When is the best time to photograph Seminyak Beach?

Late afternoon towards sunset is often a rewarding time for softer, warmer light, though check where the sun sets from where you stand. Early morning is often quieter, if you want fewer people in the frame.

How do I get to Mount Batur?

Go by road, starting from Kintamani, the well-known town on the southwest ridge of the caldera, and continue by taxi, a ride-hailing app or a hired scooter into the caldera.
