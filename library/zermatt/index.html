The 3 free photo spots

1Matterhorn

The Matterhorn is the pointed, near-symmetrical peak that towers over Zermatt and is one of the most recognisable mountains in the Alps. Its summit stands at 4,478 metres, and it sits on the border between Switzerland and Italy. It has four steep faces, and the Swiss town of Zermatt lies to the north-east of it. Its shape is so distinctive that many other mountains around the world are nicknamed 'the Matterhorn' of their own country.

It photographs well because nothing crowds the outline. The pyramid shape stands alone above the glaciers and works as a simple, strong subject on a phone screen. The first ascent was made from Zermatt in 1865, and the mountain has drawn visitors ever since the railways arrived in the area at the end of the 19th century. It has also been claimed to be the most photographed mountain in the world.

For your own picture, think of the peak as the subject and look for something nearer to put in front of it, such as rooftops, a fence, a tree or a street lamp. That gives the photo a sense of scale. Leave a little sky above the summit so the tip is not cut off. If cloud is covering the top, wait a few minutes, because it often shifts.

Where it is: The Matterhorn rises to the south-west of Zermatt, in the canton of Valais, and is the mountain that dominates the town's skyline. The mountain overlooks Zermatt.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the telephoto lens (3x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the summit closer and fill the frame. Use the default lens (1x) when you want some of the town or foreground in the picture. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only when you have a strong foreground, as it makes the peak look small.

Where to stand: Stand on Mürini (top of the Mürini hill in Le Petit Village, northeast edge of town; the official site calls it Viewpoint Riedweg). The wooden chalets of the village in the foreground with the Matterhorn rising above them, the classic village-and-peak composition. For your photo, take the mountain's position as the direction to aim at: from Zermatt town, look south-west.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and place the summit on one of the upper intersections, with the base of the mountain on the lower line. Add a foreground such as a rooftop, fence or tree to give depth. Take one wide shot and one zoomed shot from the same spot.

Settings tip: Tap on the mountain to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so bright snow and sky keep their detail. If your phone offers it, a Live Photo can help you pick the moment when the clouds clear.

Best time: Check the weather forecast and choose a clear moment, and be ready to wait if cloud is covering the summit.

2Gornergrat

Gornergrat is a rocky ridge in the Pennine Alps, 3,135 m high, looking over the Gorner Glacier to the south-east of Zermatt. It photographs well because so much of the high Alps is in front of you at once. The ridge sits between the Gorner and Findel glaciers and has views of 29 mountains over 4,000 m, including the Matterhorn, Monte Rosa's Dufourspitze, Liskamm, Dom and Weisshorn. Gornergrat can be reached from Zermatt by the Gornergrat rack railway, which opened in 1898 and is the highest open-air railway in Europe. It climbs almost 1,500 m from Zermatt. Near the top you will also see the Kulm Hotel, whose two towers have housed astronomical observatories since the late 1960s, which makes a good second subject. For phone photos, start with a wide view of the glacier and the peaks, then move in on single mountains. Look for something in the foreground, such as the rock of the ridge, the railing or a fellow traveller, to give a sense of scale. The air is thin and the light is bright, so check each shot on screen. Mountain weather changes quickly, so take a few frames whenever the peaks clear instead of waiting for a perfect moment.

Where it is: Gornergrat is a ridge about three kilometres east of Zermatt, high above the village, between the Gorner and Findel glaciers. The railway station and the Kulm Hotel sit at its south-western end.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the glacier and the whole line of peaks, the default lens (1x) for a natural view, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the Matterhorn or other summits.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. You are at the Gornergrat spot itself, so the subject is right where you are. Face the mountains and glacier and move a few steps along the ridge to find a view that is not blocked by people or buildings.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the horizon on a lower line so the peaks fill most of the frame. Include some ridge rock in the foreground for depth. Zoom in for a tighter shot of the Matterhorn, and try panorama mode for the full sweep, moving slowly and keeping the phone level.

Settings tip: Tap on a mountain to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so snow and glacier keep their detail instead of turning plain white. Hold the phone with both hands, as the wind can shake it.

Best time: Clear mornings are often the best bet, before cloud builds around the peaks, but conditions change a lot, so check the weather forecast on the day.

3Zermatt railway station

Zermatt railway station is the southern end of the metre-gauge line up from Visp and Brig, and for most visitors it is the first place they set foot in the car-free village. Several Glacier Express trains start or finish here each day, and a shuttle runs to nearby Täsch every twenty minutes, so there is usually a train coming or going. The Gornergrat Railway's valley station is directly across the street, which makes this a busy spot for arrival-day photos.

Guides call this the classic first Matterhorn photo for new arrivals. From the church steps or the Community Centre terrace, looking south, the Matterhorn rises above the old wooden Valais houses in the foreground. That mix of dark timber and a big peak is what makes the picture work. The station building is not the focus of this view, so think of this as your arrival-day Matterhorn shot, with the station a short walk east.

If you also want the station itself, take a few frames when you pass it. Trains and platform scenes give a good sense of how you arrived. If you would like a second Matterhorn view afterwards, try the river bridge, Kirchbrücke, where the peak is framed down the main street.

Where it is: In Zermatt, in the Canton of Valais, at the foot of Bahnhofstrasse. The station sits directly across the street from the Gornergrat Railway's valley station.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the Matterhorn and the wooden houses sit together in one frame. If the peak looks small, step up to 2x or 3x (depending on your phone) to bring it closer. Keep the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the station front or platforms, where you can't step back.

Where to stand: Stand in Kirchplatz (on the church steps or the Community Centre terrace, looking south). The Matterhorn rising above the old wooden Valais houses in the foreground; the sources do not say the station building itself is in frame. Stand on the church steps or on the Community Centre terrace and face south. The Matterhorn rises above the old wooden Valais houses. The station is a short walk to the east, to your left as you face south, and is not what you are framing here.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the Matterhorn on one of the upper intersections, with the timber houses filling the lower part of the picture. Keep the phone level so the roofs don't slope. For a second view, Kirchbrücke frames the Matterhorn down the main street from the river bridge.

Settings tip: Tap on the mountain to set focus and exposure, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky and snow keep their detail. Live Photos can add a nice touch if people or trains are moving through the foreground.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people on the steps. The peak can be hidden by cloud, so if it is covered, wait a few minutes and check again.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Zermatt?

The first three on this map are Matterhorn, Gornergrat and Zermatt Station. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 9.

Where should I stand to photograph the Matterhorn?

Stand on Mürini (top of the Mürini hill in Le Petit Village, northeast edge of town; the official site calls it Viewpoint Riedweg). Use the telephoto lens (3x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the summit closer and fill the frame.

When is the best time to photograph the Gornergrat?

Clear mornings are often the best bet, before cloud builds around the peaks, but conditions change a lot, so check the weather forecast on the day.

How do I get to Zermatt Station?

Zermatt is car-free, so you arrive by train: the Matterhorn Gotthard Bahn runs from Visp and Brig, and a shuttle runs from Täsch.
