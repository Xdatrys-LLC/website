The 3 free photo spots

1Royal Arcade, Melbourne

Royal Arcade is a Victorian shopping arcade in the middle of Melbourne's city centre, and it is the oldest surviving arcade in Australia. It opened in 1870 and links Bourke Street Mall with Little Collins Street. Inside, a high glass roof sits on cast iron frames above bow-fronted shops, and the black-and-white chequered floor leads your eye along the whole length. The shopfronts were restored in 2002-04 and painted yellow and white with gilded touches, so the colours photograph warmly.

The best photo is at the Little Collins Street end. Stand just inside the doors, and turn round to look up. There you'll find the carved giants Gog and Magog on either side of Gaunt's Clock, added in 1893. They are based on the figures in London's Guildhall, and they strike bells with their arms each hour. Seen from below, they make a strong, centred shot with the arched niche around them.

If you have time, take a second photo of the long view down the corridor, either from the main entrance on Bourke Street Mall or from further inside the arcade. The roof, shop fronts and floor all lead the eye in the same direction, which makes a tidy, symmetrical picture. Keep moving gently, as it is a through-route for shoppers and walkers.

Where it is: Royal Arcade is in the central business district of Melbourne, running between Bourke Street Mall and Little Collins Street. It sits close to the other Victorian arcade, the Block Arcade, and is part of the city's network of lanes and arcades.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit both giants, the clock and the arch in one frame. Then try the default lens (1x) for a cleaner view with less distortion. Use telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for a close-up of the clock face or the carved figures.

Where to stand: Stand on Little Collins Street (Little Collins Street entrance, just inside the doors, looking up at Gog and Magog and the Gaunt's Clock). The two carved giants Gog and Magog flanking the Gaunt's Clock, seen from below at the arcade's southern entrance. Just inside the doors of the arcade's southern entrance, and turn round to look up at Gog and Magog and Gaunt's Clock. From here the figures and the clock are above you, framed by the arch.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the clock up with the centre line so the two giants sit evenly on each side. Keep the phone level, as tilting it back makes the walls lean inwards. For a second picture, face north and shoot down the corridor, with the glass roof and shop fronts running away from you. The same long view is available from the Bourke Street Mall entrance.

Settings tip: Tap the clock to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the bright glass roof and sky don't wash out the carvings. Hold the phone with both hands and keep it still; the light inside can be dim.

Best time: Around the top of the hour, when the figures strike the bells. Arrive a few minutes early to get your spot and frame.

2Melbourne Cricket Ground

The Melbourne Cricket Ground, known locally as the 'G, is the largest stadium in Australia, with a capacity of about 100,000. It sits in Yarra Park in East Melbourne. The Melbourne Cricket Club established the ground in 1853, and it is built on what was a Wurundjeri camping ground. It hosted the world's first Test match in 1877, the opening and closing ceremonies of the 1956 Olympics, and today stages the AFL Grand Final and the Boxing Day Test. It is on the Victorian Heritage Register and the Australian National Heritage List.

It photographs well because of its sheer scale. From the park, the floodlights and the tops of the stands rise above the trees, and open lawn and paths give you a clear foreground. A wide shot with grass, trees and stadium layered together shows the size of the place better than a close-up of one wall. Use the paths to lead the eye towards the ground. If you want a different view, Norris Reserve gives a distant skyline panorama with the ground as one part of the frame. On match days the park is used for parking and gets very crowded, so a quiet day is much easier for photos. If you plan to go inside the stadium, check the official site before you go.

Where it is: The ground stands in Yarra Park in the suburb of East Melbourne, Victoria.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the stands, floodlights and trees together. Then try the default lens (1x) for a more natural view. If your phone has a telephoto (2x-5x), use it to pick out the floodlights and roofline above the treetops.

Where to stand: Stand in Yarra Park (west of the ground, near Jolimont station, looking back at the stadium). The MCG's floodlights and stands rising above the trees of the park, with the ground seen across open lawn and paths. From here you see the floodlights and stands rising above the trees, across open lawn and paths.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the stadium along the upper third, with lawn or a path in the lower part of the frame. Keep the horizon level, because the large stands make any tilt obvious. Let the trees frame the edges, and wait for people to walk out of your shot. For a wider view with the ground as only one part of the scene, Norris Reserve gives a distant city skyline.

Settings tip: Tap on the floodlights or the stand roofs to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour and the stadium does not wash out. Use the telephoto for detail and the ultra-wide for scale.

Best time: Choose a day with no match, as the park is busy with parking and crowds on match days. Morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light.

3Eureka Tower

Eureka Tower is a 297.3 m glass skyscraper in the Southbank part of Melbourne, finished in 2006 and officially opened on 11 October 2006. Its looks tell a story. The design draws on the Eureka Stockade, the 1854 rebellion during the Victorian gold rush. The gold crown stands for the gold rush, the red stripe for the blood spilt, and the blue glass and white lines for the colours of the stockade flag. That makes it easy to photograph, because the colours and bands read clearly even on a small phone screen.

At the base there is an art installation of gold bees in and around a white box, like a man-made beehive. If you can find it, it is a good foreground detail to include when you point your phone upwards. The tower's top floors hold the Melbourne Skydeck on level 88, which is the highest public viewing floor in a building in the Southern Hemisphere. An entry fee applies there, so if you want pictures from inside, check the official site before you go.

For the best result, think about shape rather than detail. The tower is very tall and narrow, so a photo from close by needs the camera tilted up, which can make the sides lean inwards. Keep the phone as upright as you can and use the gridlines to line up the edges. A few steps back, or a slightly different angle, will often help more than zooming.

Where it is: Eureka Tower is in the Southbank precinct of Melbourne, Victoria.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for a close-up shot of the whole tower. Use the default lens (1x) for the base, the bees and the lower levels. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) from further back for the gold crown and the red stripe.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. This is a close-up spot, not a distant view. Move to a safe spot outside near the building and step back as far as you can to fit more of the tower in, tilting the phone upwards as needed.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the tower's vertical edges up with them. Hold the phone as upright as you can, because tilting it steeply makes the tower lean. Include the gold bees at the base as foreground if you can find the bee installation, and let the tower lead the eye up to the gold crown. Portrait orientation suits the tower's height.

Settings tip: Tap the sky or the glass to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the bright sky does not wash out the blue glass and the gold top. If your phone has a Live Photo or motion option, switch it off for this shot and keep the phone still instead.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon is usually kinder to the glass than harsh midday light, and a clear day shows the blue cladding and gold top best. Check the sun's direction on the day, as it decides which face of the tower is lit.
