This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in Dublin as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Guinness Storehouse, Trinity College, Kilmainham Gaol, Dublin Castle and Grafton Street lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Guinness Storehouse

The Guinness Storehouse is the visitor centre at St. James's Gate Brewery, and it is one of the best-known sights in Dublin. The building went up in 1902 as a fermentation plant, in the style of the Chicago School of architecture. It was the first multi-storey steel-framed building in Ireland. It opened to visitors on 2 December 2000 and has since welcomed over twenty million people. Inside, seven floors wrap around a glass atrium shaped like a pint of Guinness, and the top floor has the Gravity Bar with views over Dublin.

For a free photo, the best spot is outside, in front of the Guinness gate beside the Storehouse. The brand sign sits across the entrance and the brewery buildings fill the space behind it, so one frame tells you where you are. It is at the corner where Robert Street, Market Street South and Belview meet. The Storehouse itself is a ticketed attraction, so check the official site before you go if you plan to go inside.

Keep the shot simple. Stand square to the gate so the sign reads clearly, and let the brewery buildings sit behind as the backdrop. If other visitors are posing, wait a moment and a gap usually opens. Inside, the Gravity Bar gives rooftop views rather than a view of the building, so it makes a different kind of photo.

Where it is: The Guinness Storehouse is inside St. James's Gate Brewery, west of Dublin city centre, on Market Street South. The gate for the photo is at the corner where Market Street South, Robert Street and Belview meet, beside the Storehouse.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the gates, the sign and the buildings behind. If you are close and the gates will not fit, switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x), but keep the phone level so the edges do not lean. If your phone has telephoto (2x or more), use it for a tighter shot of the sign.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject, at the corner where Robert Street, Market Street South and Belview meet (the phone icon on the map), in front of the brewery gate beside the Storehouse. The photo shows the gate and its Guinness sign, with the brewery buildings behind.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the gates up square so the sign sits near the centre. Include a little of the brewery buildings above the gates for context. For a different view, the Gravity Bar inside gives panoramic views over the Dublin rooftops, though it is ticketed and does not show the building's exterior.

Settings tip: Tap the sign to set focus, then nudge the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the gates keep their detail. Take a few shots in case people walk through the frame. Inside, photography is allowed without flash or tripods.

Best time: Go in daylight so the sign is easy to read. Outside the busiest part of the day you are more likely to get the gates with fewer people in front.

2Trinity College: Front Square and the Long Room

Trinity College is Ireland's oldest university, founded in 1592 by Queen Elizabeth I, and its old courtyards are a major part of a destination that receives over two million visitors a year. The 18th-century Parliament Square, often called Front Square, holds the freestanding Campanile. The Old Library was begun in 1712 and has housed the Book of Kells since 1661. Inside it, the Long Room is a 65-metre barrel-vaulted hall with two storeys of oak bookcases and marble busts in the niches. The grounds receive over two million visitors a year, so the main courtyards can be busy. For a calmer view, this map sends you to Fellows' Square, a quieter open-air courtyard. From here you get a wider look at the historic quadrangles, with the old library building across the lawns. Cobbles, lawns, stone façades and the Campanile give you plenty of layers to work with. Inside the Long Room, flash is prohibited and the Book of Kells itself may not be photographed. A conservation project has moved most of the books into storage, so the shelves may look emptier than in older photos. Take your wide shot here first, then walk over to Front Square for the Campanile.

Where it is: In the centre of Dublin, on the Trinity College campus beside College Green. The Front Square lies a short walk inside the college grounds.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the whole quadrangle, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the old library building or the Campanile.

Where to stand: Stand about 110 m south-east of the subject, in Fellows' Square (the phone icon on the map), and face north-west. From here your phone sees the college's historic quadrangles, with the old library building across the lawns. Front Square, the usual busy spot, is to the north-west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the buildings' vertical lines straight to stop them leaning. Put the lawn or cobbles in the lower third and the old library across the middle. As an alternative, walk to Front Square and frame the Campanile with the surrounding buildings and open ground around it. A panorama works well across the quadrangles if you pan slowly.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky and pale stone keep their detail. In the Long Room, switch the flash off, as it is prohibited, and hold the phone steady using the 1x lens.

Best time: Try going early or later in the day, when the courtyards tend to be quieter. Check the official site for visiting times before you go.

3Kilmainham Gaol

Kilmainham Gaol is a former prison in west Dublin, now run as a museum by the Office of Public Works. It opened in 1796 as the "New Gaol" and was officially the County of Dublin Gaol. It is best known for holding Irish revolutionaries, including the leaders of the 1916 Easter Rising, several of whom were executed here. The prison was decommissioned in 1924 and was later saved from demolition by a restoration society formed in 1958.

For photographs, the Victorian East Wing is the big draw. Standing on the floor of the central atrium, you look up at tiers of cells and iron walkways rising on every side beneath a skylight. The lines run in near-perfect symmetry towards the middle, which makes a strong, simple picture even on a phone. Guides often describe it as the most photographed part of the gaol.

Access to the East Wing needs a paid tour or ticket, so plan ahead. The museum's photo rules are reported as allowing photos without flash, and tripods are not allowed. Look out for signs inside in case anything has changed. Keep your phone steady, give other visitors room, and leave the flash off. The space is also a solemn one, so a quiet, respectful approach suits it.

Where it is: Kilmainham Gaol is in Kilmainham, a district of Dublin to the west of the city centre. The nearest Luas stops are on the Red Line, a short walk to the south.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural view of the atrium. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want to fit in the full height of the cell tiers and the skylight, since you cannot step back far.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From here the photo shows rows of cell tiers and iron walkways rising around the open space under the skylight.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the central vertical with the middle of the atrium, so the walkways converge evenly on both sides. Tilt the phone up slowly until the skylight sits near the top of the frame. For a different view, the entrance façade with the tricolour and the open sky above Stonebreakers' Yard with the memorial cross are also worth a photo.

Settings tip: Tap on the cells to focus, then pull the exposure slider down a little so the bright skylight does not wash out. Keep the flash off. Hold the phone with both hands and brace your elbows against your body for a steadier shot. Night mode may switch on in the dim light, so stay still for a moment while it works.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when light enters from above. Tour times are set by the site, so check the official site before you go.
