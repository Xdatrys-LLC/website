The 3 free photo spots

1Central Market

Central Market began in 1888 as a wet market, and the Art Deco building you see today was finished in 1937. It was saved from demolition and later renovated in 1985–86, then relaunched as Pasar Budaya (the Cultural Market). That history gives you plenty to photograph: a light-blue facade, long rows of stalls and a lively street scene. From there, look north towards the market, which sits with the covered pedestrian-only section of Jalan Hang Kasturi to the north. Walk about 300 m north to reach that covered walkway, which has food and drink kiosks and stalls, so the photo is full of colour, signs and people. This stretch links to Kasturi Walk, opened in 2011, which is known for snack and souvenir stalls and for street musicians. If you want the classic view of the building itself, the front entrance, with the drop-pin sculpture in the foreground, is the alternative angle. Kasturi Walk, nearby, suits a wider street scene with the market in the background. On a phone, the covered walkway works well because the roof frames the shot and leads the eye along the street. Keep your phone steady, wait for a gap in the crowd, and ask stall owners before taking close-ups of people or their goods. Check the front of the building for works or barriers before you plan a facade shot.

Where it is: Central Market is in central Kuala Lumpur, on Jalan Tun Tan Cheng Lock and the pedestrian-only part of Jalan Hang Kasturi, next to the Klang River and a few minutes from Petaling Street.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the covered walkway. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the full length of the street and the roof. Use telephoto (2x or more) for details such as signs and stall displays.

Where to stand: Stand on Jalan Hang Kasturi (covered pedestrian section beside the market). The covered walkway with kiosks and stalls running along the side of the market building.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the walkway so it runs towards the middle of the frame, with the stalls on either side. Keep the phone level so the roofline stays straight. For a different view, try the market's front entrance with the drop-pin sculpture in the foreground, or walk along Kasturi Walk for a wider street scene with the market in the background.

Settings tip: Tap on a bright area of the street to set focus and exposure, then use the exposure slider to bring back detail if the covered part looks dark. Try a Live Photo to catch the movement of people walking through.

Best time: Go earlier in the day for fewer people in the walkway. The roof gives some cover from sun and rain, so the spot works in most weather.

2Petronas Towers

The Petronas Towers are a pair of 88-storey towers linked by a sky bridge, standing 451.9 m tall. From 1998 to 2004 they were the tallest buildings in the world, and they are still the tallest twin towers. They were officially opened on 31 August 1999 on the site of the old Selangor Turf Club race track. The steel and glass skin is designed to echo patterns from Islamic art.

For a phone photo, the classic spot is KLCC Park, on the lake side, looking at the towers across the water. From here you can fit both towers into one upright frame, with the park's greenery and fountain area around them. If the water is calm, the towers also appear as a reflection. Visitor guides recommend the lake edge for exactly this view, and it is free to stand there.

The best light is blue hour, when the sky is deep blue and the towers are lit. Arrive before sunset, because the walkways can get busy and the best places at the water's edge fill up. If you want a different view, the Pintasan Saloma pedestrian and bicycle bridge shows the towers above the city, with the lit bridge structure in the foreground at night.

Where it is: The towers stand in Kuala Lumpur, above the Suria KLCC shopping mall.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) and hold the phone upright, so both towers fit with some water and greenery below them. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close to the water and want more of the park around the towers. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details of the towers' upper floors and the sky bridge.

Where to stand: Stand in KLCC Park (lake side of the park, facing the towers across the water). Both towers framed by the lake, park greenery and fountain area, with the towers' reflection in calm water. For the classic across-the-water view recommended by visitor guides, walk to the lake edge in KLCC Park. From there you see both towers framed by the lake, the park greenery and the fountain area, with the towers' reflection in calm water.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the towers with the vertical lines, so they stand straight. Keep the phone level rather than tilting it upwards. At the lake edge, put the water in the lower part of the frame so the reflection fills the foreground. For a different shot, try the Pintasan Saloma bridge, which gives a view of the towers over the city with the bridge's lit structure in the foreground.

Settings tip: Tap the towers to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the lit towers do not turn white. After dark, use night mode and rest your elbows or the phone on a solid ledge to keep it steady. At the lake, wait for a moment when the water is calm for the best reflection.

Best time: Blue hour: from about 30 to 60 minutes before sunset until full darkness. Arrive before sunset to get a good spot.

3Batu Caves

Batu Caves is a limestone hill about 13 km north of central Kuala Lumpur, with a group of Hindu temples built into its caves. It photographs well because the colour is so concentrated: a 42.7 m golden Murugan statue, a rainbow-painted staircase of 272 concrete steps (built in 1940 and repainted in 2018) and a sheer limestone cliff behind. The hill is about 325 m tall, and the temple to Murugan sits in the largest cave. From there you can photograph the 15 m Hanuman statue and the Hanuman temple (opened November 2001), along with the painted figures around the cave entrance. It is often quieter here than at the foot of the main staircase, and it gives you colourful detail shots instead of the usual wide view. Monkeys are common around the steps and forecourt, so keep your phone and food close. Thaipusam brings very large crowds to the forecourt. Photography inside the caves is restricted, with no flash and no drones, so ask permission before photographing worshippers.

Where it is: Batu Caves is in Gombak, Selangor, about 13 km north of central Kuala Lumpur.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the Hanuman statue and the painted figures. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the statue and the cave entrance together in one frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as faces, paintwork and the staircase. It also keeps you at a respectful distance from the monkeys.

Where to stand: Stand at Ramayana Cave (outside the entrance, beside the Hanuman statue at the foot of the hill, left of the main staircase). The large green Hanuman statue and the painted, colourful sculptures around the cave entrance. From here you see the large green Hanuman statue and the colourful painted sculptures around the cave entrance. It is a quieter, less crowded place to shoot than the main staircase.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the Hanuman statue upright, with the painted figures filling the lower corners of the frame. Then step back for a wider shot that includes the cave entrance. For a different view, the Lord Murugan statue is an alternative: it shows the golden figure, the rainbow staircase and the cliff together in one frame.

Settings tip: Tap on the statue to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the bright paint and sky do not wash out. Do not use flash near the cave or inside it, as it is not allowed.

Best time: No best time is given for this spot. As general advice, softer light earlier or later in the day is kinder to bright painted colours than harsh midday sun. Avoid Thaipusam if you want an uncrowded frame, though it is a very busy time for the festival itself.
