The 3 free photo spots

1Caernarfon Castle

Caernarfon Castle is a huge medieval fortress in Caernarfon, Gwynedd, north-west Wales. King Edward I began the stone castle in 1283, on a spot where a Norman motte-and-bailey castle had stood since the late 11th century. It was built on a grand scale because it was meant to be the administrative centre of north Wales. The walls are said to echo those of Constantinople, and from the outside it looks almost complete, though the buildings inside have gone and some parts were never finished. It was used for the investiture of the Prince of Wales in 1911 and again in 1969. Today it is looked after by Cadw and is part of a World Heritage Site.

For a phone photo, the appeal is the sheer mass of stone. Long, solid walls and towers fill the frame, and they sit well against open sky. Start wide, then move in for smaller details such as a tower top or the stonework. Keep your gridlines on so the long wall edges stay straight, and take a few frames at slightly different angles, as the outline looks different with every few steps you take.

Where it is: The castle stands on the Caernarfon waterfront, on the peninsula between the River Seiont and the Menai Strait, right beside the old town and its walls.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the full length of the walls and towers. Then switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural, less stretched view of the castle. If your phone has a telephoto (2x-5x), use it to pick out towers, battlements and stone detail.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a main wall or tower edge up with a vertical line so the stonework does not lean. Keep a band of sky above the towers and avoid cutting through the tops. Try one wide shot of the whole castle, then one close frame of a tower using the telephoto. If people are walking by, wait a moment: a small figure at the base shows how big the walls are.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the sky keeps its detail and the walls don't turn into a silhouette. If you are shooting wide, keep your phone level, as the ultra-wide lens makes tilted walls look more obvious.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer, lower light that brings out the texture of the stone. Midday sun can be harsh and is often busier with visitors.

2Royal Welch Fusiliers Museum

The Royal Welch Fusiliers Museum is on the Caernarfon map, and it is the second stop. It is a good place for a record shot of your visit.

The best view is from a short step back. Switch on the gridlines so the vertical edges of the building stay straight.

The default lens (1x) gives a natural, uncluttered view of the building. If you are tight on space, the ultra-wide (0.5x) will take in more, but keep the phone level and keep people away from the edges, where they can look stretched. Once you have the wide view, move closer or use the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for architectural details you can see.

This is a place to visit as well as photograph, so allow time to see what is inside. Check the official site before you go for visiting details.

Where it is: In Caernarfon.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole frontage. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you cannot step back far enough. Use the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for architectural details you can see.

Where to stand: Stand about 35 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. From here the museum is in front of you and you can fit in the whole of it.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the vertical edges of the walls with them, so the building stands straight. Hold the phone at chest height rather than tilting it up. Leave a little space around the top and sides so you do not crop the building. Then take a second, closer shot of a detail you can see on the building.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to set focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is bright, so the building keeps its detail and colour.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people in the frame. On bright days around midday, expect harder shadows on the walls.

3Segontium

Segontium is the Roman fort on the edge of Caernarfon, and the most important one in the north of Roman Wales. It was founded by Agricola in AD 77 or 78 and built to hold about a thousand auxiliary soldiers. Unlike the medieval castle down by the water, it stands on higher ground to the east, with a good outlook towards the Menai Strait. Today you see low stone foundations laid out on the hillside, with Snowdonia behind. Most of the foundations survive, although a main road now cuts through the site. Coins found here suggest the fort was still in use until at least the year 394.

From the top, the phone picture is a wide panorama over Caernarfon's rooftops and the castle, with the Menai Strait, Anglesey and the Snowdonia hills beyond. It is a view of the setting rather than of the fort itself, so treat it as the scene-setting shot. The fort lies to the south-east, so use this photo to show where Segontium sits in the landscape. For a closer picture of the foundations with the mountains behind, go to the fort itself. It is a short walk away, but check the official site before you go, as the gate has been found locked on at least one visit. Ben Twthill can be windy, so keep a firm grip on your phone.

Where it is: Segontium is on the outskirts of Caernarfon, on higher ground east of the castle and the Seiont estuary.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the rooftops, the Strait and the mountains. Then switch to the default lens (1x), or the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the castle or a patch of hillside.

Where to stand: Stand in Ben Twthill (Top of the hill, reached via the footbridge over the main road and the path opposite St David and St Helen's Church). A panoramic view over Caernarfon's rooftops and castle, the Menai Strait and Anglesey, with Snowdonia in the distance. The sources describe the town and castle rather than the Roman fort, so this is a context view, not a view of Segontium itself. From here you see Caernarfon's rooftops and castle, the Menai Strait and Anglesey, with Snowdonia in the distance. Segontium is off to the south-east and is not the main subject of this view.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the horizon on the upper or lower third line. Keep the town and castle in the foreground, with the mountains as a backdrop. Try panorama mode, moving slowly from left to right. If you also visit the fort itself, frame the excavated stone foundations low in the picture with the Snowdonia mountains behind.

Settings tip: Tap on the castle or town to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour. In wind, hold the phone with both hands and lean against something steady, and check the photo for blur.

Best time: Softer light early or late in the day usually flatters the town and hills. Clear days show Snowdonia best.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Caernarfon?

The first three on this map are Caernarfon Castle, Fusiliers Museum and Segontium Roman Fort. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 5.

Where should I stand to photograph Caernarfon Castle?

Stand about 20 m north-east of Caernarfon Castle, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the full length of the walls and towers.

When is the best time to photograph Fusiliers Museum?

Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people in the frame. On bright days around midday, expect harder shadows on the walls.

How do I get to Segontium Roman Fort?

There is no station or tram stop near here, so from Caernarfon town centre you reach it on foot.
