This map shows 5 of the most-searched sights in Tahiti as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Teahupo'o, Mont Orohena, Point Venus, Marae Arahurahu and Papeete Cathedral lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Teahupo'o

Teahupo'o is a small village on Tahiti's southeastern coast, home to roughly 1,500 people and served by a single one-lane road. It is known worldwide for the heavy surf that breaks off its shore. The wave is so hollow because it breaks over an extremely shallow coral reef, in places only about 51 cm (20 inches) below the surface. The village hosted the surfing at the 2024 Summer Olympics, which makes it a long way from Paris. For a phone photo, the most dependable picture is not of the wave itself. It is the village sign at the entrance, near Teahupo'o Beach, with the coastline and green hillsides behind it. Guides describe it as one of the most photographed spots in the village, and it makes a good arrival shot at the end of the road. Be realistic about the surf: from land the waves can look small, and the close-up wave pictures you see online are usually taken from a boat in the channel. If you want boats and surf in the frame, the landing at Debarcadère Teahupoo shows boats heading out through the channel, with the break and the mountains beyond the lagoon. Keep to the shore and never walk on the reef. Boat trips are paid, and they can be stopped on big-swell days, so ask locally.

Where it is: Teahupo'o is a village on the southeastern coast of Tahiti, at the end of the coast road, with the lagoon and reef in front and steep green hills behind.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the sign with the coastline behind it. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the hillsides in the frame. At the boat landing, use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull in the boats and the surf beyond the lagoon.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Stand at the Teahupo'o Wave Sign, at the village entrance near Teahupo'o Beach. From here the sign sits in the foreground, with the coastline and lush hillsides behind it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the sign on one side, leaving the coast and hills to fill the rest of the frame. Crouch slightly so the sign stands out against the scenery. For a second picture, go to Debarcadère Teahupoo and frame the channel with a boat in the middle, using the mountain backdrop above it.

Settings tip: Tap the sign to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright, so the sign and hillsides keep their colour. Try a Live Photo at the landing to catch boats moving. Keep your phone strap or wrist loop on near the water.

Best time: Surf season runs roughly May to October, and the wave is best seen at high tide with offshore winds. If surf is your goal, plan around those conditions. For the sign photo, morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light.

2Mount Orohena

Mount Orohena is the highest point on Tahiti and in all of French Polynesia, at 2,241 metres (7,352 ft). It is an extinct shield volcano, and it ranks 7th in the world for topographic isolation. In plain terms, it stands well apart from any other high ground, which is why the view from the top reaches so far.

This is not a casual stop. The summit is a demanding two- to three-day trek, and hiking guides advise doing it with a guide. The reward is a 360-degree panorama over the interior valleys, the coast, Mo'orea and the Pacific, on a clear day. The usual place for photos is the summit outcrop, and it is worth the effort because you can turn on the spot and capture the whole island and the ocean around it.

For your phone, the big scenes are the point. Take a wide shot of the valleys, then a closer one of Mo'orea on the horizon. Cloud is the main risk, so be ready to wait a few minutes for a gap and keep your phone handy when it opens.

If the trek is not for you, Pointe Vénus on the northern coast shows Mount Orohena rising inland, framed by the bay and the lighthouse area. It is the easy, free option, but the mountain is often hidden by cloud, so check the sky before you go.

Where it is: Mount Orohena stands in the high interior of Tahiti, the island's highest point.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the full sweep of valleys and sea, then use the default lens (1x) for general shots. Switch to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out Mo'orea and the coastline.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Stand on Mont Orohena, at the summit outcrop. From here the photo shows a 360-degree panorama over the interior valleys, the coast, Mo'orea and the Pacific, on a clear day.

Framing: Turn slowly and take one wide shot in each direction rather than trying to fit everything in one frame. Use panorama mode for the sweep of the horizon. Turn on gridlines to keep the horizon level, and include a bit of the rock outcrop in the foreground to give a sense of height. For an alternative, Pointe Vénus shows the mountain rising inland across the bay.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on the far land, then drag the exposure slider down a little so bright cloud and sea keep their detail. Take a few frames in case cloud moves across, and keep your phone battery topped up for the long trek.

Best time: Early morning, when clouds are usually lower. One guide suggests May to September for clearer skies.

3Point Venus

Point Venus is a palm-fringed headland on the north coast of Tahiti, at the north-eastern end of Matavai Bay. It is best known for its white-and-red lighthouse, which stands behind a beach of dark volcanic sand. For photographs, that dark sand, the green palms and the turquoise water behind or beside the lighthouse give you strong colour contrast in a single frame, and the lighthouse gives the picture a clear focal point. The headland also has real history. Captain James Cook anchored in Matavai Bay on 12 April 1769 to observe the Transit of Venus, and his party set up an observatory and a fortified camp here, which is how the place got its name. The lighthouse is no longer staffed but is looked after, and its open grounds are free to enter. The classic picture is taken from the beach in front of the lighthouse, looking straight at the tower, with palms framing the sides. Go early if you can: the cruise-ship day visitors arrive later, and the beach is quieter and the light softer in the morning. Golden hour is another popular time. For a second view, walk out along the headland and photograph back towards the lighthouse, with the beach and water in the frame. There is no marked spot for this, so look around when you arrive.

Where it is: Point Venus is on the north coast of Tahiti, in the commune of Mahina, about 8 km east of Pape'ete. It sits at the north-eastern end of Matavai Bay.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the classic view of the lighthouse with palms on either side. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the sand and sea, and keep the horizon level. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the tower from the beach or the headland.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (beach in front of the lighthouse, facing the tower). The white-and-red lighthouse framed by palms and black volcanic sand, with the turquoise water of Matavai Bay behind it. On the beach in front of the lighthouse, and face east towards the tower. From here the white-and-red lighthouse sits among palms and black volcanic sand, with turquoise water behind or beside it. For an alternative, walk out along the headland (Pointe Vénus) and look back across to the lighthouse, with the beach and water in the frame.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the lighthouse on one of the vertical lines, with a palm or two in the foreground to frame it. Keep the dark sand in the lower third so it leads the eye to the tower. Crouch a little for a low angle that makes the lighthouse look taller. From the headland, include a curve of the beach so the lighthouse is not alone in the frame.

Settings tip: Tap on the lighthouse to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the white tower keeps its detail and the sky and water stay rich in colour. If a shot looks too dark in the sand, nudge the slider back up. A Live Photo can catch the palms moving in the breeze.

Best time: Early morning, before the cruise-ship day visitors arrive; one guide suggests 6-7am. Golden hour is also recommended.
