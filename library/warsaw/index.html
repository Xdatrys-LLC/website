The 3 free photo spots

1Palace of Culture and Science

The Palace of Culture and Science is the tall, stepped tower that dominates central Warsaw. It was built between 1952 and 1955 to designs by the Soviet architect Lev Rudnev, who drew on American art deco skyscrapers and added Polish details, such as parapets modelled on Renaissance houses. At about 237 metres, it is still the second tallest building in Poland. It photographs well because it is so big and so symmetrical. Seen straight on from the square in front of it, the tower rises in a clean central line, with modern high-rises on either side. The details are worth a closer look too: statues of Nicolaus Copernicus and Adam Mickiewicz stand by the main entrance, and four clock faces, each 6.3 metres across, sit near the top. Since 2010 the building has been lit by LED lights that can change colour at night, so an evening shot can look quite different from a daytime one. For a phone, the easy win is the symmetrical, straight-on view from Plac Defilad. If you want a wider setting, Most Świętokrzyski gives you the palace within the skyline across the Vistula, with the river in the foreground. Park Świętokrzyski gives mid-distance shots with trees and benches around the tower. Keep the phone level so the tower does not lean, and check the square for temporary fencing or stage works before you settle on a spot.

Where it is: In the middle of Warsaw, on and around Plac Defilad, with the main facade facing the square. It is surrounded by modern high-rises.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) from where you stand, as the tower is very tall and you will be very close. Use the default lens (1x) if you step further back, and telephoto (2x-5x) for the clock faces, the statues or the spire.

Where to stand: Stand in Plac Defilad (in front of the main facade, centred on the tower). The whole tower framed straight on, with modern high-rises on either side, ideal for a symmetrical shot. You will be very close, so use the ultra-wide lens and expect to tilt the phone upward. For the classic whole-tower, straight-on view with modern high-rises on either side, Plac Defilad in front of the main facade is the recommended general viewpoint.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and line the tower's central spire up with the middle vertical line for a symmetrical shot. Standing close and shooting upward with the ultra-wide gives a dramatic, towering look, but keep the phone as upright as you can, as tilting it back makes the sides lean inwards. For a different view, try Most Świętokrzyski, which shows the palace within the skyline with the river in front.

Settings tip: Tap on the tower to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky stays blue and the building does not wash out. At night, use night mode and hold the phone steady against a railing or your own body.

Best time: Blue hour or sunset, when the building is lit against the sky.

2Warsaw Uprising Museum

The Warsaw Uprising Museum sits in the Wola district and tells the story of the 1944 uprising. The building is a converted brick industrial power station. The facade and entrance on Grzybowska 79 are the easiest place to take a clean picture, with the Wola streetscape and some nearby towers around it. The museum was set up as an institution in 1983 and opened on 31 July 2004, the 60th anniversary of the uprising. Inside there are exhibits over several floors, and a hall with a full-size B-24 Liberator. Outside, the museum's grounds (Freedom Park) have a memorial wall of names, a replica of the Kubuś armoured car and more than 30 colourised photo posters, which make good foregrounds or backgrounds if you have time to walk around. For a phone photo, start at the entrance and take one wide shot of the whole facade, then move closer for details such as the brickwork and the doorway. Keep the entrance clear so other visitors can get in and out. Photography rules inside can vary by exhibition, so follow the staff signs, and check the official site before you go for anything about tickets or access.

Where it is: In the Wola district of Warsaw, on Grzybowska 79, in a former tram power station among modern towers and city streets.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole brick facade and the entrance in one frame, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural look. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for brick texture and doorway details.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. For the facade shot, walk to the street-facing entrance on Grzybowska 79. From there the photo shows the former tram power station's brick facade and entrance, set against the Wola streetscape and nearby towers.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the facade with the vertical lines so the walls don't lean. Keep the entrance in the lower part of the frame and let the sky and towers sit above. If you want a second view, Freedom Park, the museum's outdoor grounds, gives you the memorial wall of names, the replica armoured car and the colourised posters in the background.

Settings tip: Tap on the brickwork to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the brick keeps its texture. Hold the phone steady and keep the entrance clear of other visitors.

Best time: Check the official site for current opening times.

3Royal Castle

The Royal Castle stands on Castle Square at the entrance to Warsaw's Old Town. It was a royal residence and seat of government, and today it is a state museum. It began in the 14th century as a home for the Dukes of Masovia. Under Sigismund III Vasa in the early 1600s it was enlarged into the Mannerist-Baroque building you see now. The castle was badly damaged in 1939 and deliberately blown up by German forces in September 1944. After a decision in 1971 it was rebuilt from surviving fragments, documents and rescued artworks, and the interiors reopened to the public in 1984.

It photographs well because of the bright red front and the open square in front of it. The classic picture is the red castle facade with Sigismund's Column in the foreground and Old Town rooftops behind. Stand on the square's edge near Krakowskie Przedmieście, or on the Old Town side. The square is free to enter, so you can take your time and try a few angles.

For a change, Świętojańska street gives a view of cobblestones leading to the red facade, with the square opening up beyond. The castle gardens are a quieter, greener alternative with plants in the frame. Whichever spot you pick, keep the phone level so the long building doesn't lean.

Where it is: In the historic centre of Warsaw, on Castle Square (Plac Zamkowy) at the entrance to the Old Town. The castle is part of the UNESCO-listed Historic Centre of Warsaw.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the castle, the column and the sky into one frame. Use the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view. Try telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the facade or the rooftops behind.

Where to stand: Stand in Plac Zamkowy (edge near Krakowskie Przedmieście, or the Old Town side of the square). The red castle facade framed with Sigismund's Column in the foreground, with the Old Town rooftops in the background. From here the red castle facade is framed by Sigismund's Column in the foreground, with the Old Town rooftops behind. The castle lies to the east.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and place the column on one third of the frame, with the red facade filling the rest. Keep the phone upright and level so the building doesn't lean. For a different view, try Świętojańska street for the cobblestone lead-in to the facade, or the castle gardens for a greener, quieter picture.

Settings tip: Tap on the red facade to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour. After dark, use night mode and hold the phone against a wall or railing to keep it steady.

Best time: Blue hour is the time for the illuminated castle. Dawn gives an emptier square.
