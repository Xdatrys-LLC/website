This map shows 15 of the most-searched sights in Nairobi as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Sheldrick Elephants, Nairobi National Park, Giraffe Centre, KICC and Karen Blixen Museum lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1David Sheldrick Wildlife Trust

The Sheldrick Wildlife Trust is an elephant orphanage and wildlife rehabilitation project inside Nairobi National Park. Dame Daphne Sheldrick founded it in 1977 to honour her late husband, David, a naturalist and the founding warden of Tsavo East National Park. It was known as the David Sheldrick Wildlife Trust until it changed its name in February 2019 to honour both of them. Orphaned calves are rescued from across Kenya and brought here to be raised, and later returned to the wild.

It photographs well because the animals come to you. Keepers bring the young elephants up to a rope line, so a phone can get sharp close-ups without any long zoom. Expect calves rolling in the mud and orphans walking into the viewing area, with keepers leading them to the rope, which gives plenty of lively moments. The reddish mud and the keepers' work clothes add colour to the frame.

The public can visit for one hour each day, so the window is short. Arrive early, stay behind the rope and keep your phone ready. Switch the flash off and look after your battery, because you will want to shoot for the whole hour. If you want a second angle, try the spot where the calves play in the mud and the keepers lead them to the rope. Entry is likely to be ticketed and booking may be needed, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: The Trust's elephant orphanage is inside Nairobi National Park, in Nairobi, Kenya.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for wide shots of keepers, calves and the rope line together. Switch to telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for close-up portraits of the elephants as they arrive. The ultra-wide (0.5x) is only worth trying if the group comes very close.

Where to stand: Stand about 170 m south-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-east. Once in the viewing area, stand on the left-hand side as you walk in. One guide says the elephants usually make their entrance on this side, so you can photograph the orphans approaching, framed by the rope and the keepers.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep an elephant on one third of the frame, with a keeper or the rope line giving context. Shoot the calves as they walk in, then step back to the default lens (1x) for the whole scene. For a different view, take a shot of the calves playing in the mud and being led up to the rope line.

Settings tip: Turn the flash off, as guides advise none here. Tap the elephant to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so dark skin and bright sky keep their detail. Try Live Photos or motion mode to catch calves rolling and splashing. A tripod is not needed with a phone.

Best time: The public visit is only about an hour a day, so arrive early to get a good place at the rope and be ready when the elephants enter.

2Nairobi National Park

Nairobi National Park is a wildlife reserve on the edge of a capital city, which is the main reason it is such a good place for photographs. Open grass plain, scattered acacia bushes and, on a clear day, wide sky make easy backdrops for animal pictures. It opened in 1946 and was the first national park in Kenya. It covers about 117 square kilometres and lies roughly 7 km from the centre of Nairobi. The park is fenced on three sides, but the southern edge along the Mbagathi River is open, so wildlife can move between the park and the plains beyond. It is known as one of Kenya's most successful rhino sanctuaries, and the list of animals includes lion, giraffe, zebra, buffalo and hippo. Up to 500 bird species have been recorded here too. Wildlife gathers in the park in the dry season, when the small dams along the river hold water, so that is when you are most likely to see animals. The park is a large area, so treat this spot as a starting place rather than a single view. Animals are usually far away, so use the zoom, and keep a little of the open landscape in the frame to show where you were. Always follow park staff instructions and never leave your vehicle to get closer to an animal.

Where it is: In the south of Nairobi, about 7 km from the city centre, where the city meets open grassland. The park's southern boundary is the Mbagathi River.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for animals, since they are often at a distance. Switch to the default lens (1x) for plains with trees, and the ultra-wide (0.5x) for big sky and wide landscapes.

Where to stand: Stand on Impala Viewpoint (hilltop near the Main Gate, on the highest point). A wide view across the central plains with impalas, zebras and gazelles grazing below and the Nairobi skyline in the distance. From here you look across towards the open park area.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on the lower or upper line, so the plain or the sky fills the frame. Use an acacia or a group of animals as your main subject, and keep some open grass around it to show the scale. When you zoom in, hold the phone with both hands and take several shots, because animals move.

Settings tip: Tap the animal on the screen to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright. For a moving animal, try a Live Photo or take a quick burst, then pick the sharpest frame later.

Best time: The dry season is when wildlife is most concentrated in the park. Within a day, early morning and late afternoon usually give softer light and calmer animals.

3Giraffe Centre

The Giraffe Centre is a wildlife conservation centre in Lang'ata, on the edge of Nairobi, set up to protect the Rothschild's giraffe, a species found only in the grasslands of East Africa. It began in 1979, when Jock Leslie Melville and his wife Betty started a breeding programme on their Lang'ata property, and it opened to visitors by 1983. Its best-known experience is feeding giraffes from a raised platform. Warthogs also wander freely among the giraffes, so keep an eye out for them in the background.

It photographs well because the animals are so tall and so close. From the phone icon on the map, in the lower feeding area of the garden, you can take full-length giraffe portraits with their necks stretching up against the greenery. This is a different angle from the high platform, which gives eye-level shots with a giraffe's head near your phone, often as it takes a pellet. Both are worth trying if you have time. For something more natural, the Gogo River Nature Trail is an alternative, showing giraffes browsing in woodland rather than at a feeding rail.

Step back to fit a whole giraffe in the frame, and let the greenery fill the space around it. Keep your phone strap round your wrist when you are near the animals. The centre is a paid site and the layout of the viewing areas may have changed, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: The Giraffe Centre is in Lang'ata, about 20 kilometres from the centre of Nairobi, on the city's outskirts.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the telephoto lens (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for full-body giraffe portraits without getting in the animals' way. Use the default lens (1x) to include the garden around them. The ultra-wide (0.5x) works only for a very close head-and-neck shot, and it can stretch the giraffe's shape.

Where to stand: Stand about 180 m west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face east. The lower garden is where you can fit a whole giraffe in the frame, with its neck stretching up against the greenery.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the giraffe's body on one line, leaving room above its head so the neck does not look cut off. Shoot upwards a little to show the full height against the leaves. If you have time, take a second set from the raised platform for close, eye-level portraits. The Gogo River Nature Trail is a different option, with giraffes in a natural setting.

Settings tip: Tap on the giraffe's face to set focus and drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the animal does not turn into a silhouette. Portrait mode works well when a giraffe is close. Live Photos can catch the moment when one takes a pellet.

Best time: Morning, when the light is soft and avoids the harsh midday sun.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Nairobi?

The first three on this map are Sheldrick Elephants, Nairobi National Park and Giraffe Centre. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 15.

Where should I stand to photograph Sheldrick Elephants?

Stand about 170 m south-west of Sheldrick Elephants, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-east. Use the default lens (1x) for wide shots of keepers, calves and the rope line together.

When is the best time to photograph Nairobi National Park?

The dry season is when wildlife is most concentrated in the park. Within a day, early morning and late afternoon usually give softer light and calmer animals.

How do I get to the Giraffe Centre?

No rail or tram stops are suitable here, so check current road transport options to Lang'ata, about 20 kilometres from the centre of Nairobi, before you go.
