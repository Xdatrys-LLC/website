The 3 free photo spots

1Port Elizabeth waterfront

Port Elizabeth is a town on Bequia, one of the Grenadines islands, and its waterfront is the natural place to start taking pictures. The town is the capital of Grenadines Parish. It was named in 1937 after Princess Elizabeth, later Queen Elizabeth II, to mark the coronation of her parents. That royal link gives you a nice small story to put in a caption alongside your photos.

The waterfront works well for a phone because the scene has clear layers: water and any boats in the foreground, the shoreline and buildings in the middle, and the island's hills and sky behind. You do not need to hunt for a special angle. Stand back a little, find a line such as the water's edge or a path, and let it lead the eye into the frame.

For a wide view, use the ultra-wide lens (0.5x) and keep the horizon level with the gridlines switched on. For a closer, tidier picture, switch to the default lens (1x) and pick out one boat, one colourful building or one person walking along the front. If you want details such as ropes, signs or boat names, the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) lets you take them from a distance without crowding anyone.

Tap the screen on the brightest part of the scene to set focus, then pull the exposure slider down a touch so the sky keeps its colour. Wipe the lens first, as sea air can leave a haze on it.

Where it is: On the shoreline of the town of Port Elizabeth, on the island of Bequia in the Grenadines. The town is the capital of Grenadines Parish.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the main view. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for a sweeping shot of the whole front, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as boats or signs.

Where to stand: Stand about 190 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. Walk the few minutes up to the water's edge if you want to be closer.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower third line, keeping it level. Include something in the foreground, such as a boat, a rail or a path, to give the picture depth. Try one wide frame and one closer frame of a single subject.

Settings tip: Tap on the brightest part of the scene to focus, then slide the exposure down slightly so the sky does not wash out. Try a Live Photo if boats or people are moving, and choose the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and gentler colours than the middle of the day.

2Princess Margaret Beach

Princess Margaret Beach is a stretch of shoreline in Port Elizabeth, and it is the second stop on this map. It suits a phone camera well because the scene is simple: shoreline, water and sky arranged in clean horizontal bands. That makes it easy to compose a picture that reads clearly even on a small screen.

Start with a wide view from where you stand, then walk south towards the beach for a closer angle. Look for something in the foreground, such as a boat or a curve of the shoreline, to lead the eye towards the water.

Keep the horizon level by switching on the gridlines. Tap the brightest part of the sky to set exposure, then use the exposure slider to bring the sky down slightly so the colours of the sea hold up. Bright water and sky can fool the phone into making the picture too dark or too bright, so check the preview and adjust it a little if needed.

A few people in the frame give a sense of scale. Keep your phone strap on near the water, and wipe the lens with a dry cloth before shooting, as sea air leaves a faint film that softens pictures.

Where it is: Princess Margaret Beach is on the shoreline at Port Elizabeth, a short walk from the point on the map where you stand to take the photo.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the sweep of shoreline and sky, then try the default lens (1x) for a more natural view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a boat or a detail on the water.

Where to stand: Stand about 100 m north of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower third line, depending on whether the sky or the water is more interesting. Keep the horizon straight, and include a foreground object to add depth.

Settings tip: Tap the sea or sky to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky looks washed out. Try a Live Photo to catch moving water, and use panorama for a wide sweep of the shore, moving slowly and keeping the phone level.

Best time: Early morning or the hour before sunset usually gives softer light and more colour than the middle of the day. Midday sun can make the water glare, so use the exposure slider.

3Moonhole

Moonhole is a small, unusual community at the western end of Bequia, in the Grenadines. It takes its name from a huge natural arch in the volcanic rock, through which the setting moon can sometimes be seen. Thomas and Gladys Johnston, who had retired from advertising in New York, founded it in the 1960s. They built open-sided stone houses with the help of masons from nearby Paget Farm, using whalebones, native hardwoods and objects recovered from the sea. The land is now a private nature preserve of roughly 30 acres, looked after by a conservation trust.

For a phone photographer, the appeal is the contrast between the rugged arch, the steep coastal hillside and the hand-built houses that seem to grow out of the rock. Look for layers: sea in the foreground, the arch or rocky shore in the middle, and the green slope behind. Respect the privacy of the community and shoot from where you are allowed to be, and treat the houses as people's homes, so do not point your phone into private windows or gardens.

Where it is: Moonhole sits on the narrow western tip of the island of Bequia, in the Grenadines, on a steep coastal slope close to the sea. The village of Paget Farm is nearby.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to show the setting, then switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the arch and the shoreline closer. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are close enough to the rocks to want a dramatic sweep.

Where to stand: Stand about 290 m north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east. Because of the distance, treat the arch as a distant feature in a wider coastal view rather than a close-up.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the shoreline on a lower line, with the arch or rock face on a third. Include some sea or foliage in the foreground to give a sense of scale. Zoom to 3x or 5x for a tighter shot of the arch if your phone has it.

Settings tip: Tap the arch to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky and sea keep their colour and the dark rock does not wash out. Hold the phone with both hands and rest your elbows against your body for steadier zoomed shots.

Best time: The moon can sometimes be seen through the arch as it sets, so a clear evening or night with a moon is the special-occasion option; try night mode and hold very still. For ordinary daytime photos, the softer light of early morning or late afternoon usually flatters the rock and the sea.
