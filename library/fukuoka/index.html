The 3 free photo spots

1Hakata Station

Hakata Station is the largest and busiest railway terminal in Kyushu, and the place where the Shinkansen from Osaka finally stops. For a phone photographer, the building works best as a bold city-gateway scene rather than a single tidy facade. It has big glass and stone shapes, glowing lights in the evening, and a constant flow of people and trains.

The station was rebuilt in 2011. The JR Hakata City complex opened on 3 March 2011, and the Kyushu Shinkansen services began nine days later. The very first station here opened in 1889, about 600 m north of where it stands today. In the last full fiscal year, an average of 125,462 people boarded here each day, so expect a lively scene.

For a calmer and more interesting view, head up to the rooftop garden 東7番街屋上庭園 in the station area. From there you look down on the station district at a slightly different angle. The illuminated, Hakata-coloured wall of the building is visible from above, and the height lets you compress the city streets into a layered picture. If you have time, the rooftop square つばめの杜ひろば gives a wider panorama, with trains arriving and leaving below.

Keep your phone steady against a railing, avoid including other people's faces in close-up, and take a few frames at different zoom levels before you move on.

Where it is: Hakata Station is in Hakata-ku, Fukuoka, and is the city's main rail hub. The rooftop garden is a nearby elevated viewpoint that may be on Amu Plaza's 11th floor, so check on the day.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the telephoto lens (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to compress the station district and pick out the lit wall. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a wider view of the streets, and keep the ultra-wide (0.5x) for close-up details, since it can make the buildings lean and look small.

Where to stand: Stand on 東7番街屋上庭園 (rooftop garden, Hakata Station area). An elevated, slightly different angle over the station area, with the illuminated Hakata-coloured wall of the building visible from above, suited to telephoto compression of the city. From a raised position like the garden you look down on the station area, with the illuminated Hakata-coloured wall of the building visible from above. It is a quieter, slightly different elevated angle than the main terrace.

Framing: Zoom in so the lit wall and the surrounding rooftops fill the frame, layering the city behind it. Turn on the gridlines and line the horizon of rooftops up with the top third of the picture. If you want a wider panorama with trains below, the rooftop square つばめの杜ひろば is a good second viewpoint.

Settings tip: Tap the wall or building you want sharp, then slide the exposure down slightly so the lights do not burn out. Rest your elbows on a railing to keep the telephoto frame steady, and switch on night mode after dark.

Best time: Late afternoon into the evening, when the building's lighting is on and the sky still has some colour, should give the most atmospheric results.

2Canal City Hakata

Canal City Hakata is a large shopping and entertainment complex in Fukuoka, often called "the city within the city". Shops, cafes, restaurants, cinemas, a theatre, a game centre and two hotels sit around a canal that runs right through the middle. It photographs well because the buildings are anything but boxy. The Jerde Partnership designed it in a fanciful style, with curving sculptures and fountains, and the surrounding city is hardly visible from inside. That gives your pictures a self-contained, almost stage-set feel.

Some facts to put it in context. It opened on 20 April 1996 and cost about $1.4 billion, making it the largest private development in Japan's history. For its 20th anniversary in 2016 it added the "Aqua Panorama" fountain and staged Japan's largest 3D projection mapping. In 2001 the Ramen Stadium arrived, with several of Fukuoka's well-known ramen restaurants.

For photos, think in layers: the curved facades, the water of the canal, and the people walking past give you depth and scale. Keep to the open walkways and avoid blocking the flow of shoppers. If a fountain display is running, a short video or a Live Photo will capture it better than a still. Display times change, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: Canal City Hakata is in the Hakata area of Fukuoka, next to the city's entertainment district and between its commercial and retail core.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole scene, then try the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit in the sweep of the curving buildings and the canal. If your phone has a telephoto (2x-5x), use it to pick out sculptures, signs and fountain details.

Where to stand: Stand about 160 m south of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. It is roughly 3 minutes on foot from there to the complex itself.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and line up the canal or a curved edge of the building along one of the lines, so the shape leads the eye into the picture. Include a few people for scale. Then move closer and shoot upwards with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to exaggerate the curves.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on the brightest part of the building, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so lights and screens do not wash out. If a fountain show is running, try a Live Photo or a short video. After dark, let night mode work and hold the phone steady against a rail or wall.

Best time: Late afternoon into the evening, when the lighting comes on, tends to suit the colourful design and the water. Daytime is better for clean views of the shapes without crowds of lights competing.

3Ōhori Park

Ōhori Park is a big city park built around a lake, and it is one of the easiest places in Fukuoka to take a relaxed, good-looking phone photo. The name means "large moat": the lake began as part of the moat for Fukuoka Castle, made by the lord Kuroda Nagamasa from the northern half of an old inlet. The present park was laid out by Fukuoka City, modelled on the West Lake of China, and opened in 1929. A fireworks festival is held here every August.

The picture most visitors come for is the red-and-green pavilion, 浮見堂 (Ukimido), standing over the water on the central island. Guides call it the park's symbol. It works well because the bright pavilion is reflected in the lake, with trees and the city skyline behind it. You reach it over the bridges, so the walk to the viewpoint is part of the fun.

If you want a second angle, the lake and the pavilion can also be seen from the bridge 観月橋. At dusk the water turns blue and the park lights come on, which gives a calmer, moodier shot. The path around the lake is busy with walkers, joggers and dogs, so keep an eye out when you are composing a photo. The Fukuoka Art Museum is close by if you want to combine the two.

Where it is: Ōhori Park is in Chūō-ku, in central Fukuoka, on the site of a former cove that opened onto Hakata Bay. The Fukuoka Art Museum is nearby.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the pavilion and its reflection. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the whole lake and sky from the bridges. Use 2x if you want to pick out the pavilion from across the water.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (on the central island, in front of the pavilion, approached over the bridges). The red-and-green pavilion standing over the lake, with its reflection on the water and the city and trees behind it. The best view for this spot is in front of 浮見堂 (Ukimido), on the central island, which you reach over the bridges. From there the photo shows the red-and-green pavilion standing over the lake, with its reflection in the water and the city and trees behind it.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the horizon on a line. Keep the pavilion off-centre and let its reflection fill the lower part of the frame. Hold the phone low and close to the water for a stronger reflection. For an alternative view, try the bridge 観月橋, which looks across the lake to the pavilion, but check it on the ground. Wait a moment for walkers and joggers to pass before you shoot.

Settings tip: Tap the pavilion to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the red stays rich and the sky does not wash out. In the evening, use night mode and hold the phone steady against a railing, as the lights come on and the water turns blue.

Best time: Morning, when the water is calmest, according to one guide. Dusk is another option, when the lake turns blue and the park lights come on.
