The 3 free photo spots

1Haraldshaugen

Haraldshaugen is Norway's national monument to King Harald Fairhair, set on a grassy mound in the northern suburbs of Haugesund. A 17 m granite obelisk stands on top, with four bronze panels around its base showing scenes from the king's life. The mound is ringed by memorial stones, including 29 smaller ones, one for each of the historic counties of Norway. It was unveiled on 18 July 1872, marking a thousand years since the Battle of Hafrsfjord, and the architect was Christian Christie. Tradition says Harald is buried here, but there is no clear archaeological proof.

For a phone photo, the best idea is to start with the old stone cross on the small hill, Krosshaugen, just south of the monument. It is roughly 1,000 years old, and it gives you something close and characterful for the foreground, with the obelisk behind. The wider view over the area includes houses and industry, so the cross and the monument together usually make a stronger picture than a general panorama. Once you have that shot, walk up to the mound for the obelisk itself against the sound and the coast.

Where it is: Haraldshaugen is in the northern suburbs of Haugesund, on the coast about 2 km north of the town centre. Krosshaugen is the small hill just south of the monument.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for the cross with the monument behind it. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the whole mound and obelisk, and to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the obelisk closer.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (south of the national monument, on the small hill beside it). The roughly 1000-year-old stone cross in the foreground, with the monument and the town in the background. From here the old stone cross is in the foreground, and the obelisk rises behind it to the north.

Framing: Put the cross on one side of the frame and let the obelisk rise behind it, using the gridlines to keep the upright lines straight. Crouching a little lowers the viewpoint and makes the cross stand out more against the sky. For a second picture, walk up to Haraldshaugen itself and frame the 17 m obelisk against the sound and the coast.

Settings tip: Tap the cross to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the sky keeps its colour and the grey stone does not turn flat. If the sky is bright, tap on the stone, then check the sky has not washed out.

Best time: No particular time is needed. Low sun early or late in the day tends to bring out the carving on the stone and the texture of the granite.

2Haugesund Art Gallery

Haugesund Art Gallery (Haugesund Billedgalleri) sits in the north part of the city, looking over the city park and rose garden. It is a good stop if you like buildings that have grown over time. The museum was built in three stages. The oldest part is a house from around 1915, built as a home for Lars and Inga Meling. The main building followed in 1978, designed by the architect David Sandved, and a further extension was added in 2004. Together they cover about 2,210 square metres, with around 1,000 square metres given over to exhibitions. For a photo, that mix of older house and later additions gives you something to look at beyond a single façade. The green setting of the park and rose garden gives you a natural backdrop, and in the growing season you may find flowers to use in the foreground. The gallery's roots go back to the Haugesund Art Society, founded in 1913, and the museum became a municipal institution in 1973. Its collection includes Norwegian and international names such as Edvard Munch, Pablo Picasso and Lars Hertervig, with a strong showing of Western Norway artists. You do not need to go inside to get a good picture. Stay outside, keep your phone level, and take one wide view of the whole group of buildings and one closer shot where the different stages meet.

Where it is: In the north part of Haugesund, with a view of the city park and rose garden.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole group of buildings in, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a more natural view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as where the old house meets the later extensions.

Where to stand: Stand about 45 m south-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the vertical edges of the walls with them so the buildings do not lean. Try to include some of the park greenery at the edge of the frame. Then take a second shot using the telephoto to pick out the contrast between the older and newer parts.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the walls keep their detail.

Best time: Daytime, when you can see the whole outside of the building clearly. A bright but cloudy day gives soft, even light on the walls.

6Skåre Church

Skåre Church is a white wooden church with an octagonal plan, built in 1858 to designs by the architect Christian Heinrich Grosch. It seats about 450 people. The site is much older than the building. Written records go back to 1301, and the first church here was a stave church that stood across the street from the present one. The church was built as the main church for the new town of Haugesund and its surroundings.

The view suggested here is a quieter one. From Olav Kyrres gate you look west along the street and see the church's east wall in the background, with the building at Erling Skjalgssons gate 15 on the right edge of the frame. You won't get the main facade from here. What you get is a plain white wall, a street scene and a sense of how the church sits among ordinary town buildings. That suits a phone well, because the white wall gives clean, simple shapes and the street leads the eye towards it.

The church is only a short stroll away, so it is easy to take a second shot from another side. Haugevegen gives a view from the east, with the villa at Haugevegen 17 on the left and the white Hauge skole building on the right. You can also walk the church grounds for a wider view of the whole octagonal church. Check on site that trees, parked cars or fences are not blocking the view.

Where it is: Skåre Church stands in the town of Haugesund, with Olav Kyrres gate on its east side and Haugevegen nearby.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x), which shows the church wall with some street around it. If the street is narrow or you want more of the building and sky, switch to ultra-wide (0.5x). Use telephoto (2x or more) for a tighter shot of the roofline and wall.

Where to stand: Stand on Olav Kyrres gate (looking west toward the church's east wall). The church's eastern wall in the background, with Erling Skjalgssons gate 15 on the right edge of the frame. The church's east wall is in the background, with Erling Skjalgssons gate 15 on the right edge of the frame. The church is to the west of you.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and use them to keep the walls and street edges straight. Let the street lead into the picture, with the white wall as the main subject and the building at number 15 as a frame on the right. If you want a different angle, Haugevegen gives a view of the church from the east, with the villa on the left and Hauge skole on the right.

Settings tip: Tap the white wall to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so it stays white and keeps its detail rather than turning pale grey or washed out. Hold the phone level to avoid leaning walls.

Best time: Morning is likely to light the east wall, which faces you. In the late afternoon the sun is low in the west and behind the church, so the wall may look dark. Even, cloudy light also suits a white wall.
