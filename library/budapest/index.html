The 3 free photo spots

1Hungarian Parliament Building

The Hungarian Parliament is the building most people picture when they think of Budapest: a long, pale neo-Gothic riverfront topped by a central dome. Imre Steindl designed it, and it opened in 1902. It is about 268 m long and 96 m high, and it is still the largest building in Hungary. The height is a nod to the country's 1896 millennium celebrations. The facade is almost perfectly symmetrical, and it carries 242 sculptures inside and out, many of them statues of rulers and military figures. That symmetry is the reason it photographs so well.

The best head-on view is from the Buda side of the Danube, on the embankment beside Batthyány tér. From here you look straight at the river facade, and after dark the lit building can reflect in the water.

If you have time for a second angle, try the Halászbástya (Fisherman's Bastion), where the Parliament appears through white arches and turrets. Another option is Margit híd (Margaret Bridge), which gives a diagonal view with city lights in the foreground. The lower terraces of the Bastion are the safer free choice, because the upper terraces and towers may charge admission. Keep your phone steady by tucking your elbows in against your body, and wait for a gap in the boat traffic if you want a calm reflection.

Where it is: The Parliament stands on Kossuth Square on the Pest bank of the Danube, in central Budapest, with its main facade facing the river. The viewpoint is directly opposite, on the Buda embankment beside Batthyány tér.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) and check how much of the facade fits from where you stand. Switch to the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the dome and towers. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of embankment and sky around the building.

Where to stand: Stand in Batthyány tér (Buda bank, riverside embankment next to the square, facing the Parliament's river facade). A straight-on, nearly symmetrical view of the Parliament's riverfront facade, which can reflect in the Danube after dark. Facing the Parliament across the Danube. You get a straight-on, nearly symmetrical view of its river facade.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the dome on the central vertical line so the two halves of the building balance. Keep the phone level so the long roofline does not slope. If you want the reflection, include some water in the lower part of the frame. For a different angle, try Halászbástya for the arched frame, or Margit híd for a diagonal view.

Settings tip: Tap the building to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the lit facade does not burn out against the dark sky. After dark, use night mode and brace your elbows against your body to keep the phone still. Boats on the river can blur the reflection, so take several shots.

Best time: Around sunset and just after, when the building's lights come on.

2Széchenyi Thermal Bath

Széchenyi Thermal Bath is the big yellow bath building in Budapest's City Park (Városliget), and its front is a free, front-on composition that suits a symmetrical shot. The front is symmetrical and Neo-Baroque, so a straight-on shot looks balanced, with the park around it and Vajdahunyad Castle opposite across the road. That mix of warm yellow stonework, trees and a castle makes the picture feel complete without much effort.

The address is Állatkerti körút 9-11. Photographing the front from the street side needs no ticket. The best-known view of the bath is the one of the three large outdoor pools, with steam rising from the water and the yellow main building behind. That view is inside the bath, so you need a bath ticket for it.

For the front, stand square to the middle of the building so the left and right halves match. Because the building is wide, the ultra-wide lens (0.5x) helps you fit it all in from the street. Keep your phone upright and level, using the gridlines, so the walls do not lean. If you go inside, keep your shots on the architecture. Photographing other bathers is frowned upon and staff may stop you. Drones are not allowed over the complex.

Where it is: The bath is in Városliget (City Park) in Budapest, at Állatkerti körút 9-11. Vajdahunyad Castle stands opposite, across the road.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole yellow front in from the street. If you can step back, try the default lens (1x) for a more natural look. On phones with a telephoto (2x-5x), use it for details such as the central part of the façade.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Stand at Széchenyi Gyógyfürdő, on the street side of the main front, facing the building with the park and Vajdahunyad Castle across the road.

Framing: Line up the centre of the building with the middle of the screen and turn on the gridlines so both sides match and the walls stay upright. Include a little park greenery for context. For a different view, the three outdoor pools with steam and the yellow building behind them are the classic shot, but that needs a bath ticket and is best kept free of other bathers.

Settings tip: Tap on the yellow wall to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a touch if the sky is bright, so the yellow does not wash out. Keep the phone level to avoid leaning walls.

Best time: Morning, when the sun lights the front of the building. The bath is not lit up at night, so evening shots will be dark.

3Buda Castle

Buda Castle is the great palace complex that stretches along the southern tip of Castle Hill, high above the Danube. The first castle was completed in 1265. The Baroque palace you see today was built between 1749 and 1769, badly damaged in the Second World War and rebuilt in a simpler Baroque style. It now houses the Hungarian National Gallery, the Budapest Historical Museum and the National Széchényi Library.

It photographs best from across the river, where the long palace roofline and dome sit on top of the hill and the Chain Bridge fills the foreground. This is the classic postcard view, and the castle is a long, pale shape that reads well even on a small screen.

A practical warning: large river cruise ships can moor along this stretch of embankment and block the view for much of the year, so glance at the dock before you settle on a spot. If the view is blocked, or you want a quieter option, try Margit híd, which lets you fit the castle, the Parliament and the water tower on Margaret Island into one frame.

Where it is: Buda Castle sits on the Buda side of the Danube, at the southern tip of Castle Hill. The Pest-side view is from the embankment just south of Széchenyi István tér, facing the Chain Bridge.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x), which fits the bridge and the castle together. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are close to the bridge and want its full length. If the castle looks small, try telephoto (2x or more) for a tighter shot of the hill.

Where to stand: Stand in Széchenyi István tér (on the Pest embankment just south of the square, where the whole bridge span lines up with the castle behind it). The full Chain Bridge in the foreground with Buda Castle rising on the hill behind it. On the Pest embankment just south of the square, and face the river. The castle rises on the hill to the south-west, across the water, with the whole Chain Bridge lined up in front of it. The aim is to keep the bridge span and the palace in the same frame.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the bridge along the lower third, with the castle roofline in the upper half. Keep the phone level so the bridge deck does not slope. If the river view is open, include a strip of water for a clean foreground. For a different angle, Margit híd frames the castle, the Parliament and the Margaret Island water tower together.

Settings tip: Tap the castle to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky does not wash out and the lit buildings keep their colour. After dark, use night mode and rest your elbows on the railing or a wall to keep the phone steady.

Best time: Sunset or night, when the castle is lit.
