The 3 free photo spots

1Nidaros Cathedral

Nidaros Cathedral is the cathedral in the city of Trondheim, and it is the top-ranked spot on this Trondheim map. It stands over the burial place of King Olav II, who died in 1030 and became Norway's patron saint. Building began in 1070 and the cathedral was substantially complete around 1300. Fires and rebuilding followed, and the major restoration that began in 1869 was officially completed in 2001. It is described as the northernmost medieval cathedral in the world, and its style mixes Romanesque and Gothic. It is still a working church that seats about 1,850 people, and Norwegian monarchs are still consecrated here.

It photographs well because of its sheer size and its many pointed, layered stonework shapes. Stand square-on, let the cathedral fill the middle of the picture, and leave a little sky above the roofline. Then step closer for a second shot of the stonework and carved detail. If people are passing, wait a moment for a clearer view. It is a place of worship, so be discreet when taking photos near visitors.

Where it is: Nidaros Cathedral is in the city of Trondheim, in Trøndelag county.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) from where you stand for the whole building. If the cathedral does not quite fit, switch to ultra-wide (0.5x), and use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for towers, windows and stone details.

Where to stand: Stand about 55 m west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face east. From here the whole building fits in one frame without you having to stand right up against the walls.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the cathedral up with the vertical lines so the walls do not lean. Keep the building central, with some sky above it. Take a wide shot first, then a telephoto shot of the stonework and details. If you move closer, hold the phone level to avoid distorted edges.

Settings tip: Tap the cathedral to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stone keeps its detail. Try a panorama only if you can hold the phone steady and nobody is walking across the scene.

Best time: Late afternoon or early evening is a good time to try, as the light is usually softer then. In the evening, use night mode and hold the phone steady against something solid.

2Archbishop's Palace

The Archbishop's Palace (Erkebispegården) is a big stone complex that for centuries was the seat and home of the Archbishop of Nidaros. It is one of the largest medieval stone buildings in Scandinavia, and its oldest walls are probably from the 13th century. That age shows in the heavy masonry, which gives you plenty of texture and character to photograph.

After the Reformation it became royal property. It was later rebuilt as a residential palace and then used more for military purposes. Today it houses several museums, is regularly used by the Church of Norway, and hosts the Olavsfestdagene festival. The Regalia of Norway have been on permanent display in the castle since 2006.

The complex is roughly 146 metres across, so it is too big to fit into one frame from close up. It works best if you pick one idea per photo: a long stretch of wall, a doorway or window set into the stone, or a wider view that shows how the buildings sit together. Because it lies just south of Nidaros Cathedral, you can also try a second shot that places the two together. Stand back a little and keep the vertical lines straight, using your phone's gridlines. Walk around a little before you settle on a view, as the shape of the buildings changes a lot from one side to the next.

Where it is: The Archbishop's Palace is in Trondheim, just south of Nidaros Cathedral.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the long stone walls and the sense of scale. Use the default lens (1x) for a natural-looking view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as stonework, windows and doorways.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m south-west of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line them up with the walls so the buildings do not lean. Choose one subject per shot, either a wide view of the long wall or a close detail of masonry. For variety, step back and include the cathedral just to the north as a second photo.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright so the sky is not washed out and the walls keep their colour. With the ultra-wide, keep the phone level to avoid stretched edges.

Best time: Soft light on an overcast day, or earlier or later in the day, usually suits stone walls better than harsh midday sun. Visit when it is quieter if you want clear views without people.

4Kristiansten Fortress

Kristiansten Fortress stands on a hill east of Trondheim city centre and was built to protect Trondheim from attack from the east. It was built after the great city fire of 1681. Work was finished in 1685, and the fortress did its job in 1718, when Swedish forces besieged the city and then withdrew. It was decommissioned in 1816. The centrepiece is the defensive tower, the Donjon, which has been a museum since 1997.

For a phone photo, the appeal is the mix of grass-topped ramparts, the pale tower and the open view over the city. The fortress also gives one of the best views of the 'strandlinja', a line along the hills of Bymarka that was once a shoreline of beaches and sea cliffs. It is seen best looking towards Byåsen. The grounds are fairly open, so you can walk round and try the tower from several sides. Use the walls and slopes as leading lines. If you want the tower and the sky together, step back along the grass rather than zooming in from close by. Keep an eye on your footing near the edges of the ramparts.

Where it is: Kristiansten Fortress stands on a hill east of Trondheim city centre, overlooking the city and the area between Ila and Lade.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the tower and ramparts together. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) for long wall lines, and use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the hills towards Byåsen.

Where to stand: Stand about 30 m north-west of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the tower on one of the vertical lines, with a wall or slope leading towards it. Keep the horizon level. For the view, turn towards Byåsen and use the zoom to show the line of the hills above the city.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the sky or the tower to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour and the stonework does not wash out. Use panorama, moving slowly, for the sweep over the city.

Best time: Choose a dry, clear day for the most distant view of the hills. Low sun early or late in the day gives the tower and walls more shape.
