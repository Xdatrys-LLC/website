The 3 free photo spots

1Red Fort

The Red Fort (Lal Qila) is the great Mughal fortress-palace of Old Delhi, named for its red sandstone walls. Shah Jahan had it built after moving his capital from Agra to Delhi, and it was finished in 1648. The design is credited to Ustad Ahmad Lahori, who also worked on the Taj Mahal. Since 2007 it has been part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The best free photo is of the Lahori Gate, the main entrance. On 15 August 1947 Jawaharlal Nehru raised the Indian flag above it, and the Prime Minister still raises the national flag there every Independence Day. From the approach in front of the gate you can fit the full height of the sandstone gateway, its flanking bastions and the fort's walls into one frame, with sky above. This is the fort's most photographed spot, and the outer walls can be photographed from outside without a ticket.

For a phone, this is a simple, rewarding shot. Stand back far enough to keep the whole gateway in view, switch on the gridlines to keep the walls straight, and tap on the red stone so the exposure suits it rather than the bright sky. Late afternoon light is recommended and warms the stone nicely. Take a few frames in a row and keep the sharpest. The photo spots inside the fort need an entry ticket, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: The Red Fort stands in Old Delhi, the historic walled part of the city. The Lahori Gate is the fort's main entrance, with the approach road in front.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close to the gate and want its full height and both bastions. Use the default lens (1x) from further back for a more natural look. Use 2x or the telephoto to pick out details of the stonework and the top of the gateway.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (outside, on the approach in front of the main gate). The full height of the red sandstone gateway with its flanking bastions, framed against the sky and the fort's walls. From here the red sandstone gateway rises in full with its bastions on either side, and the fort's walls stretch away to the east behind it.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the gateway up in the centre, keeping the walls level. Leave a band of sky above the top so the full height shows. For a wider view that keeps the whole front of the monument in the frame, try Netaji Subhash Marg, from across the road.

Settings tip: Tap the red stone to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright, so the sandstone keeps its colour. Hold the phone with both hands and keep it level.

Best time: Late afternoon, around golden hour, when the low light warms the red stone.

2Chandni Chowk

Chandni Chowk is the long, busy market road of Old Delhi, and the best-known frame of it is simply the street itself: shopfronts on both sides, rickshaws and people filling the middle, and the Red Fort's walls closing the view at the far end. The name means "Moonlight Square". The market was laid out in 1650 under Shah Jahan, and his daughter Jahanara is credited with the design. At first it had about 1,560 shops, and the road was around 40 yards wide and 1,520 yards long. A water channel once ran down the middle, and a clock tower later replaced the pool at the square. The centre of the market is still called Ghantaghar. Today the road is crowded and noisy, which is exactly what makes it photograph well. Layers of signs, awnings, wires and faces give a phone photo plenty of depth, and the fort at the end gives the eye somewhere to rest. Stand look towards the Red Fort end, and let the road lead into the picture. If you want a second view, Fatehpuri Masjid at the other end of the road has a white dome with a green finial and red sandstone walls. Keep your phone in a hand or on a wrist strap in the crush, and take a few quick frames rather than stopping in the flow of people.

Where it is: Chandni Chowk is the main market street of Old Delhi. It runs from the Red Fort's Lahori Gate in the east to Fatehpuri Masjid in the west.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural view of the road. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if the street feels too tight, and try telephoto (2x-3x) to pull the Red Fort closer and stack the shopfronts.

Where to stand: Stand on Chandni Chowk (middle of the road, looking toward the Red Fort end). The long, crowded market road running toward the Red Fort's walls, framed by shopfronts and street life. In the middle of the road, looking east towards the Red Fort end. From here the photo shows the long market road running towards the fort walls, framed by shops and street life. Keep an eye on rickshaws and other traffic, and step to the side when you need to take your time.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the road in the centre, with the vanishing point at the Red Fort end. Keep the shopfronts as even frames on both sides. Wait for a rickshaw or a walker to enter the lower third for scale. For a different view, the dome of Fatehpuri Masjid at the western end is worth a frame in its own right.

Settings tip: Tap on the fort or the far end of the street to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky does not wash out. Hold still for a moment: Live Photos can capture the movement, and you can pick the best frame afterwards. Wipe the lens first, as street dust is common.

Best time: Earlier in the day is usually calmer, and the light is gentler for photos looking down the street. Later the market fills up and the crowds become part of the picture.

3India Gate

India Gate is a war memorial arch at the eastern end of New Delhi's ceremonial axis. It was designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens and stands 42 metres tall on a low base of red Bharatpur stone. It remembers 74,187 Indian soldiers who died between 1914 and 1921, and the names of around 13,300 British Empire servicemen are also inscribed on it. The foundation stone was laid on 10 February 1921, and the arch was inaugurated on 12 February 1931.

It photographs well because everything around it is symmetrical. The avenue, now called Kartavya Path, runs straight towards the arch, and the lawns and lines of the path lead the eye along it. A phone copes well with this: the whole arch fits in one frame, and the long perspective gives a real sense of scale. Seen from the avenue, the arch is framed against open green lawns and the sky.

Dawn and blue hour are the recommended times for the light. Crowds and vendors are heavy near the arch, especially on public holidays, so wait for a gap or step back and use the avenue's length to keep people small in the frame. Stay on the paths, as the lawns are largely off limits to walking. For a classic straight-on view, you can take it from the paths near the arch, though it is busier there.

Where it is: India Gate is in central New Delhi, at the eastern end of Kartavya Path (formerly Rajpath), the ceremonial boulevard that runs towards Rashtrapati Bhavan.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the arch and the avenue fit in one frame. Try 2x to bring the arch a little closer without moving. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) when you turn round to take in the whole avenue.

Where to stand: Stand on Kartavya Path (the India Gate end of the boulevard, looking back west toward Rashtrapati Bhavan). The long ceremonial avenue running away from the arch, with the lawns and symmetry leading the eye along the axis. The arch lies to the east, straight along the avenue. For a second picture, turn round and look west along the boulevard towards Rashtrapati Bhavan, with the lawns and symmetry leading the eye down the avenue.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the arch on the centre line, so the avenue's lines lead towards it. Keep the phone level so the arch does not lean. Leave some path and grass in the foreground to show the length of the avenue. If you want a different view, a quieter stretch of the avenue also shows the arch in the distance, with water and grass in the foreground.

Settings tip: Tap on the arch to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is washing out. At blue hour, switch on night mode and rest your elbows against your body or a solid surface to keep the phone steady.

Best time: Dawn and blue hour, per Chasing Hippoz. The light is soft.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Delhi?

The first three on this map are Red Fort, Chandni Chowk and India Gate. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 18.

Where should I stand to photograph the Red Fort?

Stand right at the Red Fort (outside, on the approach in front of the main gate). Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close to the gate and want its full height and both bastions.

When is the best time to photograph Chandni Chowk?

Earlier in the day is usually calmer, and the light is gentler for photos looking down the street. Later the market fills up and the crowds become part of the picture.

How do I get to India Gate?

Walk from Central Secretariat metro station along the boulevard to the India Gate end of Kartavya Path, using the underpasses to cross the roads.
