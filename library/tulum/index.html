This map shows 5 of the most-searched sights in Tulum as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Akumal Bay, Tulum Ruins, Gran Cenote, Dos Ojos and Temple of Frescoes lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Akumal

Akumal is a small beach resort village on the Caribbean coast of Quintana Roo, about 100 km south of Cancún and between Playa del Carmen and Tulum. It began in 1958 as a community for scuba divers, on the site of a former coconut plantation. Today it is best known for snorkelling, because green sea turtles come into the bay to feed on sea grass. Akumal Bay was declared a marine refuge in 2016 to protect them. The main bay can be busy, so this spot suggests Half Moon Bay, a quieter bay nearby that you reach on foot from the shore.

It photographs well because the reef keeps the water calm and turquoise, and the shoreline makes a clean, simple edge to the frame. Half Moon Bay gives you the colour of the water without the crowds of the main beach. You can come away with pictures that look different from the usual Akumal shots. If you are in the main bay too, a wider view across the water with the beach and palms is a good second picture.

For a phone, keep the horizon level and let the water colour carry the shot. Wipe the lens first, as sea spray and sunscreen smear it easily. Keep your distance from any turtles and wildlife, and take pictures of them from the shore or from a respectful distance rather than getting close.

Where it is: Akumal is in Tulum Municipality, Quintana Roo, on the Riviera Maya coast between Playa del Carmen and the town of Tulum. Half Moon Bay is the quieter bay next to the main Akumal Bay.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the sweep of the bay, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as a distant swimmer or a line of palms.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (quieter bay near Akumal, reached on foot from shore). Reef-protected turquoise water and the shoreline of a quieter bay, as a quieter alternative to the main beach. From the shore you see the reef-protected turquoise water and the curve of the quieter bay's shoreline. It is a calmer alternative to the main beach.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the horizon on the upper or lower line so the water fills most of the frame. Include a strip of sand in the foreground for depth. If you also visit Akumal Bay, try a wider view of the beach and calm water there as a second picture.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest patch of water to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the white sand does not wash out and the turquoise stays rich. Keep your phone dry and clean the lens before shooting.

Best time: Check conditions on the day, as seaweed can sometimes collect along the shore.

2Tulum Archaeological Site

Tulum was a walled Maya port city on 12-metre cliffs above the Caribbean, and El Castillo, the site's best-known building, is the photograph most visitors come for. The city reached its greatest prominence between the 13th and 15th centuries and was abandoned by the end of the 16th. It photographs well because the stone temple sits right on the cliff edge with turquoise water behind it, so one frame gives you history and sea together. The site was protected on the landward side by a wall averaging 3-5 metres high and about 8 metres thick. In 1843 the explorers Stephens and Catherwood arrived by sea and were struck by a tall building, most likely El Castillo. Go early: the morning light falls on the main face and there are fewer tour groups to dodge. If you have time, two other views are worth a look: Playa Ruinas, for the cliff-top temples above the beach and surf, and the Templo del Dios del Viento, a small round-based temple on the cliff. Beach access depends on conditions and tides.

Where it is: On the Caribbean coast of Quintana Roo, on the cliffs at the edge of the Tulum Archaeological Zone, a walled Maya site about 545 metres across.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the cliff, the sea and, if you turn back along the cliff, the ruins all fit in one frame. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the paths are busy and you cannot step back far enough. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the temple's top and doorways.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (east-facing base of the pyramid, looking out over the water). The main temple on its cliff edge with the turquoise Caribbean filling the background. From here the sea is the view ahead. For the temple on its cliff edge, turn and shoot back along the cliff. The middle of the walled site lies to the west of this point, so the other ruins are on the landward side.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and put the horizon on the upper or lower third line, keeping it level. Leave a little sky above the ruins so the frame does not look cramped. For a different view, Playa Ruinas gives a cove-style shot of the cliff-top temples above the surf.

Settings tip: Tap the temple to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the bright sea stays blue and does not wash out. Try a Live Photo for waves and moving people. Use portrait mode only for a friend standing in front, not for the ruins themselves.

Best time: 8-9 AM, when the morning light hits the main face of the temple and there are fewer tour groups in the frame.

5Temple of the Frescoes

The Temple of the Frescoes sits right in front of El Castillo inside the Tulum archaeological zone. That is what makes it work for a phone photo. You get a close, detailed subject in front and the larger, more recognisable building behind it, so the picture says 'Tulum' without any caption. The outer walls carry stucco figures and corner masks, and these give your foreground plenty of texture. The painted murals inside are closed to visitors to protect them, so this is an outside-only photo, and it is the standard shot that guides describe for this area. Head for the phone icon on the map, which marks the temple itself, then step back to the southeast side, face the front of the temple and keep El Castillo beside it in the frame. Try one wide shot that shows both buildings, then step in for a tighter picture of the stucco figures and masks. Entry to the archaeological zone is required, so check the official site before you go for current rules. Flash is discouraged near the frescoes, so switch it off. Going early, soon after opening, usually gives better light and fewer people walking through your frame. If you're staying later, one guide suggests the west-facing side for warmer late-day light.

Where it is: Inside the Tulum archaeological zone, directly in front of El Castillo.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) so the temple and El Castillo both fit and look natural. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if people or ropes force you to stand close. Use 2x or 3x telephoto, if your phone has it, for the stucco figures and corner masks.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From here you see the outer stucco figures and corner masks, with El Castillo behind them for context.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and put the temple on the lower third, leaving El Castillo in the upper background. Keep the phone level so the walls don't lean. Then take a second, closer frame of the stucco figures or a corner mask. Wait a moment for people to move out of the shot.

Settings tip: Tap the temple to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stonework stays detailed. Turn the flash off.

Best time: Early morning, right after opening, when the light is better and there are fewer people. For warm late-day light, one guide suggests the west-facing side.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Tulum?

The first three on this map are Akumal Bay, Tulum Ruins and Gran Cenote. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 5.

Where should I stand to photograph Akumal Bay?

Stand right at Akumal Bay (quieter bay near Akumal, reached on foot from shore). Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the sweep of the bay, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view.

When is the best time to photograph Tulum Ruins?

8-9 AM, when the morning light hits the main face of the temple and there are fewer tour groups in the frame.

How do I get to Temple of Frescoes?

There are no railway or tram stops nearby, so from Tulum town go to the archaeological zone by taxi or hired scooter.
