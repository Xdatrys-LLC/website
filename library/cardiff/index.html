The 3 free photo spots

1Cardiff Castle

Cardiff Castle sits right in the city centre, and it is really two buildings in one: a medieval Norman castle and a Victorian Gothic mansion. The Normans built the first castle in the late 11th century on top of a 3rd-century Roman fort. Much later, the 3rd Marquess of Bute paid for William Burges to remodel it in Gothic revival style. After the Roman remains were found, Roman-style walls and a gatehouse were built into the design. That mix of eras is why the outside photographs so well: stone walls, towers and a grand entrance front all packed into one view.

The easiest free shot is from the pavement on Castle Street, looking up the street towards the entrance gate. The street acts as a natural frame and leads the eye straight to the gatehouse. If you have time, Bute Park is another place to photograph the castle from, with the castle walls and towers rising above the trees and paths.

A fact worth knowing as you look at the walls: during the Second World War, large air raid shelters were built inside them, big enough for up to 1,800 people. The castle was given to the City of Cardiff in 1947 and is now a visitor attraction and events venue. Keep your phone ready for people walking through the frame, and take a few shots so you can pick the one with the clearest view of the gate.

Where it is: Cardiff Castle is in the city centre of Cardiff, and Castle Street is the street where you take this shot. Bute Park is another place to photograph the castle from.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the street, the gate and some sky in one frame. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if people or traffic leave you too little room, and keep the castle in the middle so the edges are not stretched. Try 2x for a tighter shot of the gatehouse.

Where to stand: Stand on Castle Street (view towards the castle entrance gate). The castle's gatehouse and entrance front, framed by the street leading up to it. On the pavement, and face north towards the castle entrance gate. From here the photo shows the gatehouse and entrance front framed by the street leading up to it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the street so it leads towards the gate. Keep the phone level so the towers do not lean. Wait for a gap in the people on the pavement. For something different, Bute Park shows the walls and towers above the trees, with paths in front.

Settings tip: Tap the gatehouse to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky does not wash out the stonework. If the street is busy, take a short burst or a Live Photo and choose the cleanest frame.

Best time: Check the light on the day and adjust your exposure to suit it.

2Principality Stadium

The Principality Stadium is the national stadium of Wales. It was known as the Millennium Stadium until early 2016, when it was renamed under a sponsorship deal. It opened in June 1999, built for that year's Rugby World Cup, on the Cardiff Arms Park site where the old National Stadium stood. The new stadium was turned to run north to south rather than west to east. It holds 73,931 people, which makes it the largest stadium in Wales. It also has a fully retractable roof, and it has hosted football finals, concerts and even motorsport as well as rugby.

It photographs well because of its sheer size. The curved steel stands and roof structure make strong shapes, and the stadium sits right among the city streets, so you can place people or street life against it for scale. Up close the building is too big to fit into one frame at the default lens (1x), so think in sections: a sweep of wall, the roof line, a gate with people walking past. Turn on the gridlines to keep the vertical edges straight, and step back as far as the pavement allows before you shoot. On match and event days the surroundings are busy and some areas may be closed off, so allow extra time and check the official site before you go.

Where it is: The stadium is in Cardiff, beside the River Taff, with the River Walk on its western side. It stands next to Park Street, Wood Street and Westgate Street. Cardiff Arms Park, the home of Cardiff RFC, is next door to the north.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x). You are very close to the stadium, so the ultra-wide is the lens that lets you fit in a good part of its walls and roof. Keep the default lens (1x) for tighter shots of details such as gates, signs and the structure.

Where to stand: Stand about 30 m south-west of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-east. Face north-east so the middle of the building fills the view ahead. Hold the phone level and angle it up slightly to take in the roof line.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and line the stadium's vertical edges up with them so the walls don't lean inwards. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for one big shot, then switch to the default lens (1x) for a detail shot. Include a few passers-by to show the scale. If you want a longer view of the whole stadium, the River Taff side to the west gives more room to step back.

Settings tip: Tap on the stadium wall to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky doesn't wash out. Keep the phone level, because tilting it up makes the sides slope inwards. If you use the ultra-wide, keep people out of the extreme edges of the frame, where they look stretched.

Best time: Choose a day with some cloud or soft light, and go outside event days if you want a calm view. Early morning is usually the quietest time. On match or concert days the area is much busier, which can add atmosphere but makes it harder to find space.

7Bute Park

Bute Park is a big, green stretch of central Cardiff: 130 acres (53 ha) of parkland, tree-lined avenues and gardens that once formed the grounds of Cardiff Castle. It runs along the east bank of the River Taff, which gives you water, trees and open lawns in one frame. The park was laid out from 1873 by Andrew Pettigrew, head gardener to the 3rd Marquess of Bute, and was handed to Cardiff's council in 1947. The park and the castle grounds are listed Grade I for their special historic interest. From there the red footbridge, the river and the park's trees come together in one easy picture. If you have time, two other places are worth a look. Cooper's Field gives a view of the castle's clock tower rising above mature trees. Blackweir Bridge looks onto the river and a tumbling weir. Along the way you may spot William Burges's Animal Wall, a stone wall topped with sculpted animal heads, and the Summerhouse kiosk, built in 2010. Keep your phone steady and take a few frames of each scene, because the light under the trees changes quickly. Check the official site before you go in case of closures or event set-ups.

Where it is: Bute Park lies in central Cardiff, along the east bank of the River Taff and next to Cardiff Castle. Sophia Gardens and Pontcanna Fields are on the opposite bank, reached by footbridges.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the bridge, river and trees. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit in more of the park. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the castle clock tower from Cooper's Field or for details at the weir.

Where to stand: Stand on Millennium Bridge (near the Summerhouse kiosk). The red footbridge over the River Taff, framed by the riverside and the park's trees. From here you can photograph the red footbridge over the River Taff, framed by the riverside and the park's trees. The park itself stretches away to the north.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the red bridge along one of the lower lines, with trees on either side. Keep the horizon level. For a different picture, try Cooper's Field, where the castle's clock tower rises above the trees with the acer and camellia collection in front. Blackweir Bridge is another option, with the weir beside it.

Settings tip: Tap on the bridge to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the red stays rich and the sky doesn't wash out. Try a Live Photo by the weir to catch the moving water.

Best time: Soft morning or late-afternoon light is usually kinder on the water and the red of the bridge. Autumn is good for tree colour, but any dry day works.
