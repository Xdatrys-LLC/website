The 3 free photo spots

1Whistler Mountain

Whistler Mountain rises above the town of Whistler. It sits on the northwestern edge of Garibaldi Provincial Park in the Fitzsimmons Range of the Coast Mountains. It is the location of the Whistler-Blackcomb ski resort, and it was used for the 2010 Winter Olympics. Its Lillooet name is Nsqwítsu. It was once called London Mountain, after a mining claim and the fog that gathers at its base. The name changed to Whistler in the 1960s, after the whistling calls of the marmots that live in the high meadows. The summit is served by the Whistler Peak chair, which makes it one of the most visited summits in British Columbia. The rock is mostly andesite and dacite lava flows with shale, formed around 100 million years ago. That gives the upper slopes a rugged, layered look that shows well on a phone when the light is low and the shadows are long. This spot works best as a wide scene: the mountain filling the top of the frame, with trees or rooftops below to show its size. Use a landmark in front, such as a path or a tree line, to add depth. Zoom in later for ridges and lift lines. Weather changes quickly, so check the sky before you set out.

Where it is: Whistler Mountain rises directly above the town of Whistler in British Columbia and holds the town's ski resort. It lies on the northwestern edge of Garibaldi Provincial Park.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole slope and some foreground, then use the default lens (1x) for a more natural look. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for ridges, lift lines and rock detail.

Where to stand: Stand in Rainbow Park (northwest shoreline of Alta Lake, on the wooden dock). Lake-level view across Alta Lake to the mountain backdrop, with Whistler and Blackcomb both in frame.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the skyline on the upper third line, leaving the lower two thirds for trees, paths or buildings that show how big the mountain is. Keep the phone level so the slopes don't tilt. For a second shot, zoom in on one ridge or a lift line.

Settings tip: Tap the sky or the brightest slope to focus, then pull the exposure slider down a little so snow or pale cloud keeps its detail. A panorama can work for the full width of the mountain; sweep slowly and keep the phone level.

Best time: Choose a clear day, and go early or in the late afternoon when the light is softer and the slopes show more shape. Midday sun makes the mountain look flat.

2Peak 2 Peak Gondola

The Peak 2 Peak Gondola links Whistler Mountain's Roundhouse Lodge with Blackcomb Mountain's Rendezvous Lodge. It was the first lift to join the two mountains. It was built in 2007 and 2008 and carries 28 cabins on a tri-cable system. When it opened it held the world record for the longest free span between towers, at 3.03 kilometres. That record was passed in 2017, but it is still the highest gondola above the ground, at 436 metres. For a photographer, that means a long line of small cabins crossing open air above the valley, with the two mountainsides behind. A good place to photograph it is the Whistler Mountain terminal, outside the station entrance, where you can catch cabins leaving the station with the valley below. The station buildings are covered in a blue, translucent material, which adds a bit of colour to the shot. The short walk across from the Roundhouse can be snowy, so allow a little extra time. Take a few frames as cabins come and go, and include some of the surroundings so the height is clear. If you want a second angle, the Cloudraker Skybridge and Raven's Eye give big mountain views, though the gondola itself may not be in frame from either. Low cloud can hide the views, so check conditions before you set out.

Where it is: Inside the Whistler Blackcomb resort, the gondola spans the valley above Fitzsimmons Creek between Whistler Mountain and Blackcomb Mountain.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to show the gondola line with the valley and mountainside around it. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the slope and sky. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a single cabin on the span.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. It is not at the Whistler Mountain terminal, which is a separate spot outside the station entrance, where cabins can be seen leaving the station.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the line of the cabins along one of them, with the valley filling the background. Wait for a cabin to be clear of other objects so it stands out. For a different view, the Cloudraker Skybridge and Raven's Eye offer mountain panoramas, but the gondola may not be in frame from either.

Settings tip: Tap on a cabin to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if bright sky or snow is washing out the picture. A Live Photo can catch the cabins moving, and panorama mode suits the wide valley view.

Best time: Choose a clear daylight window, as low cloud can hide the valley and the far side. Check the weather and summit conditions on the day.

3Whistler Village

Whistler Village is the setting for this shot, and the classic way to photograph it is from Whistler Olympic Plaza. Here the Olympic Rings sit in the foreground, with the village roofs and snowy mountains rising behind them. It is the village postcard shot: a clear mountain backdrop, with plaza greenery and water features to give the picture a foreground. One guide notes there is enough room to back up and fit the rings, the trees and the mountain into a single frame, so take a few steps back before you shoot. The plaza can be busy during events and may have stage setups, so look around before choosing your spot. If you have time for a second picture, wander into the village lanes. Timber-framed storefronts, cobbled paths and street life look good with the mountains showing above the rooftops. The Covered Bridge is a quieter option: a rustic wooden bridge with the icy blue creek below and mountains beyond. The village gondola, the subject of this entry, lies south of the plaza. The plaza is the better place to stand for a view that takes in the whole scene. Keep your phone steady, tap the screen on the rings to set focus, and check the edges of the frame for passers-by before you press the shutter.

Where it is: The viewpoint is Whistler Olympic Plaza, in Whistler Village, about 676 m north of the village gondola. The village sits below the snowy mountains that appear behind the Olympic Rings.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the rings, the village and the mountains in one frame. If the plaza is crowded and you cannot step back, switch to ultra-wide (0.5x). To pick out the mountains behind the village, try telephoto (2x or 3x, depending on your phone).

Where to stand: Stand in Whistler Olympic Plaza (in front of the Olympic Rings, stepping back for the wider frame). The Olympic Rings in the foreground with the village and snowy mountains rising behind them.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the rings on one of the lower lines, with the mountains filling the upper part. Use the plaza greenery or the water features as a foreground. For a different view, the village lanes and the Covered Bridge make good alternatives: the lanes show timber-framed storefronts with mountains above the rooftops, and the bridge shows the blue creek below with mountains beyond.

Settings tip: Tap the rings to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky and snow keep their detail. In the evening, use night mode only if the lights look dim, and hold the phone steady against your body or a rail.

Best time: Evening, when the village lights and the mountain silhouettes work together.
