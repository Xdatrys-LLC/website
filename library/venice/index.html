The 3 free photo spots

1Rialto Bridge

The Rialto Bridge is the oldest of the four bridges across the Grand Canal, and it links the districts of San Marco and San Polo. The stone bridge you see today is a single broad arch designed by Antonio da Ponte, built between 1588 and 1591. Two covered ramps rise to a central portico, and both ramps are lined with small shops. At the time, the engineering was thought so daring that one architect predicted it would fall down. It has stood for more than four centuries. Before it, a series of earlier crossings stood here, starting with a floating pontoon bridge and followed by a wooden one that collapsed more than once.

It photographs well because the shape is so clear. From the San Polo bank on Riva del Vin you see the bridge side-on, with the whole arch, the little shops in the span and the Grand Canal below. Vaporetti and other boats pass through the frame, and on a calm morning the arch reflects in the water. That makes a good busy-but-tidy scene for a phone. Aim to include a bit of water in the foreground, and wait a moment for a boat to move into a gap rather than cutting across the bridge. If you want a different feel, Riva del Ferro gives a view from water level, and Riva del Carbon gives a three-quarter view that works well as the lights come on in the evening.

Where it is: The Rialto Bridge crosses the Grand Canal in the middle of Venice, joining the San Polo bank to the San Marco bank.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the side-on view of the arch. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want to include more of the canal and the boats. Try telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for a tighter shot of the arch and the shops.

Where to stand: Stand on Riva del Vin (San Polo (south-west) bank, facing the bridge side-on). The bridge's single arch seen from the side, with the Grand Canal, passing vaporetti and reflections in the water. From here you face the bridge side-on, with its single arch, the Grand Canal, passing vaporetti and the reflections in the water. The bridge is to the east of you.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and set the waterline on the lower third, so that the arch fills the middle of the picture and there is a bit of water in front of it. Keep the phone level, since the long line of the bridge makes a tilt obvious. Wait for a boat to come into a gap in the scene. If the first spot is too crowded, Riva del Ferro (at water level) and Riva del Carbon (a three-quarter view) are alternatives.

Settings tip: Tap on the arch to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the sky and the lit water don't wash out. In the blue hour, use night mode and rest your elbows on something steady, since boats move and will blur in a long exposure.

Best time: Sunrise (about 6:30-7:30) or the blue hour, when the water is usually calm and the reflections are clearest. By midday Riva del Vin can be crowded.

6Grand Canal

The Grand Canal is the main water street of Venice. It is 3.8 kilometres long and between 30 and 90 metres wide, and it winds through the city in a big reverse S from the railway station to the basin at San Marco. More than 170 buildings line its banks, most of them from the 13th to the 18th century, built by noble families who wanted their palazzi to show off their wealth. Many palaces rise straight out of the water with no pavement, so the full sweep of façades is best seen from the canal itself.

For a phone photo, the classic place is the Ponte dell'Accademia, one of only four bridges over the canal. Looking east from the middle of the bridge, the canal opens out in front of you. The domed church of Santa Maria della Salute sits as the natural focal point, and palazzi line both banks. The long view gives your picture depth, and boats passing on the water add scale and movement. Lean on the side of the bridge to keep your phone steady, and keep the dome slightly off-centre so the canal leads the eye towards it.

If you have time for a second picture, Riva del Vin shows the Rialto Bridge side-on, with reflections in the water. Fondamenta della Salute gives a view across the water towards the San Marco bell tower.

Where it is: The Grand Canal runs through the central districts (sestieri) of Venice, from the Santa Lucia railway station to the basin at San Marco. The Ponte dell'Accademia is one of its bridges, with the Salute church downstream to the east.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the full canal view. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull the Salute dome closer. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want the bridge's railing and a lot of sky in the frame.

Where to stand: Stand on Ponte dell'Accademia (facing east, toward the Basilica di Santa Maria della Salute). A sweeping view down the Grand Canal with the domed Salute church as the focal point and palazzi lining both banks. From the bridge, face east down the canal towards the Salute. From there the photo shows a long view down the Grand Canal, with the domed Salute church as the focal point and palazzi on both banks.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the canal along the centre line so the banks lead towards the dome. Keep a little water in the foreground and let a passing boat enter one third of the frame. For a different angle, try Riva del Vin for the Rialto Bridge side-on, or Fondamenta della Salute for gondolas with the San Marco bell tower across the water.

Settings tip: Tap on the dome to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour at sunset. At blue hour, use night mode and rest your elbows on the railing to stay steady. Wait for boats to pass before pressing the shutter, or use a Live Photo to pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Sunset and blue hour. The bridge is very busy in high season, so an early or late visit gives you more room to take photos.

15Fondaco dei Tedeschi

The Fondaco dei Tedeschi stands on the Grand Canal right beside the Rialto Bridge. For centuries it was the hostel and warehouse of Venice's German merchants. The first building dates from 1228. After a fire in 1505 it was rebuilt between 1505 and 1508 in Renaissance style, around one large central courtyard on four floors. Today it is a department store, and the map's stand-here point puts you at the heart of the building.

It photographs well because of its scale and its details. The building is about 82 m across, so the simple, regular geometry of the courtyard suits a phone camera, with strong lines and layers of arches. The Islamic-influenced arches are worth noticing: large ones below and smaller ones above. On the outside, look up for the large wall clock installed in 1571 and the roofline merlons with circular cut-outs topped with spheres. The façades were once frescoed, said to be by Giorgione or Titian in 1508, but only faint fragments survive, so don't expect colour on the walls.

For your pictures, take one wide shot to show the whole space, then move in on a few details: an arch, a stair, the clock or the roofline. Keep your phone level with the gridlines so the vertical lines stay straight. Because this is a shop, some areas may have access limits, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: On the Grand Canal next to the Rialto Bridge, in Venice.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the courtyard and its tiers of arches, then use the default lens (1x) for a more natural view. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the clock, the roofline merlons and other details high up.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. The map places the point at the centre of the building, which is around its large inner courtyard, so you are looking up and around at the surrounding floors rather than at one flat façade.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line them up with the arches and the building's vertical edges so everything looks straight. Include two or three levels of arches to show the layers, then take a tighter shot of one repeated detail. Outside, tilt up and use zoom to isolate the clock and the sphere-topped merlons against the sky.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on the brightest part of the architecture, then lower the exposure slider slightly so bright areas do not wash out. Hold the phone steady with both hands, as indoor light can be dim.

Best time: Soft, even daylight, for example on a bright overcast day or outside the middle of the day, suits the stonework and avoids harsh shadows. If you want the courtyard quieter, try going early, but check the official site for when it is open.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Venice?

The first three on this map are Rialto Bridge, Bridge of Sighs and Doge's Palace. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 17.

Where should I stand to photograph the Rialto Bridge?

Stand on Riva del Vin (San Polo (south-west) bank, facing the bridge side-on). Start with the default lens (1x) for the side-on view of the arch.

When is the best time to photograph the Grand Canal?

Sunset and blue hour. The bridge is very busy in high season, so an early or late visit gives you more room to take photos.

How do I get to the Fondaco dei Tedeschi?

Head to the Rialto Bridge, which the building stands right beside on the Grand Canal.
