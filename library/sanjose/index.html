This map shows 14 of the most-searched sights in San José as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Central Market, Metropolitan Cathedral of San José, Jade Museum, Teatro Nacional and Plaza de la Cultura lead the list. The 3 free photo spots

1San José Central Market

San José Central Market is the city's largest market, and it fills an entire block on Avenida Central. It opened in 1880 and is still a busy working place rather than a showpiece. Inside is a maze of narrow alleys with more than 200 shops, stalls and small, inexpensive restaurants called sodas. You'll find meat, fish, fruit, vegetables, coffee, flowers, herbal remedies, souvenirs and local handcrafts, and even snakeskin boots and cowboy saddles. Tens of thousands of people pass through each day.

That mix is what makes it good for photos. Every few steps there is a new scene: piles of colourful produce, hanging goods, hand-painted signs, cooks at counters and shoppers moving through tight lanes. Keep your phone in your hand and your bag zipped, because the alleys are crowded and you'll want to move easily.

Look for repeating patterns, such as rows of stalls, strings of goods or stacked fruit, and shoot along an alley for depth. For close-ups, choose a single stall or product and fill the frame. If you want to photograph a person, smile and ask first. Mercado Borbón, which specialises in food, is two blocks to the north if you want a second stop.

Where it is: The market sits in central San José on Avenida Central, taking up a whole city block about 250 m northwest of the Parque Central.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the narrow alleys and busy crossings, and the default lens (1x) for most stalls and people. Try telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for details such as fruit, coffee or crafts without leaning over the counter.

Where to stand: Stand about 10 m east of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face west. From here, walk the nearest alleys and shoot along them rather than staying in one spot.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up an alley along a vertical line so the rows of stalls lead the eye into the picture. Fill the frame with one stall or product for close-ups. Use portrait mode for a stallholder or a food counter, once they have agreed to be photographed.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on your subject, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so bright openings and signs don't wash out. If the light is dim in the alleys, hold the phone steady for a moment and let night mode work if it switches on.

Best time: Go when the market is open and busy so the stalls are full, but expect crowds. Ask locally or check a current map listing for opening hours before you go.

2Metropolitan Cathedral of San José

The Metropolitan Cathedral is a cathedral in San José, standing in the city centre on Calle Central, by Avenues 2 and 4. The first cathedral here was built in 1802 but was destroyed by an earthquake. That history is worth a line in your photo caption.

It photographs well because it is a large, solid civic landmark in the middle of a busy city. You can shoot it as a whole building, or step closer and pick out details such as the doorway, the stonework and the people passing in front. Including a few passers-by gives a sense of scale and shows it is a working part of city life, not just a monument.

The phone icon on the map is about 25 m south of the subject. From there you can fit a good part of the building into one frame without tilting your phone steeply upwards. Keep the phone level, use the gridlines to keep vertical edges straight, and take a few steps left or right to avoid street signs and parked vehicles. If you want a closer detail shot, walk up to the building and zoom rather than cropping later. Check the official site before you go if you plan to look inside, as visiting arrangements may change.

Where it is: In the centre of San José, Costa Rica, on Calle Central, by Avenues 2 and 4.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole front of the building. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are too close or want to include more of the street. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the doorway or stonework.

Where to stand: Stand about 25 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north. This gives you room to fit most of it in the frame without tilting the phone steeply.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the edges of the building with them so the walls stay upright. Put the cathedral in the centre or slightly to one side. Include a little foreground, such as pavement or people walking by, to give a sense of scale. For a tighter second shot, walk closer and zoom in on one detail.

Settings tip: Tap the cathedral to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is washing out. Make sure the phone is held level, as tilting it upwards makes the walls lean inwards. In dim light, night mode works well if you can hold the phone steady against something.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer harsh shadows. Midday sun can be strong, so check the exposure.

6Spirogyra Butterfly Farm Park Garden

Spirogyra is a small butterfly house in Barrio Amón, San José. Inside a climate-controlled, glass-enclosed space, roughly 50 to 60 species of live butterflies from around Costa Rica fly among flowering plants, trees and cascading waterfalls. A few free-flying birds share the space, and there is a learning centre where you can see caterpillars feeding on their host plants.

It photographs well because everything is close and colourful. The butterflies settle on flowers, so you can get near without chasing them. The waterfalls and greenery give you a backdrop for wider scenes. Species on display include the blue Morpho peleides, the large owl butterflies (Caligo) and the zebra heliconian, so there is plenty to look for. Move slowly, wait for a butterfly to land, and let it come to you rather than reaching toward it.

The best pictures are taken inside, among the flowering plants and the waterfall area, so this is a paid view. Before you go, check the official site for admission, opening hours and any rules or fees for photography. From the street, the green, plant-filled frontage of the small house also makes a pleasant outside shot of the place.

Where it is: In Barrio Amón, in the Carmen District of San José, on the edge of the Río Torres and just north of Zoológico Simón Bolívar.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for most shots of flowers, butterflies and the waterfall. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the whole waterfall scene, and to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for butterflies that land out of reach.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Inside the garden, stand among the flowering plants and near the waterfall area. From there you can frame butterflies on the flowers with the waterfall set in the glass enclosure behind them. As a second option, take a street-level shot of the entrance and the green frontage of the small house.

Framing: For close-ups, wait for a butterfly to settle on a flower, then fill the frame with it and leave a little space around the wings. For a wider scene, use the waterfall and plants as a backdrop. Turn on gridlines to keep the picture level, and use the entrance frontage as an alternative outside shot.

Settings tip: Tap on the butterfly to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so bright wings and sky do not wash out. Try portrait mode on a butterfly that sits still. Live Photos can catch a wing-flutter, and the telephoto lets you keep your distance. Wipe the lens first, since glass-enclosed, humid spaces can leave it misty.

Best time: Go when the garden is open and check the official site for current hours. Bright daytime hours suit butterflies, but a quieter visit makes it easier to frame your shots.
