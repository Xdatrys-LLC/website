The 3 free photo spots

1Real Alcázar of Seville

The Real Alcázar is a royal palace in the heart of Seville, and it is still one of the official residences of the Spanish royal family. The site began as an Islamic-era citadel in the 10th century. After the Castilian conquest in 1248 it was rebuilt again and again, and the best-known part today is the richly decorated Mudéjar palace built by Pedro I in the 1360s. Together with the neighbouring Cathedral and the General Archive of the Indies, it was made a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

For a phone photo, the outside is the easiest place to start. From the middle of Plaza del Triunfo you can step back far enough to fit the main gate, the Puerta del León, and the long brick walls into one frame, with the Cathedral and the Giralda close by. The square is open, so you have room to find the angle you want. Early morning is the time guides recommend, because the facade catches direct sun.

If you want a second picture, try Calle Agua, a narrow whitewashed lane with the palace wall on one side and bougainvillea overhead. The Jardines de Murillo also work well: palm and orange trees and tiled benches sit in front of the outer wall. The Patio de Banderas, just outside the palace exit, is free to enter and gives a good view of the Giralda, but it fills up with people leaving the palace.

Where it is: In the historic centre of Seville, right beside the Cathedral and the General Archive of the Indies. Plaza del Triunfo faces the Alcázar's main gate, the Puerta del León, across the open square.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to fit the gate and walls in one frame. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if the square is busy and you cannot step back any further. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details of the gate and the brickwork.

Where to stand: Stand in Plaza del Triunfo (centre of the square, facing the Puerta del León). The Alcázar's main gate and brick walls framed across the open square, with the Cathedral and Giralda close by. Guides recommend the centre of Plaza del Triunfo, facing the Puerta del León, as the viewpoint for the main gate and brick walls, with the Cathedral and Giralda close by.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the gate up with the centre of the frame, keeping the walls straight and level. Step back until the whole entrance fits. For a different picture, try Calle Agua, where the whitewashed lane and bougainvillea lead the eye along the palace wall.

Settings tip: Tap on the sunlit wall to set focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the brick keeps its colour and the sky does not wash out.

Best time: Early morning, when the facade catches direct sun.

2Plaza de España

Plaza de España is a huge half-circle of brick and tile on the edge of Maria Luisa Park. It was built in 1928 for the Ibero-American Exposition of 1929 and designed by Aníbal González. It mixes Baroque, Renaissance and Moorish-inspired styles, which gives it plenty of detail for a phone camera. A canal curves in front of the building, and four bridges cross it, each standing for one of the ancient kingdoms of Spain. The Vicente Traver fountain sits in the centre, and the tiled alcoves along the walls each represent a different province of Spain.

The plaza photographs well because everything is symmetrical and curved. From the upper gallery balconies above the arcades, near the central section, you get a high, wide view with the canal, the central fountain and both towers in one frame. That extra height is why several guides rate it as the most iconic wide view of the square. Staircases at several points along the building lead up to the gallery. Back at ground level, the curved facade reflected in the canal makes a good second shot, and the sweep of the building leads the eye into the frame. The tiled alcoves are popular for portraits. The plaza has also appeared in films, including Star Wars: Episode II – Attack of the Clones. Water features are sometimes drained, which changes the canal shots, so be ready to focus on the architecture instead.

Where it is: Plaza de España is in the southern part of central Seville, on the edge of Maria Luisa Park, which was the main site of the 1929 exposition.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole curve of the plaza, the canal and both towers in one frame. Use the default lens (1x) for a calmer, less stretched view of the central section. Switch to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the towers, the tilework and the alcoves.

Where to stand: Stand at Plaza de España (upper gallery balconies above the arcades, near the central section). A high, wide overview of the whole plaza, with the canal, central fountain and both towers in one frame. The place to shoot from is the upper gallery balconies above the arcades, near the central section, which you reach by the staircases along the building. From the gallery, look out over the balustrade to see the canal, the central fountain and both towers together.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the fountain with the middle of the frame so the symmetry looks deliberate. Keep the canal in the lower third and let the curve of the building run across the top. For a different shot, go back to ground level by the canal and bridge and photograph the semicircular facade with its reflection in the water.

Settings tip: Tap on the brightest part of the facade to focus, then lower the exposure slider slightly so the sky and the brick keep their detail. Hold the phone level against the gridlines, because the curved lines show any tilt. Portrait mode works well for people in front of the tiled alcoves.

Best time: Early morning gives the emptiest frames, but one guide says the sun rises behind the main building, so much of the plaza stays in shade until late morning. Crowds build from about 10–11 AM, and the central bridges get busy then.

6Santa Cruz

Santa Cruz is the old Jewish quarter of Seville, a tight knot of whitewashed lanes, small squares and orange trees next to the Cathedral and the Real Alcázar. When Ferdinand III of Castile took the city, he gathered Seville's Jewish community into this one neighbourhood. After 1492 the area declined, and it was renewed in the 18th century. The best place to start with a phone is the Plaza de Santa Cruz. The square takes its name from a Mudéjar church that once stood here. It was pulled down during the Napoleonic Wars, and the old floor became the square. The painter Murillo was buried in that church. In the middle stands the wrought-iron Cruz de la Cerrajería, set in a small raised garden with steps, hedged paths and orange trees around it. That gives you a neat, layered picture: dark ironwork against pale walls, with greenery framing it. Guides describe it as a quiet, shaded spot. It is also pleasant in the morning, before the tour groups arrive. Take a wide shot of the whole square first, then step closer for the cross and the foliage. Keep an eye on the background, because people tend to wander through. Afterwards, wander the lanes towards the Cathedral side for narrow, shadowy street scenes where a vertical frame works well.

Where it is: Santa Cruz is in the old centre of Seville, between the Jardines de Murillo, the Real Alcázar and Calle Mateos Gago, right beside the Cathedral and the Giralda. The Plaza de Santa Cruz is a small square inside the quarter.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the cross and its garden. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the whole square or the narrowest lanes, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the ironwork and orange-tree details.

Where to stand: Stand in Plaza de Santa Cruz (inside the raised central garden, on the paths beside the wrought-iron cross). The wrought-iron Cruz de la Cerrajería standing in the middle of the square, with orange trees and the surrounding hedged paths around it. Inside the raised central garden on the paths beside the wrought-iron cross.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the cross on one of the vertical lines, with the hedges and orange trees filling the sides. Crouch slightly so the cross stands out against the pale walls behind it. The ultra-wide works well for the square, but keep the cross away from the edges, where it can stretch.

Settings tip: Tap on the cross to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the white walls and bright sky stay detailed and the ironwork stays dark. In the shady lanes, hold the phone steady and let night mode help if the light is low.

Best time: Early in the morning, before the tour groups arrive, is quieter. Soft light in the early morning or late afternoon is kinder than harsh midday sun.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Seville?

The first three on this map are Real Alcázar, Plaza de España and Seville Cathedral. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 19.

Where should I stand to photograph the Real Alcázar?

Stand in Plaza del Triunfo (centre of the square, facing the Puerta del León). Start with the default lens (1x) to fit the gate and walls in one frame.

When is the best time to photograph Plaza de España?

Early morning gives the emptiest frames, but one guide says the sun rises behind the main building, so much of the plaza stays in shade until late morning. Crowds build from about 10–11 AM, and the central bridges get busy then.

How do I get to Barrio Santa Cruz?

There is no Underground or tram stop right at this spot, so head for the Cathedral and Giralda area and walk into the quarter's lanes.
