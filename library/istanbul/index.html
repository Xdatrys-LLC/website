The 3 free photo spots

1Hagia Sophia

Hagia Sophia is the great domed building at the heart of old Istanbul. Emperor Justinian I had it built between 532 and 537 as a church, and Isidore of Miletus and Anthemius of Tralles designed it. When it was finished it was the largest interior space in the world, and its dome design shaped later mosques across the city. It served as a church until 1453, then as a mosque, then as a museum from 1935 to 2020, and it is a mosque again today. That long history shows in the outline: a huge central dome, stepped semi-domes and minarets added later.

It photographs well from Sultanahmet Meydanı, the square beside it. From there the fountain gives you a foreground, and Hagia Sophia sits behind it with the Blue Mosque sometimes in the same frame. After dark the fountain lighting and the lit building add to the picture.

The square is crowded in the daytime and busiest after about 9 am, so go early if you can. Just after dawn the water is calmer and can give reflections. For a quieter alternative, the lantern-lined path by Hürrem Sultan Hamamı leads towards the big dome, though the whole building is harder to fit in. Keep your phone steady against your body or a railing, and wipe the lens before you start.

Where it is: Hagia Sophia is in Sultanahmet, the old part of Istanbul, beside Sultanahmet Square. The Blue Mosque is nearby and can sometimes be framed together with Hagia Sophia.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to take in the fountain, Hagia Sophia and perhaps the Blue Mosque together. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, less stretched view of Hagia Sophia past the fountain. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the dome and minarets, or to pick out details when the square is busy.

Where to stand: Stand in Sultanahmet Meydanı (central fountain, Hagia Sophia side of the square). Hagia Sophia framed past the fountain, with the Blue Mosque sometimes in the same frame, and the fountain lighting in the foreground after dark. By the central fountain on the Hagia Sophia side of the square. Hagia Sophia is to the north-east. From here the fountain forms the foreground, with Hagia Sophia framed behind it and the Blue Mosque sometimes beside it.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and put the fountain on the lower third, with Hagia Sophia above it. Keep the dome near the centre of the frame and hold the phone level so the minarets do not lean. For a different angle, try the three-quarter view from the corner of the square, which suits a night shot with the fountains in front.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky and floodlit walls keep their detail. After dark, use night mode and brace your elbows against your body. The fountain lights change colour, so take a few frames.

Best time: Just after dawn, when the square is quiet and the water can reflect the building. After dark is the other good time, for the floodlit facade.

2Grand Bazaar

The Grand Bazaar (Kapalıçarşı, 'Covered Market') is one of the largest and oldest covered markets in the world. It has 61 covered streets and over 4,000 shops across roughly 30,700 square metres. Its oldest part, the Old Bedesten, was begun in the winter of 1455/56, soon after the Ottoman conquest of the city, and the market grew up around it over the following centuries. For photographs, it is all about repeating patterns and colour. Hanging lamps, stacked ceramics, rugs, spices and painted vaults fill every frame, and the long, arched passages pull the eye down the street. The phone icon on the map is in the middle of the bazaar, so the subject is all around you rather than in one direction. Look for a straight stretch of passage and use the vaulted roof as your frame. Then step in closer to a single shopfront, a lamp display or a tray of tea glasses. Ask before photographing people. Keep a few moments for simple details such as tiles, lanterns and brass, which often look better than the wide view.

Where it is: In the Fatih district of the old walled city of Istanbul, in the neighbourhood that shares its name. It stretches roughly from west to east between the Beyazıt Mosque and the Nuruosmaniye Mosque.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the vaulted ceilings and long passages. Use the default lens (1x) for most shopfronts and street scenes. Use telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for details such as lamps, ceramics and spices from a polite distance.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. The market surrounds you on every side, so there is no single building to aim at. Choose a straight passage and point your phone along it, keeping to the side so you do not block the flow of people.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the centre of the arched passage with the middle of the frame, so the vault repeats on both sides. Keep the phone level so the walls do not lean. For detail shots, fill the frame with one colourful display and include a little of the passage behind it for depth.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on the brightest part of a display, then lower the exposure slider a little so lamps and glass do not burn out. If it is dim, use night mode and hold still for a second or two. Wipe the lens before you start, as bags and pockets leave smudges.

Best time: Visit when it is less crowded, if you can, so you have more room to frame a clean view down a passage.

4Topkapı Palace

Topkapı Palace sits on a hilly promontory above the water and was the main home and administrative centre of the Ottoman sultans from the 1460s until the mid-19th century. Mehmed the Conqueror ordered its construction in 1459, a few years after the conquest of Constantinople. The name Topkapı, meaning Cannon Gate, came into use after the early Ottoman period (dated to either the 18th or the 19th century). Since 1924 it has been a museum, and it forms part of the Historic Areas of Istanbul, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

For a phone photo, start at the front. The monumental stone entrance gate, Bâb-ı Hümâyun, is a strong, symmetrical subject, and with Hagia Sophia behind you it makes a good opening shot for the visit. Stand directly in front of the gate, switch on gridlines and line up the centre of the archway with the middle of the screen. As general photo advice, you can wait a moment for people to move through, or include a few of them to show how big the gate is.

Inside, the palace has four main courtyards and many smaller buildings. Two other views are popular with visitors: the Fourth Courtyard, with a wide panorama over the Bosphorus, Golden Horn, Galata Tower and the Asian shore, and the Baghdad Pavilion, with its blue-and-white tiles. Both are inside the ticketed grounds, so check the official site before you go, including whether those terraces are open that day.

Where it is: Topkapı Palace is in the east of the Fatih district of Istanbul, on Seraglio Point (Sarayburnu), the promontory overlooking the Golden Horn where the Bosphorus meets the Sea of Marmara. The main gate faces Hagia Sophia.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for the gate, as it keeps the straight lines and symmetry natural. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are too close to fit the whole gate in. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details on the upper part of the gate.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (Standing directly in front of the main gate, with Hagia Sophia behind you). The monumental stone entrance gate of the palace, framed symmetrically, usually as an opening shot. Walk north-east toward the palace and, once you reach Bâb-ı Hümâyun, stand directly in front of the main gate with Hagia Sophia behind you. From there the photo shows the monumental stone gate framed symmetrically.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the archway on the centre line, keeping the phone level so the walls do not lean. Keep a little space above the gate. If you go inside the ticketed grounds, the Fourth Courtyard gives a wide Bosphorus and Golden Horn panorama, often with kiosk roofs in front. The Baghdad Pavilion gives a close-up of its blue-and-white tiles with the Golden Horn and Galata Tower behind.

Settings tip: Tap the gate to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright, so the stone keeps its detail. As general advice, take a few frames while people cross the entrance. Tripods are not permitted and drones are banned, so hold the phone steady with both hands.

Best time: No best time is given for this spot. Check the official site for closing days before you plan the visit.
