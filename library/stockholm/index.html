This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in Stockholm as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Vasa Museum, Stortorget, ABBA The Museum, Djurgården and Skansen lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Vasa Museum

The Vasa Museum (Vasamuseet) sits on the island of Djurgården and houses the 64-gun warship Vasa, which sank on her maiden voyage in 1628. It is the only almost fully intact 17th-century ship ever salvaged. The museum opened on 15 June 1990, and from outside it is easy to pick out. A large copper roof carries stylised masts that show how tall Vasa stood when fully rigged. Some of the walls are clad in wooden panels painted dark red, blue, tar black, ochre yellow and dark green, which gives you colour and texture to work with.

The best outdoor photo is from the waterfront edge of Galärparken, a free, leafy park right beside the museum. From there you see the roofline and masts with boats moored in the harbour in front. Four other museum ships lie in that harbour: the ice breaker Sankt Erik, the lightvessel Finngrundet, the torpedo boat Spica and the rescue boat Bernhard Ingelsson. They make good foreground interest. Leave a little space around the roof and masts so the shape reads clearly. Photographing the ship itself means going inside, where it can be seen from six levels. Check the official site for photo rules and ticket details before you go.

Where it is: On the island of Djurgården in Stockholm, on the harbour waterfront.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole roofline with the boats in front. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close and the roof and masts will not fit. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the masts and roof detail.

Where to stand: Stand about 45 m south-east of the subject, in Galärparken (the phone icon on the map), and face north-west. The museum lies to the north-west. From here the photo shows the museum's copper roof and the boats moored in the harbour in front of it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the roofline along the upper third, with the moored boats along the bottom. Keep the phone level so the masts stand upright. If you want an alternative, the museum's front exterior shows the copper bowsprit above the roof, with the harbour and vintage ships behind.

Settings tip: Tap the roof to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is washing out the copper. Take a few shots at different zoom levels, because telephoto and the default lens (1x) give quite different results here.

Best time: Softer morning or late-afternoon light usually flatters the roof and the water. Midday sun can cause glare off the harbour.

3ABBA The Museum

ABBA The Museum is an interactive exhibition about the Swedish pop band, on the island of Djurgården in Stockholm. It opened in May 2013 and sits in a building near the Gröna Lund theme park. It is the permanent home of the ABBAWORLD exhibition, which toured Europe and Australia in 2009–2011, and it holds stage costumes donated by the band members. Inside you will find a recreation of the Polar Studio, a section styled on Brighton at the 1974 Eurovision Song Contest, and Benny's self-playing piano, which is linked to the one in his home.

For a phone photographer, the outside of the building is the easy, reliable shot. It is a big, simple subject that sits well in a wide frame, and Djurgården gives it a relaxed, leisurely setting. Find a spot outside with a clear view of the facade and photograph the building as a record of your visit, perhaps with a friend in the foreground. Step back a few paces to fit more of it in, and switch on gridlines so the walls stay straight.

Inside, photography rules can differ from room to room, so look out for signs and check the official site before you go. If photos are allowed, the costumes and studio recreations make good close-up details.

Where it is: On the island of Djurgården in central Stockholm, in a building near the Gröna Lund theme park.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the building as a whole. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close and cannot step back far enough. Use the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for signs and details.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Find a spot outside the building with a clear view of the facade, and take a few steps back to fit more in.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the edges of the building with them so the walls look straight. Keep your phone level, as tilting it makes the building lean. If the area is busy, wait a moment for a gap in the crowd, or include a few people for a sense of scale.

Settings tip: Tap the building to focus, then lower the exposure slider slightly if the sky is bright, so the walls do not turn washed out.

Best time: Daytime in even light is easiest. Earlier in the day it is likely to be quieter. If you come in the evening, try night mode and hold the phone steady against something solid.

4Djurgården and the Blue Gate

Djurgården is an island in central Stockholm that mixes forest, meadow, waterfront promenades and old wooden buildings, all within a short distance of the city. This spot covers the island as a whole, with the Blue Gate (Blå Porten), the café at the Liljevalchs art gallery, as one of its landmarks. The island photographs well because the scenes change so quickly. You can have water and boats in one frame, then tall trees, historic buildings or open grass in the next. The island was once a royal game park. Its name comes from King John III's plan of 1579 to keep deer, reindeer and elk there. Later it became a popular place for recreation, and Gröna Lund opened in 1883 and Skansen in 1891. Many buildings in the western part date from the Stockholm World's Fair of 1897. The island is part of the National City Park, founded in 1995. For your pictures, look for a clear foreground such as a path, railing or tree branch, and let the water or the buildings fill the background. Walk a little along the waterfront rather than shooting everything from one place, because the island is large and every stretch looks different. Keep your phone lens clean, as spray and fingerprints soften the picture. If you want the Blue Gate itself, look for the café by the Liljevalchs gallery and take a straight-on shot of its entrance.

Where it is: Djurgården is an island in central Stockholm, with parks, museums and the Gröna Lund amusement park. The Blue Gate (Blå Porten) is the café at the Liljevalchs art gallery on the island.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the overall scene. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) for close waterfront or park views. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out buildings or boats in the distance.

Where to stand: Stand about 1.4 km north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and put the horizon on a top or bottom line so the water, trees or sky get the space they deserve. At this distance, try 2x or 3x to pull the island's shoreline and buildings closer, and keep a bit of foreground in the shot for depth. Once you are on the island itself, go back to the default lens (1x) or ultra-wide (0.5x) for paths, gates and shopfronts.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the sky or water to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky keeps its colour. Hold the phone still with both hands, and try a Live Photo on the water so you can pick the sharpest frame.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. In midday sun, look for shade under the trees.
