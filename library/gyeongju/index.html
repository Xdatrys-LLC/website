This map shows 13 of the most-searched sights in Gyeongju as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Bulguksa Temple, Seokguram Grotto, Cheomseongdae, Woljeong Bridge and Anapji lead the list. The 3 free photo spots

1Bulguksa

Bulguksa is a large Buddhist temple on the slopes of Tohamsan, and it is the most rewarding stop on this map. The site is made up of stone terraces, timber halls and gates, so there is something to photograph in almost every direction. The best-known subjects are the entrance stairway with its two bridges, Cheongungyo (Blue Cloud Bridge) and Baegungyo (White Cloud Bridge), which together have 34 steps. Beyond them are the two pagodas in the main courtyard. Dabotap is 10.4 metres tall and richly carved. Seokgatap is 8.2 metres tall and much plainer, and the contrast between the two makes a good pair of photos.

The records say the present temple was begun in 751 and finished in 774. The wooden buildings were burned during the Imjin wars and rebuilt later, with a major restoration between 1969 and 1973. The famous stonework is preserved from the original Silla construction. Bulguksa and nearby Seokguram Grotto were added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1995.

For your phone, work with the shapes: the diagonal of the stairway, the pagodas against the hall behind them, and the rooflines. Take a wide shot first to show the setting, then move closer for carved details. Keep people out of the frame by waiting a few seconds between groups. The Lotus Flower Bridge and Seven Treasures Bridge, a smaller pair to the west, are closed to walkers, so photograph them from where you can see them.

Where it is: Bulguksa stands on the slopes of Tohamsan in Jinhyeon-dong, Gyeongju, North Gyeongsang Province.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the stairway, gate and courtyard, where the buildings are close and fill the frame. Use the default lens (1x) for most general shots, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for pagoda carvings and roof details.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m west of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face east.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and line a pagoda or a hall column up with a vertical line so the stonework does not lean. Frame Dabotap and Seokgatap in separate shots first, then try one wider shot with both in it. For the stairway, crouch slightly and let the steps lead the eye up to the gate.

Settings tip: Tap on the pale stone to focus, then pull the exposure slider down a little so the sky and stone keep their detail. Use portrait mode only for people, not for the buildings.

Best time: Go early in the morning or later in the afternoon, when there are usually fewer visitors in your shots and the light is softer.

9Dabotap

Dabotap is a stone pagoda in the courtyard of Bulguksa temple in Gyeongju. It is the more decorated of the two pagodas that stand facing each other. When you come in over the Cheongun and Baegun Bridge, Dabotap is on the right and its plainer partner, Seokgatap, is on the left. Tradition says it was built in 751, in the reign of King Gyeongdeok of Silla. It is National Treasure no. 20 and stands 10.29 m tall, and its three storeys are carved in an ornate style. It has a staircase on each of its four sides, square pillars under the first roof, and an eight-sided upper section with a lotus carved with sixteen petals. Only one of the four stone lions that guarded the stairs is still in place. The pagoda also appears on the 10 won coin.

It photographs well because it is full of detail and sits on its own in an open courtyard. Take one full-height shot, then move in with zoom for the railings, roofs and lotus carving. If you want the story of the two pagodas, turn and include Seokgatap in a wider frame. Wait a moment for other visitors to move out of the shot, and keep your phone level so the pagoda does not lean.

Where it is: Dabotap is inside the grounds of Bulguksa temple, in the main courtyard just past the Cheongun and Baegun Bridge. It stands on the right-hand side, facing Seokgatap.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole pagoda from where you stand. Switch to the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the carved roofs, railings and the lion. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want the pagoda and part of the courtyard together, and keep it away from the edges of the frame, where it can stretch the shape.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face east. Keep to the visitor areas and do not climb on or lean over any railing.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a vertical grid line up with the pagoda's centre so the sides stay straight. Leave a little sky above the top. Then take a second, tighter shot using zoom for the upper roofs and the lotus carving. For a third shot, include Seokgatap on the opposite side to show the pair.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the carved detail does not wash out. Take a few frames in case people walk through.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually means softer light and fewer visitors. Midday sun can flatten the carving, so zoom in on details then.

10Seokgatap

Seokgatap is a plain, elegant stone pagoda in the grounds of Bulguksa temple in Gyeongju. It dates from the Silla period and probably from around 751, when the temple was completed. It stands 10.75 metres tall, directly across from its more ornate partner, Dabotap. The two make a striking pair: Dabotap is more elaborate, while Seokgatap is very simple. It has three storeys on a two-tier base, with no carvings on its faces, and the storeys step in at a steady 4:3:2 ratio that gives it a calm, balanced look. That clean shape is what makes it easy to photograph. It reads clearly as a single upright subject against the temple buildings and sky, and eight lotus-flower stones sit around it, which add a little foreground detail. Its fame also rests on what was found inside. During repairs in 1966, workers uncovered relics and a woodblock-printed Buddhist text, believed to be the oldest surviving printed material of its kind. For a good photo, stay at the edge of the courtyard and keep the whole pagoda in frame, vertical lines straight. Then take a second, wider shot that includes Dabotap or the roofline behind. Courtyards at temples can fill up, so be patient and wait for a gap between groups.

Where it is: Seokgatap is in the courtyard of Bulguksa temple in Gyeongju. It stands opposite Dabotap.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole pagoda. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want the pagoda, Dabotap and the buildings in one frame, and keep the pagoda away from the edges, where it can stretch. Use telephoto (2x-5x) for the stepped roof edges and the lotus stones.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. This is very close, so you will probably need the ultra-wide lens (0.5x) or a few steps back to fit it in.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the pagoda up with a vertical line so it stands straight. Hold the phone level, as tilting upwards makes the sides lean. Try one shot centred to show the symmetry, then one with Dabotap in the edge of the frame to show the contrast between the pair.

Settings tip: Tap the pagoda to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little if the sky is bright, so the stone keeps its detail. Take a few frames, as people often walk through.

Best time: Early in the day or later in the afternoon is usually calmer and the light is softer. Midday light and crowds make it harder.
