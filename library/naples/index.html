The 3 free photo spots

1Galleria Umberto I

Galleria Umberto I is a public shopping gallery in downtown Naples, directly across from the San Carlo opera house. It was built between 1887 and 1890 as part of the city's big rebuilding programme, the risanamento. The architect, Emanuele Rocco, drew on the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II in Milan. It is a tall, cross-shaped space with four glass-and-iron wings. A glass dome braced by 16 metal ribs rises above the crossing, 57 m high at the tip of its spire. Shops, cafés and flats were meant to share the building, and it is still a busy part of city life.

It photographs well because everything leads the eye upwards: curved ironwork, long arcades and a big patch of sky overhead. The phone icon on the map is at the middle of the gallery's mapped outline. Walk to the crossing under the dome and look up for the most dramatic frame, then turn to face one of the arcades for a symmetrical view with people walking through. Keep your phone level so the vertical lines stay straight, and wipe your lens first, as pockets leave smears that show up against bright glass. If you want the outside too, the wing that opens towards the San Carlo theatre gives a different view from the one on via Toledo.

Where it is: In downtown Naples, directly across from the San Carlo opera house. One wing of the gallery opens onto via Toledo (via Roma), the main downtown street.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the dome and the full height of the arcades. Switch to the default lens (1x) for the long arcade views. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details of the ironwork and the glass roof.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Then walk to the crossing under the dome if you want the dome overhead.

Framing: For the dome, hold the phone with the screen parallel to the wall and tilt up slowly, using the gridlines to keep the verticals straight. For a symmetrical arcade, stand in the middle of a wing and line the centre gridline up with the middle of the roof. Wait for a gap in the crowd, or include a few people to show the scale.

Settings tip: Tap on a darker part of the gallery to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the bright glass roof doesn't wash out. Panorama mode works for a wing, but hold the phone steady and move slowly.

Best time: Early in the day usually means fewer people. Daylight through the glass roof lights the gallery evenly, and the shops are likely to be lit in the evening.

2Spaccanapoli

Spaccanapoli is the long, straight street that cuts across the historic centre of Naples. It photographs well because it is so straight. From above, it reads as a single line drawn through a crowded patchwork of rooftops, with church domes and bell towers rising on either side. At street level the same straightness gives you a deep, narrow perspective, with buildings and laundry lines closing in on both sides.

For the classic overview, go to Largo San Martino, the open belvedere in front of the Certosa di San Martino in Vomero. Several guides call it the best view of the street's straight cut through the city. From here you look out over the rooftops towards the old town, and the street runs across the picture as a thin line. The view is high and fairly distant, so you will want your phone's telephoto lens.

If you would rather be in the thick of it, Via Benedetto Croce gives a straight-on view of the lane receding towards its eastern end. At the far end, Piazzetta Nilo frames the lane around the ancient statue of the Nile river-god. Allow time for both: the overview shows the scale, and the street-level views show the life of the place. Take care in narrow lanes with scooters and pedestrians, and stay out of the road when you line up a shot.

Where it is: Spaccanapoli runs through the historic centre of Naples, east of Dante station and north of Università station. The overview point, Largo San Martino, is up the hill in Vomero, in front of the Certosa di San Martino.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use telephoto (3x-5x, depending on your phone) from Largo San Martino to pull the straight line of the street and the domes closer. Switch to the default lens (1x) for the street-level views.

Where to stand: Stand about 1.1 km west of the subject, in Largo San Martino (the phone icon on the map), and face east. At the belvedere in front of the Certosa di San Martino in Vomero. Look east across the rooftops of the historic centre to find the straight line of Spaccanapoli with the church domes and bell towers around it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and let the straight line of the street run across the frame, with domes and bell towers as anchor points. Keep the rail or any foreground clutter out of the shot. As an alternative, Via Benedetto Croce gives a straight-on view of the lane receding eastwards. Piazzetta Nilo shows the far end around the Nile statue.

Settings tip: Tap on the rooftops to focus, then lower the exposure slider slightly so the sky doesn't wash out. Hold the phone steady with both hands, or brace it against a wall, to keep the zoomed shot sharp.

Best time: Check on the day for the best light, and try morning to see what suits. The view is also clearer on a dry day with good visibility.

3Piazza del Plebiscito

Piazza del Plebiscito is a very large, open square in central Naples, close to the Gulf of Naples. The Royal Palace closes it on the east side. On the west side is the church of San Francesco di Paola, with its curved twin colonnades reaching out to either side. The square is about 277 m across, so there is plenty of space to step back and fit things in. The statue of Charles of Bourbon on horseback stands in the middle of the paving.

It photographs well because nothing blocks the view. From the middle of the square you can look down Via Cesario Console, past the trees to the sea, with Vesuvius showing over the skyline. The colonnade and palace give strong, symmetrical lines for wider shots.

The square takes its name from the plebiscite of 21 October 1860, which brought Naples into the unified Kingdom of Italy. For a long time it was used as a car park. It was cleared and pedestrianised in 1994, when the G7 summit was held in Naples, which is why it feels so open today. The church's dome is 53 m high, so it stands out over the colonnades.

Go early, when it is quiet, and check for events first, as the square is sometimes used for concerts and can be crowded. If you want a second angle, try the curved colonnade from near the church, or the Royal Palace belvedere for a view over the whole square.

Where it is: In central Naples, close to the waterfront on the Gulf of Naples. The square sits between the Royal Palace on its east side and the church of San Francesco di Paola on its west side.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the main view down to the sea. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) when you want the whole colonnade and palace in one frame. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring Vesuvius and the far end of the street closer.

Where to stand: Stand about 55 m east of the subject, in Piazza del Plebiscito (the phone icon on the map), and face west. In the centre of the square, and face back toward the Royal Palace and the sea. From the centre you get a clear view down Via Cesario Console, with trees, the sea beyond and Vesuvius over the skyline. The edges of the square do not give the same clear line of sight.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the middle of the street with the centre of the frame, so the trees lead the eye to the water. Keep the horizon level. For a second angle, shoot the curved colonnade toward the church from near the church side, or use the Royal Palace belvedere for a high view of the square (check the official site before you go).

Settings tip: Tap on the sea or Vesuvius to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky keeps its detail. If people are walking through, the ultra-wide (0.5x) usually works better than panorama mode, because moving people can look distorted in a panorama.

Best time: Early morning, before the square fills with visitors.
