The 3 free photo spots

1Sky Tower

The Sky Tower is Auckland's tall landmark, rising 328 metres from ground level to the top of the mast. That makes it the second-tallest freestanding structure in the Southern Hemisphere. It stands at the corner of Victoria and Federal Streets in the CBD, as part of the SkyCity complex, and opened on 3 August 1997 after two years and nine months of building. The slim concrete shaft carries the observation decks and restaurants near the top, so the outline is easy to recognise even in a small phone frame.

The best photo is of the tower from outside, because you can't photograph it from inside it. Victoria Park works well. The tower rises above the green lawns, with the city's high-rises and the harbour side of the CBD around it. The grass gives you a calm foreground and the tower gives the picture a clear centre.

Hold the phone upright, since the tower is much taller than it is wide. Leave a little space above the mast so the tip isn't clipped. If you have time, two other spots are worth a look: Wynyard Crossing, for the tower as the main element of the skyline from the Viaduct side, and Federal Street, for a street-level view, often reflected in nearby glass. The tower is also lit up for charities and causes, and for New Year fireworks, so an evening visit can give a very different picture.

Where it is: The Sky Tower is in the middle of Auckland's CBD, at the corner of Victoria and Federal Streets, as part of the SkyCity complex. Victoria Park, where the photo is taken, lies to the west of it, also in the CBD.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x), held upright, to take in the tower with the lawns below it. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for more grass and sky, or zoom to 2x-3x (if your phone has a telephoto) to fill the frame with the shaft and the pod.

Where to stand: Stand in Victoria Park. The tower rises above the green lawns of the park, with the city's high-rises and the harbour side of the CBD around it. From there the tower rises above the green lawns, with the high-rises and the harbour side of the CBD around it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the shaft up with a vertical line so it doesn't lean. Put lawn or a tree in the lower third and leave a little space above the mast. For other angles, try Wynyard Crossing for the skyline from the Viaduct side, or Federal Street for the tower reflected in glass.

Settings tip: Tap on the pod near the top of the tower to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the sky stays blue and the tower keeps its detail. If you shoot at dusk, try night mode and hold the phone steady against something solid.

Best time: Late afternoon often gives softer light on the tower and the lawns. Dusk is a good time to catch the tower lit up.

2Maungawhau / Mount Eden

Maungawhau / Mount Eden is a grassy volcanic cone, and at 196 metres it is the highest natural point on the Auckland isthmus. That height is why it makes such a good photo spot. You get a deep, bowl-shaped crater close at hand, with the city skyline and the Sky Tower rising behind it. The crater is about 50 metres deep, and its smooth green sides give the foreground a clean, simple shape.

The name means 'mountain of the whau tree'. The mountain was once an important Māori fortified settlement, and the terraces and storage pits on the upper slopes are still protected. Boardwalks around the crater rim were opened in 2020 to look after them, so please stay on the boardwalk. The summit road has been closed to most vehicles since 2011.

For the classic shot, use the boardwalk platform closest to the access road. Reviewers say it gives the best view of the city and the Sky Tower, with the crater bowl in front. Include a strip of the grass in the bottom of the frame to give the picture depth. The summit is open and exposed, so allow a minute or two to line things up. If the platform is busy, wait for a gap rather than shooting over people's shoulders. For a quieter picture of the cone itself, try Mount Eden Road, which shows the green slopes rising above the street without the summit crowds.

Where it is: Maungawhau / Mount Eden sits in the suburb of Mount Eden, on the Auckland isthmus. The summit looks out over the whole city.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole crater bowl and the skyline in one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view of the city. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the Sky Tower and the city towers.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From here the crater's grassy bowl fills the foreground, with the Auckland skyline and Sky Tower behind it.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on the upper line. Keep the crater rim as a curve leading towards the skyline, and put the Sky Tower on one of the vertical lines rather than in the middle. For a quieter alternative, take a 1x or 2x shot of the green cone from Mount Eden Road.

Settings tip: Tap the Sky Tower or the skyline to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour. At dusk, use night mode and rest your elbows on the rail to keep the phone steady. Wait for the countdown to finish before moving.

Best time: Sunset or sunrise, which several guides single out for photos.

3Karangahape Road

Karangahape Road, usually just called K' Road, is one of Auckland's main streets. It follows a ridge along the southern edge of the city centre, so a photo along it shows the street climbing away between older buildings. The classic view is from the corner looking west. The historic H B Building stands on one side and the Naval and Family Hotel on the other, with the Family Bar sign far down the street. Photos from this same corner appear in 1909 postcard views, so you are shooting a very old composition.

The road is older than Queen Street. Māori used the ridge as a walking route long before Europeans arrived, and the ridge was known as Te Ara o Karangahape, 'The Path of Karangahape'. The meaning of the name itself is uncertain. From about 1900 to the early 1960s it was Auckland's busiest shopping street. In 1935 the whole street was lit with lights under the shop awnings, known as the 'Great White Way'. Today it is known for off-beat cafes and boutique shops.

For a phone photo, wait for a gap in the traffic and people, and look for the lines of the old facades leading your eye up the street. If the signs or shopfronts are the main interest, step back and use the buildings as a frame. Some parts are busy, and the area has people sleeping rough, so be thoughtful when people are in your shot. Building work for the new City Rail Link station near the Upper Queen Street end may change some views, so check on site.

Where it is: Karangahape Road runs west to east along a ridge at the southern edge of Auckland's city centre, crossing Queen Street's line at right angles. It runs from Ponsonby Road in the west to Grafton Bridge in the east.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural view of the street. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want both rows of buildings and more sky, but keep the phone level so the walls don't lean. Try the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pull the distant Family Bar sign closer and squeeze the street together.

Where to stand: Stand at the corner of Karangahape Road and Pitt Street (corner, looking west along the strip). The street running away up the ridge, framed by the historic H B Building on one side and the Naval and Family Hotel on the other, with the Family Bar sign in the distance. From here you see the street running away up the ridge, with the historic H B Building on one side and the Naval and Family Hotel on the other, and the Family Bar sign in the distance.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and put the street's centre line near the middle, with the two buildings as framing on either side. Tap the far end of the street to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little if the sky is bright. For a different view, St Kevin's Arcade has heritage architecture, vintage signs and mural-covered walls. Grafton Bridge shows the city skyline and its concrete arch.

Settings tip: Wait for a quiet moment so cars and people don't cover the buildings. If you want a shot with people in it, take a few quick Live Photos or a short burst and choose the best one afterwards. In the evening, use night mode and hold the phone steady against a post or wall.

Best time: Daytime gives the clearest view of the building details. Late afternoon to evening can be good for lit signs and shopfronts, though the low sun may be in front of you when looking west.

Where should I stand to photograph the Sky Tower?
