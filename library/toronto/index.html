The 3 free photo spots

1CN Tower

The CN Tower is Toronto's signature landmark, a 553.3-metre communications and observation tower that was completed in 1976. It stands on the former Railway Lands in downtown Toronto, and its name comes from Canadian National, the railway company that built it. For 32 years, from 1975 until 2007, it was the world's tallest free-standing structure. It still photographs well because of its simple outline: one slender concrete shaft, a pod near the top and a thin antenna above. Up close it is almost too tall to fit in a phone frame, so the trick is to step back. Roundhouse Park is a short walk from the base, with open space around you and room to include the whole tower from bottom to antenna. Keep the phone upright, use the gridlines to line the shaft up with a vertical line, and leave a little sky above the antenna. A bit of the park, a path or a bench in the lower part of the frame gives a sense of scale. If you want a second angle, Harbourfront Park on the waterfront offers a symmetrical view across open lawns. Please be aware that the observation levels are a paid attraction, and the tower itself is not visible from inside it.

Where it is: The CN Tower is in downtown Toronto, on the former Railway Lands just south of Front Street and close to the Harbourfront. Roundhouse Park sits a short walk south-east of the tower's base.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole tower in one frame, then try the default lens (1x) for a cleaner look with less stretching at the edges. Telephoto (2x-5x) is good for a close-up of the pod and antenna.

Where to stand: Stand in Roundhouse Park (a short walk south of the tower, away from its base). The whole tower fits in frame from a little distance, with room to use a wide-angle lens. From there the tower is to the north-west, and standing back from its base lets the full structure fit in the frame, with space around you away from the busiest crowds near the entrance.

Framing: Hold the phone upright, switch on the gridlines and keep the tower's shaft on one vertical line so it doesn't lean. Leave a little sky above the antenna and use the park's grass or paths at the bottom of the frame. For a different view, Harbourfront Park gives a direct, symmetrical line of sight to the tower across open lawns.

Settings tip: Tap on the tower to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky stays blue and the shaft keeps its detail. Wipe the lens first, as ultra-wide shots show smudges easily.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually means softer light and fewer people. At dusk, steady your phone and use night mode for the tower against the darkening sky.

2Casa Loma

Casa Loma is a Gothic Revival, castle-style mansion on a hill in midtown Toronto. It was built between 1911 and 1914 as a home for the financier Sir Henry Pellatt, to designs by the architect E. J. Lennox. With 98 rooms, it was the largest private residence in Canada when it was built, and it now operates as a museum. It sits 66 metres above Lake Ontario, which is why the towers and battlements stand out so clearly above the neighbouring streets.

It photographs well because the stonework, turrets and towers give you plenty of shapes to work with, and the quiet, tree-lined residential street in front gives a pleasing setting. From Austin Terrace you can frame the front of the castle without going inside, with the towers rising above the trees. If you want a different feel, the Baldwin Steps climb the slope towards the castle's western side, and Spadina Park shows the building on its grassy rise with more space around it.

The formal front gardens are not open all year, and the view from the top of the towers needs a ticket, so keep your plans flexible. Take a few frames from the street first, then decide whether to go in. Wipe your phone lens before you start, as city dust and fingerprints show up as haze on pale stonework.

Where it is: Casa Loma stands on a hill in midtown Toronto, north-west of Dupont station and north of Spadina Road. Austin Terrace is the quiet residential street beside it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole front of the castle. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the street is narrow and you cannot fit the towers in. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for towers, windows and stonework.

Where to stand: Stand on Austin Terrace (street-side view looking southeast at the castle's front). The castle's front facade framed by the tree-lined residential street, with the building's towers and stonework in view. From here you see the castle's front facade framed by the tree-lined street, with the towers and stonework above. Keep to the pavement and stay clear of driveways.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a tower up with a vertical grid line so the walls don't lean. Let the trees and the street frame the castle at the sides. For a different angle, try the Baldwin Steps, which lead up towards the castle's western side, or Spadina Park for the castle on its grassy slope. Check both positions on the map first.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky doesn't wash out and the walls keep their colour. Take a few shots at 0.5x, 1x and 2x so you can pick the best one later.

Best time: Try mid to late afternoon, when the front of the castle may catch softer light. Trees along the street can screen the building when in leaf, so you may get a clearer view of the stonework in late autumn, winter or early spring.

3Royal Ontario Museum (the Crystal)

The Royal Ontario Museum is one of the largest museums in North America, and its Michael Lee-Chin Crystal is the part that stops people on the pavement. Daniel Libeskind designed it as a cluster of tilted, angular prisms of aluminium and glass. It was added to the older stone museum building, which opened in 1914, and the Crystal's architectural opening took place on 2 June 2007. The pairing is why it photographs so well: sharp modern shapes sit right up against heavy, rounded stonework, and both fit in one frame.

From there you look south across the Crystal's northern face, with the older stone wing beside it. The contrast between old and new reads most clearly from this spot. Dusk is the favourite time, when the lit glass glows against the darkening sky.

The museum is also being renovated at the moment, with work continuing into 2026, so parts of the building may look different from older pictures. There you can see the Crystal's angles and the north front of the East Wing together along the line of the street. Watch for pedestrians and keep your phone close to you on a busy pavement.

Where it is: The museum is in Toronto's University district, just north of Queen's Park and next to the University of Toronto's St. George campus. Its main entrance is on Bloor Street West.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to show the Crystal beside the older stone wing. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the whole tilting form in one frame from the pavement. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for close shots of the angled glass and edges.

Where to stand: Stand at the corner of Bloor Street West and Queen's Park (sidewalk at the southwest corner, looking across the Crystal's northern face). The angled aluminum-and-glass prisms of the Crystal rising over Bloor Street, set against the older stone wing of the museum. From here you look across the Crystal's northern face, with its angled aluminium-and-glass prisms rising over Bloor Street and the older stone wing of the museum beside it. As an alternative, try Bloor Street West and Avenue Road for the Crystal's sharp angles and the East Wing's north front along the street line.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and line up the stone wing and the Crystal so each fills about half the frame, to show the contrast between old and new. Keep the phone level so the vertical lines don't lean too much, and use 0.5x only when you can't fit the whole building in at 1x. From the Avenue Road corner, use the line of the street as a lead-in towards the Crystal.

Settings tip: Tap on the lit part of the glass to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the bright panels don't wash out. At dusk, turn on night mode and hold the phone steady against a lamp post or your own arms to keep the lines sharp.

Best time: Dusk, when the lit glass glows against the darkening sky.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Toronto?

The first three on this map are CN Tower, Casa Loma and Royal Ontario Museum. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 20.

Where should I stand to photograph the CN Tower?

Stand in Roundhouse Park (a short walk south of the tower, away from its base). Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole tower in one frame, then try the default lens (1x) for a cleaner look with less stretching at the edges.

When is the best time to photograph Casa Loma?

Try mid to late afternoon, when the front of the castle may catch softer light. Trees along the street can screen the building when in leaf, so you may get a clearer view of the stonework in late autumn, winter or early spring.

How do I get to the Royal Ontario Museum?

Take the Line 1 subway to Museum station, whose north-western entrance serves the museum directly.
