This map shows 14 of the most-searched sights in Kobe as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Harborland, Nankinmachi Chinatown, Kobe Port Tower, Meriken Park and Ikuta Shrine lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Harborland

Harborland is a shopping district in Chuo-ku, Kobe, and it is where Kobe Port Tower stands. The area has a tidy, modern feel, so it suits easy phone photography: wide open spaces, big buildings and plenty of lines to build a picture around.

There is some history behind the view. The land was once a freight yard belonging to the Japanese National Railways. The yard was removed in 1982 and the shopping district opened in 1992.

The phone icon on the map is not in the middle of Harborland. It is about 449 m to the north-east of the district's centre point, so you see Harborland from a little way off, with more of its surroundings in the frame. Treat it as a place to take in the whole scene before you walk closer for details.

From the phone icon on the map, start with the default lens (1x) for a straightforward view of the district. Then try the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the sky and the surroundings. The telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) is useful for picking out the Port Tower and other distant shapes without walking any closer. Turn on the gridlines so the horizon and the tall buildings stay straight. Once you have the wide view, walk into the district for close-ups of the shopfronts and people strolling.

Where it is: Harborland is in Chuo-ku, Kobe, on the site of a former railway freight yard. Kobe Port Tower is in the district.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the overall view. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for more sky and surroundings, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out distant details such as the Port Tower.

Where to stand: Stand about 450 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower third line so buildings and sky stay balanced. Keep the phone level so tall structures do not lean. Take one wide shot to show the setting, then zoom in for a tighter picture of the main shapes. Afterwards, walk into the district for close-ups.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the sky or the main building to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky looks washed out. In the evening, switch to night mode and hold the phone steady for a moment.

Best time: Morning or early evening usually gives softer, more even light than midday. After dark, the district is worth a try with night mode if the lights are on.

2Nankin-machi

Nankin-machi is Kobe's Chinatown, a compact network of streets packed with Chinese restaurants, food stalls and shops. It began in 1868, when Kobe's port opened to foreign traders and Chinese settlers made their home at the western edge of the foreign district. The area was destroyed in the Second World War and rebuilt by the Chinese community that stayed on. It was badly hit again by the 1995 Great Hanshin earthquake, then rebuilt once more.

It photographs well because so much happens within a few steps. Three decorative archways mark the way in: Chang'an Gate to the east (put up in 1985), Xi'an Gate to the west and Nanlou Gate to the south. A pair of lions guards the northern entrance. At the crossing of the two main streets stands a pavilion with stone carvings of the 12 zodiac animals, a popular spot for photos. Between the gates you can shoot food on display, such as dumplings, buns and pastries, along with shopfronts and busy street scenes.

Take a few photos here, then walk to a gate or the pavilion for a different view. Start with the wide street scene, then move in close on the food and the carvings.

Where it is: Nankin-machi is in central Kobe, just south of Motomachi station and next to the Daimaru department store. It is the city's Chinatown.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the gates and the packed streets, and the default lens (1x) for everyday scenes. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the zodiac carvings, lions and food on display.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Turn slowly and look down each street before you choose a shot.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and keep the gate or shopfront straight to your lines. Frame a gate with a little street life in front of it to give a sense of scale. Then go in close and fill the frame with one dish, one carving or one lion.

Settings tip: Tap the food or carving to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if bright shopfronts wash out the picture. Use portrait mode for close-ups of food. In the evening, try night mode and hold the phone still against a wall or railing.

Best time: Go earlier in the day for emptier streets and easier wide shots. Late afternoon into evening suits atmospheric shots with the shops lit up.

3Kobe Port Tower

Kobe Port Tower is a slim red landmark that rises 108 m above the port. It was designed by Nikken Sekkei and completed in 1963. Its shape is meant to recall a tsuzumi, a Japanese hand drum, and it was the first tower built using a pipe lattice. The structure is wrapped by 32 red steel staves, said to symbolise a welcome for vessels returning to shore. That curved, open framework makes a clean silhouette, and the red stands out well against sky or water. The tower also has an LED lighting scheme, installed in 2010, with 40 lighting effects, so it can look quite different after dark. It reopened on 26 April 2024 after a long renovation and seismic work. The phone icon on the map is about 190 m north-east of the subject. That distance suits a single, tidy shot with the tower as the clear subject, and it lets you include some foreground for a sense of place. Keep the phone upright to fit the full height, and use the gridlines to keep the tower's vertical lines straight. If you want a tighter picture, step in closer or use the telephoto lens rather than cropping later.

Where it is: Kobe Port Tower is in Chuo-ku, Kobe, Hyogo Prefecture, in the port area of the city. It is a landmark of Kobe.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the whole tower with some surroundings. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter, cleaner shot of the tower's shape, and use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you move close and want the full height.

Where to stand: Stand about 190 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. The tower is to the south-west of you, so face that way.

Framing: Hold the phone upright and turn on the gridlines. Line the tower up with a vertical grid line and keep the phone level so the sides don't lean. Leave a little sky above the tip, and include something in the foreground to give a sense of scale.

Settings tip: Tap on the tower to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the red stays rich and the sky does not wash out. After dark, use night mode and hold the phone steady against something solid.

Best time: Late afternoon through dusk is a good choice: the light is softer, and you can stay on for the tower's lighting as the sky darkens.
