The 3 free photo spots

1Dejima

Dejima was a small artificial island built in Nagasaki in 1636 to hold foreign traders. For 220 years it was Japan's main channel for trade and cultural exchange with the West, and the Dutch were based here from 1641 until 1858. The island measured only about 120 m by 75 m and was joined to the mainland by one small bridge. Land reclamation later folded it into the city, so today you are looking at a historic site within Nagasaki rather than a true island. It was named a national historic site in 1922, and work continues to bring back its island setting.

It suits a phone because the story is told by the setting: a compact, enclosed site with a clear sense of place. Stay back from the middle and take a wide view first, then move in for details such as rooflines, doorways, signs and any flags. The phone icon on the map is about 20 m east of the subject. From here you can take a wide first view of the site. Keep the horizon level with the gridlines on, and tap on the building you want so the exposure is set for it, not for the sky. Access to parts of the site may be ticketed or restricted, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: Dejima is in Nagasaki, Japan, on the site of the former artificial island that is now part of the city after land reclamation.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the overall scene. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you move closer and want to fit in more of the site. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as roof edges, windows and signs.

Where to stand: Stand about 20 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west. From here you can take in a wide view of the site before you walk in closer.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the horizon level. Frame the whole site with the default lens (1x), then take a second shot with a doorway, wall or other foreground element in the edge of the frame for depth. Finish with a tighter telephoto shot of one detail.

Settings tip: Tap on the main building to focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is bright, so pale walls don't wash out. Use portrait mode only for a person standing in the foreground.

Best time: Morning is a good choice, as the sun is in the east behind you when you face west from where you stand. Overcast days also work, because the light is even and the walls show no harsh shadows.

2Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum

The Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum is a place of remembrance for the atomic bombing of the city on 9 August 1945, at 11:02 in the morning. It was completed in April 1996 and replaced the older International Culture Hall, where relics from the bombing had first been shown. It stands in Nagasaki Peace Park, beside the Nagasaki National Peace Memorial Hall for the Atomic Bomb Victims, which was built in 2003. Inside, the story runs from the city before the bomb through the aftermath and rebuilding to today's calls for peace. One of the objects on show is a clock stopped at 11:02.

As a photograph, the museum works best as a quiet, respectful record of the place rather than a showy picture. The building and its neighbour make a calm, well-ordered scene, and the surrounding park gives you room to step back and fit the walls into the frame. Keep people in the shot to a minimum and avoid anything that feels staged. This is a site of mourning for many visitors.

For your phone, use the exterior of the building and the park around it. Photography rules inside are not covered here, so check the official site before you go and follow any signs once you are in the galleries.

Where it is: The museum is in Nagasaki, in Nagasaki Peace Park, next to the Nagasaki National Peace Memorial Hall for the Atomic Bomb Victims.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the building as you see it. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close and cannot fit the whole front in. Use the telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for details such as the entrance or the lines of the walls.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. To photograph it, walk out into the surrounding park and turn back to face the building.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the building's edges with them so the walls look straight. Hold the phone level, and include a little open ground or sky to give a sense of calm. For a second picture, move sideways so the neighbouring Peace Memorial Hall appears alongside, which shows how the two buildings sit together. Keep strangers out of the frame, or let them be small and unidentifiable.

Settings tip: Tap the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the walls keep their detail. Skip portrait mode and heavy filters; a plain, natural image suits the subject better.

Best time: Daylight with soft or evenly overcast light tends to give the cleanest, least glaring pictures of the building. Earlier or quieter parts of the day usually mean fewer people in the frame.

5Urakami Cathedral

Urakami Cathedral, also called the Immaculate Conception Cathedral, is the Catholic cathedral of Nagasaki, and its twin-towered brick-coloured front makes a strong, simple subject for a phone photo. The story behind it is part of why people stop here. Local Christians, whose faith had been banned and who were sent into exile between 1869 and 1873, began building the first cathedral in 1895 on land where they had once been forced to tread on sacred images. When it was finished in 1925 it was the largest Christian building in the Asia-Pacific region. The atomic bomb of 9 August 1945 exploded only about 500 m away and destroyed it. The congregation rebuilt on the same site, finishing in October 1959, and in 1980 the walls were re-faced with brick tiles to look more like the original. Today a bell and the damaged statue known as the Atom-bombed Mary are displayed in the grounds, so there is more to see and photograph once you are close. For photographs, the front with its two towers is the natural frame. It is a working place of worship and a memorial, so keep your photography quiet and unobtrusive, and avoid pointing your phone at people who are praying.

Where it is: In the Motoomachi area of Nagasaki, in the Urakami district. The Nagasaki Peace Park is nearby.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: From where you stand, use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the cathedral closer. Switch to the default lens (1x) as you get nearer, and use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you can stand close to the front and want both towers and the entrance in one frame.

Where to stand: Stand in 平和公園 (Nagasaki Peace Park) (beyond the Peace Statue, on the far side of the park). A distance view of the rebuilt cathedral standing on its hill, with the surrounding neighbourhood below it. At that distance the building will be small at 1x, so zoom in or walk closer to the grounds for a bigger picture.

Framing: Centre the twin towers and turn on gridlines so the verticals stay straight. Hold the phone level, because tilting it up makes the towers lean inwards. Once you are in the grounds, a second photo of the bell or the Atom-bombed Mary can add context to the story of the place.

Settings tip: Tap the cathedral front to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright and the brickwork is turning dark. A sharper, steadier shot comes from holding the phone with both hands and resting your elbows against your body.

Best time: Soft morning or late-afternoon light usually flatters brick-coloured walls and avoids harsh midday shadows. Early in the day there are also likely to be fewer people in your frame.
