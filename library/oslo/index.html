This map shows 19 of the most-searched sights in Oslo as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Oslo Opera House, Munch Museum, Viking Ship Museum, Vigeland Park and Akershus Fortress lead the list. The 3 free photo spots

1Oslo Opera House

The Oslo Opera House is the home of the Norwegian National Opera and Ballet. It sits in the Bjørvika neighbourhood at the head of the Oslofjord. Snøhetta designed it, and it opened on 12 April 2008. Its sloping white surfaces are covered in Carrara marble and white granite, so the whole building seems to rise straight out of the water. That clean, angled shape is what makes it so easy to photograph.

One guide suggests a quieter alternative view from Langkaigata, across the harbour, which shows the front of the building with its reflection in the water. The waterfront promenade gives you a foreground, and the same guide describes it as lined with floating saunas, which adds some local colour. It is also a quieter place to stand than the building itself.

The roof slopes down to ground level so that visitors can walk up it, which makes a good second set of pictures. From the top you get a wide view over the fjord and the Barcode towers. The two views complement each other: the reflection shot shows the building as a whole, and the roof shot shows what surrounds it.

White marble can look flat or too bright on a phone, so check the exposure before you take the picture. The reflection also looks best when the water is calm.

Where it is: The Opera House is in the Bjørvika neighbourhood of central Oslo, on the waterfront at the head of the Oslofjord.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole front, its reflection and some of the promenade in one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a tidier, more natural view. If your phone has a telephoto (2x-3x), use it to pick out the marble slopes and the roofline.

Where to stand: Stand on Langkaigata (waterfront promenade across the harbour from the building, looking back at its front). The front of the Opera House with its reflection in the harbour water, framed by the promenade. On the waterfront promenade across the harbour from the building, and look back at its front.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the water's edge on the lower third line, so the building and its reflection fill the frame evenly. Keep the phone level so the angled roof and the horizon look straight. For a second shot, go over to the Opera House itself and photograph the white marble slope rising out of the harbour, with the fjord and the Barcode towers behind it.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the white marble keeps its detail. Take a few frames in case ripples spoil the reflection.

Best time: Afternoon, when the sun falls on the front of the building. Calm weather gives the clearest reflection.

2Munch Museum

The Munch Museum, marketed simply as MUNCH, is the big art museum on the Bjørvika waterfront, right beside the Oslo Opera House. It is dedicated to Edvard Munch and holds almost 28,000 artworks, which moved here from the old museum at Tøyen before the new building was opened by King Harald V on 22 October 2021. The building is tall and tilted, so it stands out clearly against the harbour and the city behind it.

From the raised ground there you look across the water, and the museum becomes a distant, modern shape on the waterfront. That distance is the point of the shot: the old stone fortress, the open harbour and the museum all sit in one frame, with the old and the new side by side.

On a phone, the museum will look small at the default lens (1x). Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring it closer, and keep a strip of water in the foreground so the picture has depth. If you want a different view, the Operahuset is a good second viewpoint. From there you can frame the museum's tilted tower against the Barcode high-rises and the fjord.

Take a few shots at different zoom levels. Then pick the one where the building and the harbour feel balanced.

Where it is: The museum is in Bjørvika, on the Oslo waterfront next to the Oslo Opera House.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to see the whole scene, then switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to make the museum bigger in the frame. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of harbour and sky.

Where to stand: Stand in Akershus Festning. The museum seen across the harbour from the fortress grounds, with the building as a distant subject. From the fortress grounds the museum is across the harbour to the east, seen as a distant building on the waterfront. Keep the main view over the water clear, so nothing blocks the line between you and the museum.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the museum on one of the vertical lines, with the water along the lower third. A small piece of fortress wall or a tree at the edge of the frame can add depth, as long as it does not cut across the view of the museum. For a different view, try the Operahuset, where the tilted tower can be framed against the Barcode high-rises and the fjord.

Settings tip: Tap on the museum to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky is bright so the building does not turn into a silhouette. Hold the phone with both hands when using telephoto, as zoom makes every shake more visible.

Best time: Late afternoon or early evening is likely to give the best light, because the sun is then behind you and falls on the museum's face. Soft, clear light after sunrise or before sunset works well too.

3Viking Ship Museum

The Viking Ship Museum (Vikingskipshuset på Bygdøy) stands on the Bygdøy peninsula in Oslo and is part of the Museum of Cultural History of the University of Oslo. It was built to shelter three Viking-era burial ships, from Tune, Gokstad and Oseberg. The Oseberg ship is the best known, and it came from the largest known ship burial in the world. The building is also a story in itself. Arnstein Arneberg won the architectural contest, the Oseberg hall was ready for the ship's move in 1926, and the halls for the Gokstad and Tune ships followed in 1932. The last hall was finished in 1957, after delays that were partly due to the Second World War. The outline of the complex is about 103 m across, so it is a big, calm subject for a phone. For the best photo, step back from the entrance and let the building fill the frame, with the sky above it and a little foreground below. Keep the phone level so the long walls stay straight. Because the museum is temporarily closed from September 2021 until 2027, interior photos are not possible at present, and it is worth checking the official site for what is open and what you can see before you go.

Where it is: The museum is on the Bygdøy peninsula in Oslo.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) when you are close to the walls, then switch to the default lens (1x) as you step back for a calmer, truer-looking view. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as rooflines and entrance features.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Walk back a little from the walls until the whole front fits in the frame.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the roof and walls with them so the building stays straight. Leave a strip of ground in the foreground for depth. If the building is too large to fit, take a panorama and move the phone slowly and steadily.

Settings tip: Tap the building to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky looks too bright. Tap again if the exposure shifts when you move the phone.

Best time: Soft morning or late-afternoon light usually flatters a building like this. Early in the day also tends to be quieter.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Oslo?

The first three on this map are Oslo Opera House, Munch Museum and Viking Ship Museum. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 19.

Where should I stand to photograph the Oslo Opera House?

Stand on Langkaigata (waterfront promenade across the harbour from the building, looking back at its front). Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole front, its reflection and some of the promenade in one frame.

When is the best time to photograph the Munch Museum?

Late afternoon or early evening is likely to give the best light, because the sun is then behind you and falls on the museum's face. Soft, clear light after sunrise or before sunset works well too.

How do I get to the Viking Ship Museum?

Head for the Bygdøy peninsula in Oslo, and look for Vikingskipshuset på Bygdøy.
