The 3 free photo spots

1Ritsurin Garden

Ritsurin Garden is a large, historic strolling garden in Takamatsu, laid out around ponds, little hills and winding paths, with the wooded slope of Mt. Shiun ("Purple Cloud Mountain") rising behind it. Work began in 1625 under the local feudal lord Ikoma Takatoshi, and successive lords kept improving it until it was completed in 1745. It opened to the public in 1875 and was named a Special Place of Scenic Beauty in 1953. The garden covers 750,000 square metres, so you will not see it all in one frame. The southern part is in the traditional Japanese style, with old teahouses and pine trees clipped into careful shapes. That is where phone photos tend to work best. Still water gives you reflections, the pines give strong shapes, and the mountain gives a calm green backdrop. The ponds also hold koi, which can add a bit of life to a close shot. Take your time and walk a little between pictures, because the view changes at almost every bend in the path. Keep the phone steady with both hands, and wait a moment for other visitors to move out of the shot.

Where it is: Ritsurin Garden lies in Takamatsu, on the east side of Mt. Shiun, which forms its backdrop. It sits in a former river bed, so the mountain is always close by.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the wide, natural view of pond, pines and mountain. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) when you are close to a pavilion or a clipped pine and want to fit it all in. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a single pine, a teahouse roof or koi at the water's edge.

Where to stand: Stand about 30 m south-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-east.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and put the water's edge on a lower line, with the trees and Mt. Shiun in the upper part of the frame. Keep the horizon level. Include a branch or a pine in the foreground to give a sense of depth. For a wider scene, try a slow panorama along the water, moving the phone smoothly.

Settings tip: Tap on the main subject to focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is bright, so the greens and reflections stay rich. If you shoot koi, try a Live Photo, then choose the best frame afterwards. Wipe the lens before you start.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people, and a calm day gives the clearest reflections.

2Takamatsu Castle

Takamatsu Castle sits right on the edge of the Seto Inland Sea, in front of Takamatsu port, and is now a public park. It is also known as Tamamo Castle, a name that refers to its moats, which are filled with seawater. Only three castles in Japan are known to use seawater moats, so the water is part of what makes this spot different. For a phone photo, the combination works well: dark stone walls, still water, pine-dotted banks and old wooden turrets all fit into one frame.

The castle was begun in 1588 by Ikoma Chikamasa and finished by 1590. The whole site covered about 400 by 200 metres. The main keep was taken down in 1884 because of its age, so you will not see a tall tower. Instead, look for the surviving Edo-period turrets and gate buildings, such as the Tsukimi Yagura and the Ushitora Yagura, which date from the late 1670s. The Sakuragomon gate, burned in the Second World War, was rebuilt in 2022. Also in the grounds is the Hiunkaku, a former Matsudaira family villa built in 1917.

Take your time and walk the paths beside the water, because the best angles change every few steps. Use the moat as a foreground and let the stonework lead the eye towards the turrets.

Where it is: In central Takamatsu, Kagawa Prefecture, on the Shikoku island coast beside Takamatsu port. The castle is located on the coast of the Seto Inland Sea, in front of Takamatsu port.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the walls, water and turrets together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close to the moat and want more stone and sky. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details of the roof tiles and the timber turrets.

Where to stand: Stand about 75 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west. Pause here first, then move a few steps along the path to try different angles on the water and walls.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the line of the moat edge on the lower third, with the turret or wall above it. Include some water in the foreground so the stone has a reflection or a sense of depth. Keep your phone level so the walls do not lean.

Settings tip: Tap on the stonework to set focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is washing out. If the water is calm, wait for a still moment before you press the shutter.

Best time: Morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light on the stone, and fewer harsh shadows than midday.

3Takamatsu Symbol Tower

Takamatsu Symbol Tower is a 30-storey skyscraper in Takamatsu, Kagawa Prefecture, finished in 2001 and standing 151 metres tall. What makes this spot work is the walkway on the west side. From there you can fit the tower, the lit trees around its base and a distant strip of sea into a single frame. A travel blogger calls it a favourite, and notes it is much quieter than the big-city sights in Tokyo or Osaka.

Because the tower is so tall, the main job is deciding how much of it to include. Stand back along the walkway and keep the whole building in the picture, with the trees in the foreground and the water behind. The phone icon on the map is right at the tower, so you do not need to hunt for a separate viewing place.

If you want a different angle, two alternatives are worth a look. The rooftop of the neighbouring Sunport Takamatsu building gives a lower view of the tower. Takamatsu Port is another viewpoint for the tower. Keep your phone lens clean, and have a quick look at the edges of the frame for lamp posts and signs before you tap the shutter.

Where it is: In Takamatsu, beside the Sunport Takamatsu complex. The photo spot is the pedestrian walkway on the tower's west side.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the tower, the lit trees and the sea together. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close and cannot fit the whole tower in. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the top of the tower or to pick out the sea in the distance.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From here the tower is in the foreground, with the lit trees around it and the sea in the distance.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the tower up with a vertical grid line so it does not lean. Keep the trees low in the frame and let the sea sit as a thin band behind. Hold the phone level, as tilting it upwards makes the tower lean inwards. For a different view, try the rooftop of the neighbouring Sunport Takamatsu building for a lower angle, or Takamatsu Port for another view of the tower.

Settings tip: Tap the tower to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the sky stays blue and the lights do not wash out. If you shoot after dark, switch on night mode and hold the phone steady against a railing or with both elbows tucked in.

Best time: The lit trees are part of the view, so the lights should be visible after dark, but check locally for the lighting times if you plan around them. Daytime also works for a clear view of the tower and the sea.
