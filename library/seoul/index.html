The 3 free photo spots

1Gyeongbokgung Palace

Gyeongbokgung was the first royal palace of the Joseon dynasty, established in 1395, and it is now one of the most significant tourist attractions in South Korea. It photographs well because the grounds are large and open, with long stretches of tiled roofs, stone paving and timber halls that give your pictures plenty of depth and pattern. The palace flourished under Sejong the Great, and Hangul, the Korean script, was developed here during his reign. It has a turbulent story too. It was burned down in 1592 during the Imjin War and left unused for around 270 years, then rebuilt in the 1860s. During the colonial period most of its roughly 500 buildings were sold off or demolished, and by 1945 only 40 pre-colonial buildings remained. Restoration since the 1980s has recreated dozens of them. For photos, plan on a mix of wide views and close details. Start with a wide shot to show the scale of the courtyards, then move in on painted eaves, doorways and stonework. A changing of the guard ceremony takes place twice a day, which adds colour and movement if you time your visit for it. Please be considerate when people wander into your frame. Check the official site for current visiting arrangements before you go.

Where it is: Gyeongbokgung Palace is in Seoul, South Korea, and covers a large area.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the main view. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want a courtyard and the buildings around it in one frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for roof ends, painted beams and carved details.

Where to stand: Stand about 70 m south of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the main hall or gate on a vertical line, keeping the horizon level so the long rooflines do not slope. Include a bit of paving in the foreground to add depth. Take one wide shot and then zoom in for a few detail shots. Wait a moment for people to pass if you want a clearer view.

Settings tip: Tap on the building to set focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is bright, so the roof colours do not wash out. Try a Live Photo for the guard ceremony or for moving crowds.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. The palace is open at night for part of the year, so check the official site for dates before planning an evening visit.

2Sungnyemun (Namdaemun)

Sungnyemun, also called Namdaemun or the South Great Gate, is the southern gate of the old Seoul City Wall. It stands in Jung District, with the busy Namdaemun Market right beside it. The gate was first built in 1398 and rebuilt in 1447. It was named South Korea's National Treasure No. 1 in 1962. In February 2008 a fire destroyed the wooden upper storey, and after a long restoration the gate reopened in May 2013. It photographs well because the two-tiered tiled roof sits in the middle of a modern city of traffic, towers and market streets. That contrast is the picture.

From up there you look down over the station and the streets towards Namdaemun Market, and the gate appears as part of the wider scene rather than filling the frame. This makes a calmer alternative to standing at street level. For a close, full view of the gate, walk over and shoot from outside it. It is framed by the surrounding city and lit up in the evening. Keep the gate itself in the frame rather than cropping the roof, and use the city lights around it to add depth.

Where it is: Sungnyemun is in Jung District in central Seoul, between Seoul Station and Seoul Plaza, with Namdaemun Market next to it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole scene of station, streets and gate. Then try telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to bring the gate closer. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want the walkway and the city in the same frame.

Where to stand: Stand in 서울로7017 (elevated walkway over Seoul Station, reached by lift, stairs or ramp). A looking-down view over Seoul Station and the streets toward Namdaemun Market, with the gate in the wider scene.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the gate on one of the lines, with the streets leading towards it. If the gate looks small, zoom in rather than walking away from the railing. For a full, close view of the two-storey wooden roof, shoot from outside the gate itself instead.

Settings tip: Tap the gate to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the lit roof and city lights do not wash out. In the evening, switch on night mode and rest your elbows on the railing to keep the phone steady.

Best time: Evening or night, when the city-view lighting is on and the gate is lit up.

3Namdaemun Market

Namdaemun Market is a big, busy traditional market right beside Namdaemun, the main southern gate of the old city. It opened in 1414, in the Joseon period, which makes it one of the oldest markets in Korea that still operates. Fires have destroyed it several times, including one during the Korean War, and each time it was rebuilt to roughly the same size. Today it is modernised but still lively. A 2016 count by the Seoul Institute listed about 5,200 stores and 9,090 workers across roughly 64,600 square metres. That scale is what makes it good for photos. You get crowded lanes, stacked goods, hanging signs and people at work, all in strong colour and close to hand. A phone suits this kind of picture well. Take one wide shot to show the busy lane, then move in on the details: a pile of goods, a handwritten sign, a vendor's hands. Keep your elbows in, step to the side of the walkway before you stop, and ask before you photograph anyone up close, since these are people's workplaces. Check the exposure after each shot, because bright signs and dark awnings can fool the camera.

Where it is: In central Seoul, next to Namdaemun, the main southern gate of the old city.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for lane scenes. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) in tight, crowded aisles, and to telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for stall details without leaning into people's space.

Where to stand: Stand on Kalguksu Alley (narrow lane of noodle stalls inside Namdaemun Market). Vendors' bowls of handmade noodle soup in a narrow lane, with rising steam and colorful ingredients in the foreground.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a lane up with the centre so it leads the eye into the picture. Take one wide shot of the whole scene, then a closer one of a single stall or product. Keep the phone straight to avoid leaning walls.

Settings tip: Tap on a mid-tone area to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so bright signs and lights don't wash out. Try portrait mode on goods piled on a stall, and use Live Photos for moving crowds.

Best time: Earlier in the day usually means fewer people and easier framing, and the market has long been known for its morning trade. Daylight also keeps colours true, so use night mode only if you shoot after dark.
