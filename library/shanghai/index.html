The 3 free photo spots

1Shanghai Tower

Shanghai Tower is the tallest building in China: 128 storeys and 632 metres, standing in Lujiazui, in the Pudong district. It is the tallest of a close trio of supertall towers, alongside the Jin Mao Tower and the Shanghai World Financial Center. Planners drew up that grouping as far back as 1993. Building work began in November 2008 and the structure topped out in 2013. Its sightseeing decks on the 118th and 119th floors have been fully open since April 2017. A higher deck, "Top of Shanghai" on the 121st floor at 562 metres, was later added.

The best way to photograph it is from across the Huangpu River, on the Bund (外滩). From there the Lujiazui towers line up along the far bank, with the river in front and the old Bund facades behind you. It is the classic postcard view, and the tower's height and tiered shape stand out clearly against the sky. Most photographers set up at the stone barrier at water level, so arrive a little early if you want a clear spot at the railing. Keep your phone level so the tower does not lean, and include a strip of river at the bottom of the frame to give the picture depth. If you want a different angle, Lujiazui Central Green (陆家嘴中心绿地) shows all three towers together with trees in the foreground.

Where it is: Shanghai Tower is in Lujiazui, in the Pudong district of Shanghai, on the east bank of the Huangpu River. The best viewpoint is on the opposite, western bank, on the Bund waterfront near the Meteorological Signal Tower on Zhongshan East 1st Road.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to take in the river and the whole row of Lujiazui towers. Switch to telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to isolate Shanghai Tower and pull it closer. Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of waterfront and sky around the towers.

Where to stand: Stand on 外滩 (Bund waterfront on the west bank, near the Meteorological Signal Tower on Zhongshan East 1st Road). Shanghai Tower across the Huangpu River, the Lujiazui towers lined up on the far bank, with the river and the historic Bund facades behind you. Face across the river: Shanghai Tower is to the north-east, with the other Lujiazui towers lined up beside it and the historic Bund facades behind you. Most photographers use the stone barrier at water level.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the tower on a vertical line, with the river filling the bottom third. Hold the phone upright and level so the tower does not lean. For a different view, try Lujiazui Central Green (陆家嘴中心绿地), where the three towers rise together with trees in the foreground, or the crossing of Dongtai Road and Huayuan Shiqiao Road (东台路与花园石桥路交叉口), shooting low and upward.

Settings tip: Tap the tower to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the lit windows and sky colour do not wash out. At dusk, let night mode do its work and rest your elbows on the barrier to keep the phone steady. Tripods may be discouraged by security staff in parts of Lujiazui, so shoot handheld.

Best time: About 20 to 40 minutes after sunset, when the sky still has colour and the towers are lit. This depends on clear weather, as haze can dull the view.

2Yu Garden

Yu Garden is a classical Chinese garden in the Old City of Shanghai, packed with curved-roof halls, ponds, zigzag bridges and rockeries in a space of about 2 hectares. Pan Yunduan began it in 1559, in the Ming dynasty, as a comfort for his father in old age. Work was held up for years and the garden was finished in 1577. It opened to the public in 1961 and became a national monument in 1982. It photographs well because so much sits close together: grey-tiled 'dragon walls' with wavy ridges, carved gateways, and the 12-metre Grand Rockery. A prized porous stone called the Exquisite Jade Rock, about 3.3 metres tall, is another highlight. Just outside, the Nine-turn bridge and a teahouse pond lead towards the entrance, and the lively Yuyuan Bazaar surrounds the garden with shops and snack stalls. That mix of calm garden and busy street gives you plenty of different pictures. On the map, the phone icon on the map is a short step from the garden's centre, so start with the broad view of roofs and walls. Then move in for details such as roof corners, doorways and reflections in the water. Keep your lens clean, as crowds and humidity can smear it. Check the official site before you go to see what is open and how entry works.

Where it is: Yu Garden is in the northeast of the Old City of Shanghai, in Huangpu District, right beside the City God Temple. The Yuyuan Bazaar and the Huxinting Teahouse lie next to it.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole scene of roofs, walls and water. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) in tight courtyards, and use telephoto/zoom (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for roof ridges and carvings.

Where to stand: Stand about 55 m south-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-west.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and place a roofline or dragon-wall ridge along one of the horizontal lines. Include a little water or a bridge in the foreground for depth. Then zoom in on a single roof corner or doorway for a simpler picture. Keep the phone level so the buildings do not lean.

Settings tip: Tap to focus on the buildings, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so bright sky does not wash out the roofs. If the app offers Live Photos, they can catch people moving across a bridge.

Best time: Early in the day is usually calmer for photos, and soft light or gentle overcast suits the grey tiles. Evening, when lanterns and lights come on, suits night mode.

3The Bund

The Bund is the long riverside promenade in central Shanghai, with a line of grand early 20th-century bank and trading-house buildings on one side and the Huangpu River on the other. Across the water stand the glass towers of Lujiazui in Pudong. That pairing of old stone facades and a modern skyline is why the view is so popular. The Bund runs for about 1.6 km, from Yan'an Road in the south to Waibaidu Bridge in the north, and has some 52 buildings in a mix of Neoclassical, Gothic, Art Deco and other styles. Some echo the waterfront of Liverpool. The riverside embankment was raised about 10 metres above street level after typhoon floods, and the promenade was widened and reopened in March 2010. From here you can see the colonial facades at the left edge of the frame, the river in the centre and the Pudong skyline on the right. It is a busy spot, so arrive early to claim a place at the railing. If you want a different angle, try Waibaidu Bridge for its red-brown steel frame with the towers beyond, or the Lujiazui waterfront for the reverse view back at the Bund's lit facades.

Where it is: The Bund is on the western bank of the Huangpu River in the eastern part of Huangpu district, central Shanghai, directly opposite Lujiazui in Pudong.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the classic river-and-skyline view. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the crowd is close and you want to keep the facades, river and towers all in one frame. Use telephoto (2x-5x) to pick out the Pudong towers or a single facade detail.

Where to stand: Stand in 陈毅广场 (Middle of the Bund promenade, near Bund 9–12, facing the river). A full frontal view of the Bund's colonial facades on the left edge, the river in the centre, and the Pudong skyline across the water on the right. From here the Bund's colonial facades fill the left edge, the river runs through the centre and the Pudong skyline sits on the right. It is the most reproduced Bund angle, so expect company at the railing.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the river on the lower third, keeping the skyline level. Include a little of the facades at the left to give a sense of scale. Panorama mode can work for the full sweep if people are not passing in front of you. For a different view, try Waibaidu Bridge with the towers beyond.

Settings tip: Tap on the sky or the towers to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the lit buildings do not wash out. In dim light, hold the phone against the railing or your body and let night mode work for its full count.

Best time: Blue hour is the recommended time, when the facades and towers are lit against a darkening sky. Arrive early to claim a spot. The Bund's lights are reported to go off around 22:00, and light-show schedules can change, so don't leave it late.
