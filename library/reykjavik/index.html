The 3 free photo spots

1Sky Lagoon

Sky Lagoon is a geothermal spa on the waterfront at Kársnes Harbour in Kópavogur. Construction began in early 2020 and was completed in 2021. The design takes its cues from Icelandic landscape and heritage. The palette is grey-blues, deep greens, whites and creams, and there are turf walls, a turfhouse and a plunge pool. Visitors enter through a cave tunnel that leads to the hot soaking pool. The lagoon water is kept at roughly 38 to 40 °C, so on a cool day you can expect some steam, which suits photos. Snæfellsjökull, the glacier-capped volcano, and the mountain Keilir can both be seen from the lagoon.

The phone icon on the map is about 65 m north-east of the subject. That is enough distance to take in the whole setting, with the low buildings, turf-covered edges and the sea behind them, rather than a close detail. Start with a wide shot that shows how the spa sits against the water and sky, then move in for the textures of the turf walls and the cave entrance. Keep the horizon level using your gridlines. Be considerate of bathers: frame your shots so that other guests are small or out of the picture, and check the official site before you go for any rules on photography.

Where it is: Sky Lagoon is at Kársnes Harbour in Kópavogur, on the coast of south-western Iceland. Kópavogur is the town next to Reykjavik, so this is just outside the city rather than in a central district.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural view of the spa and its setting. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more sky and shoreline, and use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the turf walls or the entrance.

Where to stand: Stand about 65 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower third line, depending on whether the sky or the spa is the more interesting part. Include the turf edges and the water to show the landscape-inspired design. If the weather is clear, look for Snæfellsjökull or Keilir in the distance and add them to a wide shot.

Settings tip: Tap the spa to focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky or steam is washing out. If you want to capture steam drifting, try a Live Photo and pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Soft light near sunrise or sunset usually flatters the buildings and the sky. In winter, the low sun means this light lasts longer, and the spa's lighting may show up well once it gets dark, though night shots need a steady hand or night mode.

2Hallgrímskirkja

Hallgrímskirkja is the big, pale concrete church that rises above the roofs of central Reykjavík. Its stepped tower and swept-back side wings are meant to recall the basalt columns, mountains and glaciers of the Icelandic landscape. That strong, simple outline is why it photographs so well on a phone: it stands out clearly against the sky and works as a single bold shape.

It is a Lutheran parish church, the largest church in Iceland, and at 74.5 metres tall the second tallest building in the country. It stands on the Skólavörðuholt hill and can be seen from much of the city. The church is named after the poet and cleric Hallgrímur Pétursson. Building took 41 years, from 1945 to 1986, but the steeple and wings were finished in 1974, long before the nave was consecrated.

Hold the phone upright for the tower, then turn it sideways to take in the wings. The statue of Leif Erikson stands in front of the church and can make a good foreground, although you will need to walk round to it. The church also has a lift up to a viewing deck, and its bells ring through the day, so it is worth spending a little longer here than the first photo takes.

Where it is: Hallgrímskirkja stands on the Skólavörðuholt hill, near the centre of Reykjavík, and is visible from many parts of the city.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole tower and the wings into one upright shot. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a tidier, less stretched view. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out the stepped concrete columns of the spire.

Where to stand: Stand about 80 m north of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a vertical grid line up with the tower so the sides stay straight. Keep the phone level and upright so the building does not seem to lean backwards. Leave some sky above the spire. For a second shot, turn the phone sideways to include the wings, or move closer with the statue of Leif Erikson in the foreground.

Settings tip: Tap on the tower to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the pale concrete keeps its detail and the sky does not turn white. Use Live Photos or motion if people are walking past, and pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Try early morning or late afternoon, when the light is softer and the concrete shows more texture. The church can be photographed at any time of year, but check the weather first, because a clear sky shows the shape of the tower best.

10Statue of Leif Erikson

Leif Erikson stands on a tall granite pedestal shaped like the prow of a Viking ship, looking out over the city, with the stepped front and tall tower of Hallgrímskirkja rising directly behind him. That pairing is why the spot works so well on a phone: dark bronze in the foreground, pale church behind, and a clear sky above both.

The statue is by the American sculptor Alexander Stirling Calder. The United States gave it to the people of Iceland to mark the 1,000th anniversary of the Alþingi, the Icelandic parliament, in 1930. It was unveiled on 17 July 1932 on the hill of Skólavörðuholt. The pedestal is about 4.6 metres high, made of Texas red granite, and carries an inscription about the gift.

The best-known view is from Hallgrímstorg, the square in front of the church. Stand close to the base of the pedestal and face the church, so the figure sits in the foreground and the whole height of the façade fits behind him. For a quieter, church-focused alternative, the green garden at the Einar Jónsson Museum gives a side view of Hallgrímskirkja. Another option is to go down Skólavörðustígur and look back up at the statue and plaza, with the street running away towards downtown.

The square can be busy, so allow a little patience for a clear frame. Take a few shots with and without people in them, as visitors also give a sense of scale.

Where it is: The statue is in central Reykjavik, on the square called Hallgrímstorg on the hill of Skólavörðuholt. It stands directly in front of Hallgrímskirkja.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to get the statue and church together. If you can stand a little back, try telephoto (2x or more) so the church looks larger behind the figure. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you are right at the pedestal and want the church's full height.

Where to stand: Stand in Hallgrímstorg (stand right at the base of the pedestal, facing the church). The bronze figure in the foreground with the full height of Hallgrímskirkja's stepped façade and tower rising behind him. From there, walk to the statue for the classic shot: stand right at the base of the pedestal, facing the church. The bronze figure fills the foreground and the full height of Hallgrímskirkja's stepped façade and tower rises behind him.

Framing: Hold the phone upright (portrait) to fit the figure and the tower in one frame, and turn on gridlines to keep the church's vertical lines straight. Tilt the phone as little as you can, or the tower will lean. For something different, try the side view of the church from the Einar Jónsson Museum garden. Or go down Skólavörðustígur and shoot back up at the statue and square.

Settings tip: Tap on the bronze figure to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is bright, so the statue does not turn into a silhouette. If your phone has Live Photos or motion, leave it on, as it can help you pick a frame without people in it.

Best time: Early morning tends to mean fewer people. Sunset is said to give softer light on the bronze.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Reykjavik?

The first three on this map are Sky Lagoon, Hallgrímskirkja and Phallological Museum. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 20.

Where should I stand to photograph Sky Lagoon?

Stand about 65 m north-east of Sky Lagoon, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. Start with the default lens (1x) for a natural view of the spa and its setting.

When is the best time to photograph Hallgrímskirkja?

Try early morning or late afternoon, when the light is softer and the concrete shows more texture. The church can be photographed at any time of year, but check the weather first, because a clear sky shows the shape of the tower best.

How do I get to Leif Erikson Statue?

There are no Underground or tram stops for this spot, so start from the centre of Reykjavik and head uphill towards the tall church tower, which is easy to spot.
