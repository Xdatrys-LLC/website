The 3 free photo spots

1Roman Baths

The Roman Baths are a well-preserved Roman bathing complex in the middle of Bath. A temple was built here between 60 and 70 AD, and the Roman town of Aquae Sulis grew up around it. The hot spring still rises on the site. The water comes from rain that has soaked deep into the limestone of the Mendip Hills and been heated underground.

For a photographer, the appeal is the contrast between the Roman remains and the grand building around them. The buildings at street level are mostly 19th century. The visitor entrance is an 1897 concert hall with a glass-domed centre and a curved corner. Next to it is the Grand Pump Room, finished in 1799, whose front on Abbey Church Yard has four Corinthian columns and a pediment. This front is the easiest shot from outside, and the stone looks warm and even in good light.

Stand back far enough to fit the whole front, then move in for details such as the columns and the curve of the dome. Inside, you can tour the baths and museum, but you cannot enter the water. Check the official site before you go for entry arrangements and for any rules on photography indoors.

Where it is: In the centre of Bath, Somerset, on Abbey Church Yard beside the Grand Pump Room. A bridge of the museum and Queen's Bath building spans York Street.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole front when you are close. Use the default lens (1x) for the general view, and the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the columns, pediment and dome.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Step back a little along Abbey Church Yard to take in the Grand Pump Room front and the entrance building together.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the columns upright against the vertical lines. Try one wide shot of the whole front, then a tighter one of the Corinthian columns and pediment. If people are walking past, wait a moment for a clear gap.

Settings tip: Tap the stone front to focus, then slide the exposure down slightly if the sky is bright, so the detail in the stonework is kept. Indoors, where it is dimmer, hold the phone steady with both hands and let night mode work if it switches on.

Best time: Early in the morning or later in the afternoon, when there are usually fewer people in front of the building.

2Thermae Bath Spa

Thermae Bath Spa is where Bath's old spa tradition meets a very modern building. The main block, the New Royal Bath, is a glass-enclosed cube built in Bath stone and designed by Grimshaw Architects. It sits among Georgian streets and neighbours the Georgian Cross Bath, which is grade I listed. That contrast between clean glass and honey-coloured historic stone is what makes it such a good subject for a phone. The spa reopened in 2006, ending a 28-year period when bathing in the city's hot spring water was not allowed. The water comes from the only naturally hot, mineral-rich springs in the UK, and the council has been its guardian under a royal charter of 1590. Inside the glass cube there is an open-air rooftop pool, though the pools themselves are for paying spa guests, so for most visitors the photo is of the building from the street. Look for reflections in the glass, where the older buildings nearby show up as softer shapes. Step back a little to fit the whole cube in, then try a second shot closer in, aiming at the meeting point of the stone and the glass. Keep the phone level so the vertical lines stay straight. Please be considerate of spa guests, and do not photograph people inside or on the rooftop.

Where it is: In the centre of Bath, in the Georgian streets of the old spa quarter, close to the historic Hot Bath and Cross Bath buildings.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole glass cube. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if the street is too narrow to step back. Use telephoto (2x-5x) for details of the glass and stone.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. Choose a place on the nearby public street from where the whole cube fits in the frame.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line up the edges of the cube with the vertical lines so the walls do not lean. Include a slice of the Georgian stone buildings beside it to show the contrast between old and new. For a second shot, go close with 2x and frame the join between glass and Bath stone.

Settings tip: Tap on a mid-tone part of the building to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky or the glass is washing out. Ultra-wide shots from close up can bend vertical lines, so keep the phone upright.

Best time: Soft morning or late-afternoon light is kind to the stone, and glass reflections are often clearest on bright but not harsh days. At dusk the lit-up interior can glow against the darkening sky, so try night mode, holding the phone steady against a wall or railing.

3Bath Abbey

Bath Abbey is the great Gothic church at the heart of the city, and its west front is the view most visitors want. The west front has sculptures of angels climbing two stone ladders, representing Jacob's Ladder, which make a lovely detail to look for. The church was founded in the 7th century, rebuilt in the 12th and 16th centuries, and restored by Sir George Gilbert Scott in the 1860s. It is a fine example of Perpendicular Gothic, which means tall windows and strong vertical lines. Inside it is famous for its fan vaulting, and it is still an active place of worship that seats up to 1,200 people.

The building photographs well because the open square in front gives you room to see the whole façade in one frame. A few people or a street performer in the foreground add life and give a sense of scale. The square can get busy, so be patient and wait for a gap. For a quieter shot, try the York Street archway for a tighter, more detailed frame, or Church Street for a slightly wider view with the small fudge shop in the foreground.

Where it is: Bath Abbey stands in the centre of Bath.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the west front. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you cannot step back far enough to fit the whole façade in. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the carved angels on the ladders and the window tracery.

Where to stand: Stand in Abbey Churchyard (in front of the Abbey's main west entrance). The full Gothic west front of the Abbey, framed by the open square, with the option of adding people or street performers in the foreground. In front of the Abbey's main west entrance. This gives the classic straight-on view of the full Gothic west front, framed by the open square. The Abbey is to the east of you.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line up the edges of the towers with the vertical lines so the building does not lean. Keep your phone upright and level, and avoid tilting it up, which makes the walls lean inwards. Let a few people or a street performer into the lower part of the frame for life and scale. For a tighter, more detailed shot, try the York Street archway. For a slightly wider view with the fudge shop in the foreground, try Church Street.

Settings tip: Tap the stonework to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the pale sky does not wash out the detail. If people walk through your shot, take a Live Photo or a quick burst and pick the best frame.

Best time: Go early in the morning for fewer people in the square. The west front is likely to catch the light in the afternoon and evening.
