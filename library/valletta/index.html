The 3 free photo spots

1St John's Co-Cathedral

St John's Co-Cathedral is a Catholic church in Valletta, built by the Order of St John between 1573 and 1578 and designed by the Maltese architect Girolamo Cassar. From the street it looks plain and almost fortress-like: a calm, well-proportioned stone façade with a bell tower at each side and an open balcony above the doorway, from which the Grand Master once addressed the crowds. That sober front is part of the fun, because the inside is a complete contrast. In the 1660s the artist Mattia Preti redecorated the interior in rich Baroque style, with carved stone walls and a painted vaulted ceiling showing scenes from the life of John the Baptist. The painted figures beside the columns are made to look like three-dimensional statues, so look closely. The marble floor is a patchwork of tombs, said to hold about 400 knights and officers of the Order. This is why the cathedral photographs so well: you get a strong, simple exterior and a golden, ornate interior in one stop. On the outside, stand back far enough to fit both bell towers and keep the vertical lines straight. Inside, work with the ceiling, the long view down the nave and the patterned floor. A phone copes well with all three if you hold it steady. Rules on photography inside can change, so check the official site before you go.

Where it is: St John's Co-Cathedral stands in Valletta, Malta, where the Order of St John commissioned the cathedral after the Great Siege of 1565.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) for the nave and ceiling, then switch to the default lens (1x) for the façade and for calmer, less stretched views. If your phone has a telephoto (2x-5x), use it for carved details and ceiling figures.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. For the façade, walk out to the street or square outside and step back until both bell towers fit.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and keep the two bell towers level with the lines so the façade does not lean. Inside, stand in the centre of the nave and aim along it for a symmetrical shot, or tilt up for the painted vault. Include a patch of the tomb-covered floor in the foreground for depth.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest part of the scene to focus, then slide the exposure down a little so the gold and carved stone keep their detail. Inside, brace your elbows against your body and hold still, as the light is dim and night mode may start up. Panorama can work outside but tends to bend the interior lines.

Best time: Visit early or late in the day, when it is often quieter, so you can shoot the interior without too many people in the frame.

14Collegiate Parish Church of St Paul's Shipwreck

The Collegiate Parish Church of St Paul's Shipwreck is a Roman Catholic parish church and one of Valletta's oldest. It honours St Paul, whose shipwreck on Malta is told in the Acts of the Apostles. The church goes back to the 1570s, was designed by Girolamo Cassar and was finished in December 1582. The tall, narrow facade you see today is later: it was rebuilt in 1885 to a design by Nicholas Zammit. The twin belfries and the Baroque front are what make it worth a photo. The church is squeezed between old stone buildings on a narrow street, so the picture is of a slim, richly detailed front framed by plain walls on either side. Inside are an altarpiece by Matteo Perez d'Aleccio and a wooden statue of St Paul carved in 1659 by Melchiorre Cafà. The statue is carried through Valletta's streets on the feast day, 10 February. The street is tight, so a clean, straight-on shot takes some care. Cross to the far side of the narrow street and step back as far as you comfortably can before you raise your phone. Keep the facade upright and wait for passers-by to clear the doorway. If you want a casual second angle, you could try Republic Street (Triq ir-Repubblika) and look down St Paul Street to see the front at an angle among its neighbours. Photography inside may be allowed for personal use except during services, so check on the day.

Where it is: On St Paul Street (Triq San Pawl) in Valletta, wedged between the old stone buildings that line the narrow street.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) if the street feels too tight to fit the whole facade, then try the default lens (1x) from the far side. Use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the belfries and the carved detail.

Where to stand: Stand on Triq San Pawl (opposite the church facade, across the narrow street). The high, narrow Baroque facade and twin belfries of the church, framed by the street's old stone buildings. Opposite the church facade across the narrow street. From here the church is to the north-west, and you can see the high, narrow Baroque facade and twin belfries framed by the street's old stone buildings.

Framing: Step back to the far side of the street to get the whole front in. Switch on gridlines and line the facade up with the vertical lines so the walls do not lean. Include a little of the neighbouring stonework on each side to show how closely the church is hemmed in. For a casual alternative, you could try Republic Street (Triq ir-Repubblika) and photograph the front at an angle down St Paul Street.

Settings tip: Tap on the facade to set focus, then nudge the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stonework keeps its detail. If you use the ultra-wide, keep the facade away from the edges of the frame to limit stretching.

Best time: Daylight is easiest, when the facade is evenly lit. In a narrow street the light can change quickly, so check the screen and retake if one side is in deep shade. In the evening, try night mode and hold the phone steady against a wall or railing.

16National Library of Malta

The National Library of Malta, known locally as the Bibliotheca, stands on Republic Square in the middle of Valletta. It was founded in 1776 by Grand Master Emmanuel de Rohan-Polduc. The building was designed by the architect Stefano Ittar, and his son Sebastiano carried on the work after Stefano died. It was finished in 1796 but not opened until 1812, and it is an early example of neoclassical architecture in Malta. That helps your photo: the front is calm and symmetrical, with Doric and Ionic columns, an open loggia under the first floor and a balustraded balcony above the central doorway. The square gives you space to stand back and take it all in. The statue of Queen Victoria in the foreground, the trees and the café terraces make the picture feel like Valletta rather than a plain shot of a facade. The library is a Grade 1 national monument. Photography is not allowed inside, so keep your pictures to the outside. Republic Street is the city's busiest street, so expect people walking through your frame. Take a wide shot first, then move closer for the arches and the doorway behind them. Shooting through a gap between passers-by usually works better than waiting for the square to empty.

Where it is: In the centre of Valletta, on Republic Square (Misrah ir-Repubblika), close to the Grandmaster's Palace.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the full facade with the statue in front. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you have to stand close and want to keep the whole front in the frame. Use telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for the arches and the doorway.

Where to stand: Stand in Misrah ir-Repubblika (across the square from the main facade, with the Queen Victoria statue in the foreground). The neoclassical front of the library framed by the Queen Victoria statue and the square's trees and café terraces. Across the square from the main facade, with the Queen Victoria statue in the foreground. From here your photo shows the neoclassical front framed by the statue, the square's trees and the café terraces.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the columns up with the vertical lines so the building doesn't lean. Put the statue slightly off-centre, in the lower part of the frame, and let the facade fill the rest. For an alternative, walk closer in the square and take a tighter shot of the arcaded entrance. Or take a street-level view of the facade from Triq ir-Repubblika, the main shopping street.

Settings tip: Tap the facade to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little if the sky is washing out. This keeps the stonework looking rich rather than flat. Take two or three frames, because people keep walking through.

Best time: Go early in the day or later in the afternoon if you want fewer people on Republic Street and in the square.

When is the best time to photograph St Paul's Shipwreck?

How do I get to the National Library?
