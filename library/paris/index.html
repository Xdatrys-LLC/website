This map shows 20 of the most-searched sights in Paris as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Eiffel Tower, Arc de Triomphe, Louvre, Catacombs and Notre-Dame lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. Directions and Navigate buttons take you to the standing point on foot or by public transport. The first 3 spots are free to view; the full map is for members.

The 3 free photo spots

1Eiffel Tower

The Eiffel Tower is the lattice iron tower on the Champ de Mars that most people picture when they think of Paris. Gustave Eiffel's company built it between 1887 and 1889 as the centrepiece of the 1889 World's Fair. Parisians nicknamed it "La dame de fer", the Iron Lady. It stands 330 metres tall, with a square base 125 metres on each side, and it is still the tallest structure in Paris.

The phone icon on the map is about 600 m north-west of the subject. That distance helps a phone photographer. You are far enough back to fit the whole tower in one frame, from the four legs and arches at the base to the aerial at the top. You are still close enough for the ironwork to look sharp and detailed. The tower's shape is simple and symmetrical, so a straight, steady shot usually looks good.

Hold the phone level and use the gridlines to keep the tower upright. Leave a little space above the tip, because tilting up to fit the top in makes the legs lean inwards. If people are walking in front of you, wait for a gap or include them to give a sense of scale. Take a few frames at different zoom levels, since the tower looks quite different with sky around it than when cropped tightly on the girders.

Tickets are sold for going up the tower.

Where it is: The Eiffel Tower stands on the Champ de Mars in Paris.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) to fit the whole tower in the frame from where you stand. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you want a lot of foreground or sky. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to isolate the upper section or the ironwork.

Where to stand: Stand about 600 m north-west of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line one of the tower's vertical edges up with a grid line. Keep the phone upright and level, and leave a small gap of sky above the tip. For a variation, turn the phone sideways and include trees or people in the foreground.

Settings tip: Tap the tower to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the sky keeps its colour and the ironwork stays dark and sharp. Use portrait mode only if a person stands close to you, as it will not work on the distant tower.

Best time: Early morning or the hour before sunset usually gives softer light and fewer people. After dark, switch on night mode and rest your elbows on a wall or railing to keep the phone steady.

2Arc de Triomphe and the Champs-Élysées

The Arc de Triomphe stands at the western end of the Champs-Élysées, in the middle of the big star-shaped junction now called Place Charles de Gaulle, where twelve avenues meet. Napoleon commissioned it in 1806 after the victory at Austerlitz, but it was not finished until 1836. It is about 49.5 m tall and nearly 45 m wide, so it is a big, simple shape that is easy to recognise in a photo. Beneath the vault lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier from the First World War, marked by an eternal flame. The arch is also the central point of the Axe historique, the long line of monuments and grand streets running from the Louvre out to La Défense.

It photographs well because the Champs-Élysées leads the eye straight to it. The phone icon on the map is about 95 m east of the subject. That is far enough to fit the whole monument in the frame, with the tree-lined pavements and traffic leading towards it. The sculpture and engraved names are easier to see up close, so walk nearer afterwards for detail shots. Keep an eye on traffic and other visitors on the pavements, and keep your phone held firmly near busy kerbs. If you plan to go inside the arch, check the official site before you go.

Where it is: In the west of central Paris, where the 8th, 16th and 17th arrondissements meet. The Arc de Triomphe sits at the western end of the Champs-Élysées, in the middle of Place Charles de Gaulle.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole arch with the avenue leading to it. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the trees, street and sky around it. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the sculpture and the top of the arch.

Where to stand: Stand about 95 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the arch's centre line on the middle vertical line, with the avenue's trees and lamp posts leading in on both sides. Hold the phone level so the arch's sides stay upright. Try portrait orientation to include the full height, or landscape for the wider street scene.

Settings tip: Tap the arch to focus and drag the exposure slider down slightly if the sky is bright, so the stonework keeps its detail. In the evening, use night mode and hold the phone steady against a lamp post or your body.

Best time: Morning to early afternoon usually works best, as the side facing the Champs-Élysées is lit. Early morning also tends to be quieter. In the evening, use night mode.

3Louvre and its Pyramid

The Louvre Pyramid is the glass entrance to the Louvre Museum, set in the palace's main courtyard, the Cour Napoléon. Architect I. M. Pei designed it, and it was completed in 1989 as part of the Grand Louvre project. Three smaller pyramids stand around it, and on three sides there are low triangular reflecting pools with fountains. That mix of clear glass and old palace stonework is why it photographs so well: the pyramid reflects the sky and the palace wings, and the pools add a second layer of reflections.

It is 21.6 metres tall, with a square base 34 metres along each side. It is made of 673 glass panes (603 rhombus-shaped, 70 triangular), and the Louvre itself confirms that count. The pale, repeating diamond pattern gives you strong lines to work with, and the older buildings behind it make a good backdrop.

The phone icon on the map is about 200 m west of the subject. From there you see it at a distance, with the palace around it, rather than filling the frame. That works best with your phone's zoom. For close-up shots of the glass pattern, walk into the courtyard and shoot upwards. Bear in mind that this is the museum's main entrance, so it may be busy around the base. Check the official Louvre site before you go if you plan to go inside.

Where it is: The pyramid is in the Cour Napoléon, the main courtyard of the Louvre Palace, in the centre of Paris.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Use the default lens (1x) for a wider view with the palace around it. Save the ultra-wide (0.5x) for close-up shots in the courtyard.

Where to stand: Stand about 200 m west of the subject, at the west end of the Cour Napoléon by the Arc du Carrousel (the phone icon on the map), and face east. The whole pyramid sits in front of you with the palace wings around it.

Framing: Switch on the gridlines and put the pyramid on one of the lower third points, so the palace roofline and the sky fill the rest of the picture. Keep the phone level so the pyramid's base stays straight. For a close-up, use the ultra-wide (0.5x) and tilt up slightly. Keep the base level so the glass diamonds make clean diagonal lines.

Settings tip: Tap on the pyramid to focus, then lower the exposure slider a little so the bright glass and sky keep their detail. Try a Live Photo to catch people moving past the pools.

Best time: Early morning or around sunset usually gives softer light and fewer people. After dark, try night mode with the phone held steady against a wall or railing, if the pyramid is lit when you visit.

Questions

What are the top photo spots in Paris?

The first three on this map are Eiffel Tower, Arc de Triomphe and Louvre. They are ranked by how often people search for them. The full map has 20.

Where should I stand to photograph the Eiffel Tower?

Stand about 600 m north-west of the Eiffel Tower, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-east. Start with the default lens (1x) to fit the whole tower in the frame from where you stand.

When is the best time to photograph the Arc de Triomphe?

Morning to early afternoon usually works best, as the side facing the Champs-Élysées is lit. Early morning also tends to be quieter.

How do I get to the Louvre?

Take Métro line 1 or Métro line 7 to Palais Royal - Musée du Louvre.
