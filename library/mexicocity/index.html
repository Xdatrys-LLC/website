The 3 free photo spots

1Frida Kahlo Museum

The Frida Kahlo Museum, better known as La Casa Azul (the Blue House), is the cobalt-blue house in Coyoacán where the artist was born, grew up and later died. It was built in 1904 and has since been simplified to the plainer facade you see today. Diego Rivera gave the house and its contents to be turned into a museum in her honour, and it has been open as one since 1958. It is among the most visited museums in Mexico City, with around 25,000 visitors a month, so expect company. The house is built around a central courtyard garden, but from the street you see only the strong blue walls.

It photographs well because the colours do the work for you. The deep blue is set off by red trim, green barred windows and a large tree overhanging the street, and the corner position lets you show two walls at once. The best view is from the corner of Londres and Allende, where the full facade fits in one frame. If the entrance is busy, Calle Allende gives a calmer view of the blue wall from the side. Weekday mornings are quieter than weekends and holidays, when the queue at the entrance can be long. Keep your phone level so the vertical lines stay straight, and leave a little pavement and sky in the frame so the house does not look cramped.

Where it is: The museum is in Colonia del Carmen, in the Coyoacán borough of Mexico City. It stands on the corner of Londres and Allende streets.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the whole corner facade. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if the pavement is narrow or you want to include the tree and the sky. Use 2x if you want a closer shot of the windows and trim without stepping into the road.

Where to stand: Stand at the corner of Londres and Allende (corner of the cobalt facade facing the Londres and Allende junction). The full cobalt-blue corner facade of Casa Azul with red trim, green barred windows and a large tree above the street. Stand on Londres y Allende, at the corner of the cobalt facade facing the junction. From this corner you see the whole blue corner facade, with its red trim, green barred windows and the large tree above the street.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and line the corner of the house up with one vertical line so the walls stay straight. Keep the tree at the top of the frame and leave a little pavement at the bottom. For a quieter alternative with fewer people in the shot, walk along Calle Allende and photograph the blue wall from the side.

Settings tip: Tap the blue wall to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the blue stays rich and the sky does not wash out. Try a Live Photo if people are passing, so you can pick the best moment afterwards.

Best time: Late afternoon, roughly 3:30 to 5:00 pm, should give side light on the facade, but check it on the day. Weekday mornings are calmer for crowds, though the entrance side may be in shade.

2Plaza Garibaldi

From there the nine large letters spelling GARIBALDI sit beside the maguey plant, with mariachi groups and other performers usually in the frame, so the sign, the people and the atmosphere all read at once. It is the postcard view of the square. The plaza was called Plaza Santa Cecilia until 1920, when it was renamed after Lt. Col. Peppino Garibaldi, who fought in the Mexican Revolution. Mariachi players have gathered here since the 1920s, when the Salón Tenampa, still on the north side of the square, became the place to hear them. The square was remodelled after 2009, so older photos with the kiosk no longer match what you will see. Keep your shot simple: let the letters fill the lower or middle of the frame and leave room above for the buildings and sky. For a different angle, the patio at the Museo del Tequila y el Mezcal looks over the square from slightly below street level, though you may need museum access, so check the official site before you go. If you ask musicians to pose, some may expect a small tip. Keep an eye on your phone and bag while you shoot.

Where it is: Plaza Garibaldi is in the historic centre of Mexico City, on Eje Central (Lázaro Cárdenas), a few blocks north of the Palacio de Bellas Artes.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the letters with the performers behind. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you are close to the letters and want the whole sign and the market front in one frame. Use 2x telephoto, if your phone has it, for musicians at a distance.

Where to stand: Stand at Mercado San Camilito (in front of the market, beside the large GARIBALDI letters). The nine large letters spelling GARIBALDI with the maguey, mariachi groups and performers in the frame, so the square's signature sign reads clearly in one shot. The square itself lies to the south of you, so the letters, the maguey and the groups of mariachis fall into one view.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the letters up with a horizontal grid line so they sit level. Include a few musicians or passers-by for scale and life, and avoid cutting the end letters off. If you want a calmer alternative, try the quieter Callejón de la Amargura, an older-looking alley nearby.

Settings tip: Tap on the letters to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so bright signs and lights don't wash out in the evening. In low light, try night mode and hold the phone steady against your body. Moving musicians may blur, so take a few shots.

Best time: The square is liveliest in the evening, and Friday or Saturday night from 11:00 p.m. is a good choice for the full atmosphere. For a calmer shot with fewer people in front of the letters, visit in daylight.

4National Museum of Anthropology

The National Museum of Anthropology is the largest and most visited museum in Mexico, with 3.7 million visitors in 2024. It stands in Chapultepec Park and opened in 1964. Its architects, Pedro Ramírez Vázquez, Jorge Campuzano and Rafael Mijares Alcérreca, built the exhibition halls around a big central courtyard. The best-known feature there is El Paraguas, 'the umbrella': a huge square concrete canopy held up by one slender pillar, with a large reflecting pool in the courtyard. Water falls around the column, so the scene has movement as well as strong shapes.

It photographs well because everything lines up: the canopy overhead, the carved column, the reflecting pool and the visitors walking beneath. These give you a sense of scale. The museum has 23 exhibition rooms and covers almost 8 hectares, so the courtyard is a calm centre in a very large building. Outside, the gardens around the halls hold outdoor exhibits, including the Olmec colossal heads.

For a phone, this is an easy place to get a striking picture. Stand under the canopy, include a little of the pool and a few people, and keep the column upright in the frame. Flash is not allowed, and tripods should be treated as banned, so hold the phone steady with both hands and rest your elbows against your body. The courtyard is inside the museum, so check the official site before you go for tickets, hours and photography rules.

Where it is: The museum is inside Chapultepec Park in Mexico City, in the area between Paseo de la Reforma and Mahatma Gandhi Street. Its entrance faces Paseo de la Reforma.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the whole canopy, the column and the pool into one frame. Use the default lens (1x) for a calmer, more natural view. Try telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) for the carved details on the column.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (central courtyard, under the canopy, just inside the entrance). The cantilevered umbrella canopy on its single column, with water falling around it and the reflecting pool and visitors in the frame. In the central courtyard under the canopy, just inside the entrance. From here the camera sees the umbrella on its single column, the water falling around it, the reflecting pool and visitors moving through the frame.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line the column up with a vertical line so it stands straight. Leave some of the pool and a few people in the lower part of the picture to show scale. For a second view, a free option is Paseo de la Reforma, which shows the museum facade and entrance with the park trees around it.

Settings tip: Switch flash off, as it is not allowed. Tap on the shaded underside of the canopy to focus, then nudge the exposure slider down if the bright sky is washing out. A Live Photo can capture the falling water, and you can pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon light suits garden photos. Check the official site for current hours before you go.

Where should I stand to photograph the Frida Kahlo Museum?

When is the best time to photograph Plaza Garibaldi?
