The 3 free photo spots

1Hopperstad Stave Church

Hopperstad Stave Church is a small, dark timber church with steep, stepped roofs, and it is one of the oldest stave churches still standing in Norway. It is assumed to have been built around 1130 and still stands where it was first put up. The best-looking parts are the layered roofs and the carved western portal, which is a fine example of medieval wood carving. Inside, the old timbers carry runic graffiti, though you may not be able to photograph indoors, so check on the day. The building has a long, varied history. It was an election church in 1814 and was left unused after 1877. The Society for the Preservation of Ancient Norwegian Monuments bought it in 1880. Between 1884 and 1891 the architect Peter Andreas Blix rebuilt the apse, corridors and roof, using Borgund Stave Church as his model. Since then it has mostly served as a museum. Then move closer for the portal and the roof details. Keep the phone level so the vertical timbers don't lean in the picture.

Where it is: Hopperstad Stave Church stands in Vikøyri, in Vik Municipality, Vestland county. It sits in the old Hopperstad parish area, on the spot where it was originally built.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Begin with the default lens (1x) for the whole church. Switch to the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you can't fit the building in, and use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for the portal carving and the roof tiers.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. If the whole building doesn't fit, switch to the ultra-wide lens.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and line a vertical wall or corner up with one of them, so the church doesn't lean. Let the stepped roofs fill the top of the frame. For a second picture, walk closer and use the telephoto on the carved western portal.

Settings tip: Tap the church to focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little. This keeps the dark timber rich and stops a bright sky from washing out the roof detail.

Best time: Soft, even light, such as early morning, late afternoon or a bright overcast day, suits the dark wood and carving best. Midday sun can leave harsh shadows under the roof edges.

2Vik Church

Vik Church is the white wooden parish church of Vikøyri, and it makes a tidy, classic subject for a phone photo. The plain white walls stand out well against greenery and sky, and the simple long-church shape is easy to fit into a single frame. It was built in 1877 to plans by the architect Haakon Thorsen, and it seats about 450 people. It was built because the two older parishes nearby, Hove and Hopperstad, had churches that were too small and in poor condition. The two parishes were merged, and a royal decree in December 1875 ordered a new church in Vikøyri. The church was consecrated on 23 August 1877. The two older churches were then taken out of regular use and kept as historical sites. The church had a major renovation in 1961–1962. For photos, think of this as a gentle, wide view rather than a close-up. Take a first shot at the default lens (1x) to show the setting, then zoom in to pick out the walls and roofline. White walls can easily turn bright and flat, so tap on the building and pull the exposure slider down a little to keep the detail. Turn on gridlines to keep the walls and roof level. If there are people about, wait a moment for a clear gap.

Where it is: In the village of Vikøyri, in Vik Municipality, Vestland county.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the church and its surroundings. Then try telephoto (2x–5x, depending on your phone) for a tighter view of the building. Use ultra-wide (0.5x) only if you move much closer.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject (near the entrance to the churchyard, on the path into the grounds). The dark stave church in the foreground with the white, red-roofed Vik Church (1877) visible in the distance behind it. From here the whole church should be in view, with space around it.

Framing: Switch on gridlines and line up the church's walls and roof with them. Leave a little sky above the roof and some foreground below so the building does not look squeezed. Take one wide shot with the default lens (1x), then one zoomed in. Hold the phone level to keep the vertical lines straight.

Settings tip: Tap on the white wall to focus, then drag the exposure slider down slightly so the walls keep their detail rather than turning bright white.

Best time: Soft morning or late-afternoon light is kind to white walls. A bright but cloudy day also works well, because it avoids harsh shadows.

3Moahaugane

Moahaugane is a mapped location on the Vikøyri map. This guide shows you where to stand and how to frame it with a phone camera.

The phone icon on the map is about 100 m east of the subject. From here you can try to frame the subject in context rather than pressing close to it. Walk a little closer afterwards if you want to capture details.

For a good picture, keep the horizon level using your phone's gridlines and leave enough room in the frame for the subject to read clearly. If there is something suitable in the foreground, such as a fence, a path edge or a few stems of grass, include it to give a sense of depth.

Take a few frames at different zoom levels, then choose the one that looks most natural. Respect any signs or fences you come across.

Where it is: Moahaugane lies in Vikøyri, a little to the west of the stand-here point, which is about 104 m away.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the overall scene, then try telephoto (2x or 3x, depending on your phone) to bring the subject closer and flatten the view. Keep the ultra-wide (0.5x) for when you are standing close, as from where you stand it would make the subject look small and far away.

Where to stand: Stand about 100 m east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face west. From here you can try to frame the subject in context.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower third line, depending on whether sky or ground is more interesting. Include a little foreground to give depth. If you walk closer, you can use the ultra-wide (0.5x) at a low angle for a more dramatic perspective.

Settings tip: Tap on the subject to set focus, then slide the exposure down a touch if the sky is bright so it does not wash out. Take a Live Photo if anything is moving in the wind, and pick the best frame afterwards.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon, when the low-angle light is usually softer and gives more texture. Bright midday light tends to be flatter and harsher.
