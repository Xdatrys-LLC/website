This map shows 15 of the most-searched sights in Willemstad as photo spots, ranked by how often people search for them: Fort Nassau, Fort Amsterdam, Queen Emma Bridge, Otrobanda and Kurá Hulanda Museum lead the list. Every spot has a pin on the subject and a phone icon showing where to stand, with tips for shooting it on your phone: which lens to use, how to frame it, a settings tip and the best time of day. The 3 free photo spots

1Fort Nassau

Fort Nassau sits on top of a hill above Willemstad, and that height is what makes it worth a stop with a phone. From the terrace you look out over St. Anna Bay, the arched Queen Juliana Bridge and the rooftops of Punda and Otrobanda below. The fort was built in the 1790s, finished in late 1797, and it is a rectangular battery inside a ring wall. It has had three names: Fort Republiek, then Fort George under British rule from 1807, and Fort Oranje Nassau from 1816, which most people shorten to Fort Nassau. It later served as a control tower for the Queen Emma Bridge, and since 1959 it has been a restaurant. The hill is about 68 metres high, so the view carries a long way across the harbour. The best picture here is the bay, with the fort's terrace in the foreground to give the scene depth. The far west end of the restaurant terrace is the spot to aim for. One guide calls it the best place for photos of St. Anna Bay, and it is usually calmer than the busy sunset corner. You can walk around the top of the fort, but not into the harbour control area. If you turn north, the refinery comes into view, which some visitors find less pleasing, so keep your frame towards the bay.

Where it is: Fort Nassau stands on a hill overlooking Willemstad and its harbour, with Punda and Otrobanda visible below.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the default lens (1x) for the bay and the bridge with the terrace in front. Switch to ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want more of the harbour and the city in one frame. Use telephoto (2x or more, depending on your phone) to pick out the arch of the Queen Juliana Bridge.

Where to stand: Stand right at the subject: the phone icon on the map marks the spot. From here you look over St. Anna Bay and the arched Queen Juliana Bridge, with the fort's terrace in the foreground. If you want the walls and terrace against a sunset sky over the harbour, the sunset corner is the alternative, though it is more crowded.

Framing: Turn on gridlines and place the horizon on the upper or lower line. Include a piece of the terrace, such as the edge or a wall, in the bottom of the frame so the view has a foreground. Keep the bridge arch off-centre and avoid pointing north towards the refinery.

Settings tip: Tap on the bridge or the water to set focus, then drag the exposure slider down a little so the sky keeps its colour. Wipe the lens first, as terrace air can be salty and leave a haze on the glass.

Best time: Late afternoon into sunset gives warm light over the harbour, but the terrace is busier then, and the restaurant advises booking for sunset. A morning visit is quieter.

3Queen Emma Bridge

The Queen Emma Bridge is a floating pontoon bridge across St. Anna Bay, joining the Punda and Otrobanda sides of Willemstad. Locals call it the "Swinging Old Lady" because it swings open to let ships through. That movement is what makes it such a good subject: you can photograph a long, pedestrian-only walkway one minute and a wide gap of open water the next. The bridge was built in 1888, and has been renovated several times since, most recently in 2005–2006. The lighting arches date from 1955, put up for a royal visit by Queen Juliana and Prince Bernhard. Cars stopped using the bridge in 1974, so people on foot now have it to themselves. When it swings open, two free ferries carry pedestrians across, which gives you a second photo opportunity. Allow a few minutes to watch the bridge swing, since the process only takes a few minutes. Keep your phone ready with the camera app open, and look for the arches and the line of the waterfront buildings as natural frames.

Where it is: The bridge crosses St. Anna Bay in the middle of Willemstad, linking the Punda quarter with the Otrobanda quarter on the opposite shore.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) to fit the long, low bridge and the water in one frame. Switch to the default lens (1x) for a cleaner, less stretched view, and use the telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) for details such as the lighting arches or people crossing.

Where to stand: Stand about 30 m south-east of the numbered pin, at the phone icon on the map, and face north-west.

Framing: Turn on the gridlines and place the bridge along the lower or upper third, keeping the horizon level. Include the lighting arches to give the picture a sense of scale. If the bridge is swinging open, hold the same spot and take a before-and-after pair, or try a Live Photo to capture the movement.

Settings tip: Tap on the bridge to set focus, then slide the exposure down a little if the sky or water looks washed out. In dim evening light, let night mode do its work and keep your phone as still as you can, for example by resting your elbows on a rail or wall.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and fewer people. If you want to see the bridge swing open, ask locally or check the official site, as we cannot promise a time.

7Handelskade

Handelskade is a waterfront street in Willemstad, and it can work well for phone photos if it gives you a line of buildings with water beside it. If the street has a row of colourful buildings, they make a good subject. Strong colours and repeating shapes make a picture easy to read, even on a small screen. If water is in the frame, calm conditions can give reflections.

The simplest approach is to treat the street as a long, flat band of colour. Stand back far enough to fit a good stretch of the row, then turn the phone to landscape. If you want a closer, more graphic shot, move in and photograph just two or three façades, keeping the vertical lines straight. Switch on the gridlines so the rooflines and any water's edge sit level.

If people or vehicles pass through, waiting a few seconds for a gap usually gives a cleaner frame, but a passer-by in a bright top can also add scale and life. Look for details as well as the wide view: shutters, doorways, shop signs and the way colours sit side by side.

Where it is: Handelskade is a waterfront street in Willemstad.

Shooting it on your phone

Lens: Start with the ultra-wide (0.5x) if you want the whole street scene in one frame, but keep the main subject away from the edges of the picture, where buildings can look stretched. Use the default lens (1x) for a more natural look. Use telephoto (2x-5x, depending on your phone) to pick out a few façades and flatten the perspective.

Where to stand: Stand about 120 m north-east of the subject, at the phone icon on the map, and face south-west. Take your first shots from here, then walk the couple of minutes towards the subject for closer frames.

Framing: Turn the phone to landscape and switch on the gridlines. If water is in view, line its edge up with a horizontal grid line, and keep the buildings' vertical edges parallel to the vertical lines. Leave a little space around the subject for breathing room. For a second picture, zoom to 2x or more and fill the frame with two or three façades.

Settings tip: Tap the brightest façade to focus, then slide the exposure down a touch so the colours stay rich and the sky does not wash out. If the phone offers panorama mode, move slowly and keep the phone level for a long, clean sweep of the row.

Best time: Early morning or late afternoon usually gives softer light and warmer colour on painted walls. Midday sun can be harsh and bleach the colours. Check the light on the day, as it depends on which way the façades face.
